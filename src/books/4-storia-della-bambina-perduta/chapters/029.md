## Summary

A few weeks later, events decide for Elena. **Mariarosa** leaves for Bordeaux and, before going, asks Elena to stay close to **Franco**. Elena suddenly understands that Mariarosa has not been playing charitable sister to him: she truly loves him, as mother, sister, and lover, and her worn body comes from constant anxiety that he might break.

During Mariarosa's eight-day absence, Elena spends evenings talking with Franco. He remembers their Pisa springtime, the Lungarno, and confidences from his childhood, and he listens to Elena's worries about her new publishing contract, a possible return to Naples, and **Nino**. He speaks bluntly: if she cares for Nino more than for herself, she must take him as he is — wife, children, other women, and all the *porcherie* ("filth," vile acts) he will commit. His dragging walk suddenly makes her think of **Pasquale**, swallowed by another darkness.

One morning Elena finds a note pinned to Franco's door: *non far entrare le bambine* — "don't let the girls come in." She opens the room and finds him dead. The narration refuses detail except to say that death is repellent: the living body she had known has become a horrible spectacle, as if Franco had violently rid himself of memory, language, meaning, and self-hatred.

After the funeral, attended by old militants with weakly raised fists, Elena tries to reach Mariarosa, but Mariarosa shuts down. The apartment empties, hostility replaces sisterliness, and Elena packs up **Dede** and **Elsa** for Naples.

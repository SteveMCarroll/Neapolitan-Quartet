## Summary

Elena hides **Nino** from **Adele**, speaking instead about the French article and thanking her, unwillingly, for looking after **Dede** and **Elsa**. But she sees that Adele has raised a real question: how can she hold together work, money, public appearances, Nino, and the children? She has almost no savings, no certain income, and no place where the girls' daily life can take shape.

She goes to Genoa full of questions. Her in-laws receive her with polite coldness; Elsa greets her uncertainly, Dede with hostility. The Genoa apartment is rich, book-filled, bright toward the sea. The girls move through it as if it were theirs, using the housekeeper with the courteous authority learned from their grandmother. Dede has attached herself to her grandfather, while Elsa clings to Adele's lap. Elena wonders whether they have learned to do without her or are afraid to trust her again.

After dinner, **Guido Airota** avoids the family rupture through politics, then through Elena's book, which he criticizes in professorly tones. A passing mention of feminism makes him mock slogans in a vulgar little singsong, briefly cracking his authority. Finally, after a bedtime ritual in which the girls repeat that they are Airotas, he attacks Nino as *ondivago* ("wavering") and politically servile. Adele is blunter: Nino, like Elena, is *un'intelligenza senza tradizioni* — an "intelligence without traditions," someone unreliable because they come from nowhere. Elena answers that she is Elena Greco and the girls are hers. Adele warns that the Airotas will not let her ruin them.

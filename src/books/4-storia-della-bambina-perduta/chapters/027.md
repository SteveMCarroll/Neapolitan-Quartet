## Summary

When Elena travels, she leaves **Dede** and **Elsa** with **Mariarosa**, but soon realizes that **Franco** is the one truly caring for them. Usually withdrawn in his room, uninterested in lessons and visitors, he becomes fond of the girls: he cooks, invents games, and teaches them. Dede learns from him to mock the old fable of Menenius Agrippa as an *apologo insulso* ("stupid apologue"), and to see on a globe the geography of enormous wealth and unbearable poverty.

One evening Franco tells Elena, with regret, that the girls might have been theirs. She corrects him — by now they would be older — and notices how changed he is from the rich, brilliant student of Pisa: still himself, but dimmed, no longer reading or writing, speaking politics with irony rather than passion.

Twice, however, she sees flashes of the old Franco. First **Pietro** arrives to inspect his daughters' life and school, quarrels with Mariarosa and Elena, and calls them irresponsible. Elena shouts that he should take the girls away. Franco intervenes, and he and Pietro end up debating family, children, and Plato so intelligently that Pietro leaves agitated but almost pleased.

The second visit is worse. **Nino** arrives exhausted and tense. Elena hopes he has finally resolved everything and will take her to Naples. Instead he says he cannot leave **Eleonora** and yet cannot live without Elena — proposing, in effect, that she become a *moglie parallela*, a "parallel wife." When Elena orders him out, he confesses that Eleonora is seven months pregnant.

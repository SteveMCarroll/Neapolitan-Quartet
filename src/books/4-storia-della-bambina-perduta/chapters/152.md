## Summary

**Imma** proves an eager pupil. In Piazza dei Martiri she repeats to Elena a long history learned from **Lila**, mixing facts, her own inventions, and Lila's. The square moves from countryside to patriotic bloodshed, from Ferdinand II's planned column of peace to a monument for Neapolitans who died for liberty. Elena senses that Lila uses the city's past to steady Imma's stormy present: in these stories, something ugly or chaotic becomes a road, building, or monument, then loses meaning, decays, and shines again.

Lila's Naples is cyclical: splendid and ruined, rationalized and wounded. The Vasto, the Risanamento, vanished gardens, hidden fountains, the Vesuvio that can reduce human grandeur to nothing — all become lessons in change. Yet Elena worries that Imma is being drawn into a permanent stream of *splendori e miserie* — splendors and miseries — where corruption and beauty endlessly replace each other.

The stories darken into spirits, demons, dead children, and books full of ghosts. Lila teaches that ghosts live not in palaces but in ears, eyes, voices, and minds, because words and images are full of them. Imma especially loves tales of Masaniello and of a minister who took archaeological treasures for himself. When Imma concludes that people who were not taught the public good may not understand guilt, Elena intervenes: powerful men know what they do. Then Imma asks whether **Nino** will return to parliament. Elena finally says no, adding that Imma can love him even if he is not important.

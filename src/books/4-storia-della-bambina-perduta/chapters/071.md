## Summary

Elena takes stock. Her earlier books no longer bring in much money; her advance is nearly gone; articles pay little or nothing. She lives on **Pietro**'s monthly support and **Nino**'s contributions to rent, bills, and clothes. After the dinner with the editor, she decides she urgently needs autonomy: regular books, a clear authorial identity, and income. The question is practical, not romantic — can she really expect Nino to support her and her daughters forever?

A part of Elena begins to admit, without much drama, that she can count on Nino only so far. She still loves him and admires his intelligence and recent work on economics, which may have impressed **Guido** Airota. But his renewed pleasure in the approval of her ex-in-laws troubles her. He belittles Pietro while praising Professor Airota and calls **Adele** a great lady of public relations. Elena sees his sensitivity to authority and his readiness to diminish those without it.

His politics also become harder for her to bear. Nino mocks people who are scandalized by corruption as *mammolette* ("little violets," soft naïfs). Parties, he says, are distributors of favors; ideals are furniture. At dinners he provocatively defends right-wing positions from the left, argues for dirty hands, controls on judges, and wage restraint. Elena, increasingly uneasy, often stays silent. When she softens his pragmatism for **Dede**, he tells the girl that her mother invents beautiful stories but knows little about the real world. The words sound disturbingly like Pietro, and Elena listens while he gives Dede a civics lesson she actually agrees with.

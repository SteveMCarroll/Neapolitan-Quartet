## Summary

Writing from the present, Elena says she is ashamed of how often luck favored her. Her new book immediately draws attention. Reviewers praise its readability, the construction of the protagonist, its brutal realism, its baroque imagination, and its soft feminine narration. The formulas are all positive but contradictory, as if each critic had summoned a *libro-fantasma* — a ghost-book — made from personal prejudices. After the *Panorama* article, however, everyone agrees on one point: the novel offers an unusual way of writing about Naples.

When Elena receives her contractual author copies, she is so happy that she decides to give one to **Lila**. She has never done this with earlier books, and she assumes Lila will not read it now either. Still, she feels close to Lila and wants to show gratitude. Lila reacts badly. Perhaps she is busy with neighborhood conflicts around the coming June election, or perhaps something else has irritated her; in any case, she refuses even to touch the book and says Elena should not waste her copies.

**Enzo** rescues Elena from embarrassment. He says that although he has never loved reading, he will keep the book for **Tina**, so that when she grows up she can read it. He asks Elena to write a dedication. Uneasy, Elena writes, "To Tina, who will do better than all of us." Lila immediately narrows the phrase into "better than me" and says that would take little. Elena and Enzo let the remark drop, put the book among the computer manuals, and discuss Elena's invitations and future trips.

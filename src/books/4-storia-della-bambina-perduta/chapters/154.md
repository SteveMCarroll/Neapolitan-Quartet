## Summary

Imma's rebuke — that Elena writes books but cannot see far like **Lila** — pushes Elena to admit that Lila, at fifty, has officially returned to books, study, and even writing. **Pietro** had guessed that this was a self-prescribed therapy against **Tina's** absence. In Elena's last year in the neighborhood she tries to question Lila directly, but Lila evades her, insisting she merely looks at a building or monument and then searches for information.

Sometimes, though, Lila catches fire and speaks of Naples as if it has revealed to her a secret glitter. Elena feels ashamed of having lived there so long while knowing so little. Lila moves rapidly from porcelain factories and San Pietro a Majella to foreign travelers who praised Naples's port, sea, castles, Vesuvio, and gardens, then complained of corruption, misery, noise, and disorder.

One day Lila focuses on violence. She and Elena once thought violence belonged to the *rione*, but Lila traces it through San Giovanni a Carbonara, from garbage ditch to gladiatorial games, stone fights, vendettas, bloodshed, and finally a church and monastery built over the horror. She remembers childhood beatings involving **Antonio**, **Enzo**, **Pasquale**, her brother, the Solara brothers, Elena, and herself. Elena is fascinated but also reduces Lila inwardly: brilliant, barely schooled, mixing truth, fantasy, and memory. Still, she suspects Lila has already written much of this into some larger text whose shape Elena cannot grasp.

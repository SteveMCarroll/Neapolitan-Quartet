## Summary

The clash with the Airotas is only the first. Afterward they press Elena more subtly: if she insists on putting herself first, she should leave **Dede** and **Elsa** with them. Elena resists daily, imagining taking the girls anywhere rather than leaving them in Genoa. But something always testifies against her. **Nino** calls and she runs to him; her new book begins to find readers; public meetings and love affairs stretch her absences.

She feels the girls' accusing gaze, yet once on the train, preparing for a talk or anticipating Nino, she feels shameless joy rising in her. She becomes used to being happy and unhappy at once, *felice e infelice* ("happy and unhappy"), and **Lila**'s warning about the harm to her daughters becomes an epigraph to her misery. At night her mother's curses and Lila's words merge, making her feel both autonomous and abandoned.

Elena tries to reconnect with **Mariarosa**, who organizes a Milan bookstore event for her. Elena is criticized and praised by opposing groups of women, then discovers an ability to mediate the discussion. At Mariarosa's apartment she finds **Franco**, **Silvia**, and Silvia's son **Mirko**, whom she studies for traces of Nino. Talk turns to scattered children, politics, and the fading of revolutionary certainties. Franco, physically marked by past violence and gently cynical, praises Elena's "masculine" capacities and urges her not to lose them to female obligations. Back in Genoa, ashamed even as she asks, Elena lets the girls choose whether to travel with her or stay with their grandparents. They choose the grandparents, asking only that she return with presents.

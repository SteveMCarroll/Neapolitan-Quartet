## Summary

Elena goes anxiously to the newsstand and finds the *Corriere della Sera* article. Again it uses the photograph of Elena with **Tina**, this time in black and white. The lawsuit is in the headline and is framed as an attempt to put a *bavaglio* — a gag — on one of the few courageous women novelists. The article does not name the neighborhood or the Solaras. Instead it makes the affair part of a broad conflict between medieval leftovers that block Italy's modernization and the advance of political and cultural renewal even in the South. It is brief but effectively defends literature, separating it from what it calls *tristissime beghe locali* — very sad local quarrels.

Elena feels reassured and protected. She praises the article by phone, then shows it to **Lila**, expecting delight. But Lila is cold. The publisher, she says, only wants to sell books; Elena should have written the article herself. Lila reminds her of the old piece on **Bruno Soccavo**, which annoys Elena because Bruno is dead and she dislikes remembering how she attacked him. Elena insists that article was about factory work, not against Bruno.

Now Lila's aim becomes clear. She does not care about freedom of expression or modernization. She wants Elena to force **Michele** and **Marcello** into the open so they can no longer hide behind **Carmen** and rule the neighborhood. Elena argues that a national paper will not care about Carmen, the Solaras, and local facts unless they carry general meaning. Lila rejects Elena's assumption that Carmen sold herself: Carmen is still Elena's friend, and she was forced. If Elena will help, good; otherwise, Lila says, they will manage alone.

## Summary

What Pietro can't bear, Elena sees, is *il disordine* — the disorder spreading
across every part of his life. He would have wanted regulated days: study,
teach, play with the girls, make love, contribute his small share to
untangling the Italian *matassa ingarbugliatissima*. Instead the university
exhausts him, colleagues belittle work that abroad is taken seriously, he
feels constantly vilified and threatened, and he has the impression that her
own *irrequietezza* — which, she protests inwardly, is hardly real; she's an
opaque woman — exposes the whole family to risk.

One afternoon, the apartment quiet, Elsa playing by herself, Elena drilling
Dede on reading, Pietro shut in his study, the doorbell jolts the air. Elena
opens. In walk Pasquale and Nadia.

They are carrying enormous army-surplus backpacks. Pasquale has a battered
hat over an enormous head of curly hair that flows into an equally enormous
curly beard. Nadia is gaunt, exhausted, with huge eyes, *da bambina spaventata
che finge di non aver paura* — a frightened child's eyes pretending not to be
afraid. Carmen gave them the address; she'd gotten it from Elena's mother.
They are all warm hugs and old-friend chatter — and they make themselves at
home as if the place were theirs. Nadia kicks off her shoes and stretches out
on the couch. Pasquale keeps his hat on, prowls the rooms, paws Pietro's
books, helps himself to two beers from the fridge without asking, drinks one
in a single pull and lets out a burp that makes Dede laugh. They are *in
giro* — on a trip — and won't say from when, to when. *Il lavoro?* Pasquale
laughs: enough, I've worked too much, now I rest.

He shows Pietro his palms and orders Pietro to show his; rubs them together.
*Senti la differenza?* He grabs a copy of *Lotta Continua* and drags his right
hand across the front page, proud of the rasping sound his calluses make on
the newsprint. Then, almost menacing: *senza queste mani raspose, professó,
non esisterebbe nemmeno una sedia, un palazzo, un'automobile, niente, nemmeno
tu* — without these rough hands, *professó*, there wouldn't even be a chair, a
building, a car, anything; not even you. If the workers stopped, the sky
would fall, the Arno would flood your nice houses, plants would take back the
city, and only those who have always labored would know how to survive —
while you two, with all your books, would be torn apart by dogs.

Pietro takes it without answering. So does Nadia, on her back, staring at
the ceiling. When Elena gets up to make coffee, Nadia follows her into the
kitchen. She watches Elsa cling to her mother and says: she loves you a lot.
She's little. So when she's big she won't? No, I hope she will. *Mia madre
parlava di te spessissimo* — my mother used to talk about you all the time;
you were just her student, but it was as if you were her daughter more than I
was. *Ti ho odiata per questo* — I hated you for it — *e perché mi avevi tolto
Nino*. Elena: it wasn't because of me that he left you. *Chi se ne frega* —
who cares, now I don't even remember what his face was like. Elena, after a
beat: as a girl I wanted to be like you. *Per fare cosa?* You think being born
with everything already done is nice? Well — you have to struggle less. Wrong:
you have no good reason to struggle either; you only feel guilt for what you
are and didn't earn. Better that, says Elena, than the guilt of having
failed. *Questo te lo dice la tua amica Lina?* No.

Nadia's head jerks. Her face turns cruel — a cruelty Elena hadn't suspected
she had: *preferisco lei a te. Siete due pezzi di merda che niente può
cambiare, due esemplari di feccia sottoproletaria. Ma tu fai la simpatica e
Lina no.* I prefer her to you. You're both pieces of shit nothing can change,
two examples of sub-proletarian filth. But you act nice, and Lina doesn't.

Elena, speechless, hears Nadia shout for Pasquale to shower with her. They
lock themselves in the bathroom. They come out semi-naked and dripping,
laughing as if nobody else were there. Pietro tries to find a footing: how
long have you two been together? Nadia, ice: *non stiamo insieme, voi due
casomai state insieme* — we aren't together; if anyone is, it's you two. He
pulls his querulous-academic tone: what does that mean. *Non puoi capire.*
You can't understand. When something can't be understood, he insists, one
explains. Pasquale, laughing, intervenes: *non c'è niente da spiegare,
professó: tu devi pensare che sei morto e non lo sai* — there's nothing to
explain, *prof*; you have to understand you're dead and you don't know it.
*È morto come vivete, come parlate, la vostra convinzione di essere
intelligentissimi, democratici e di sinistra. Come si fa a spiegare una cosa
a chi è morto?*

Tense silence. Elena says nothing; she can't get Nadia's insults out of her
head, here, in her own house. Pasquale and Nadia pack up and leave almost
without warning, as they came. On the threshold Pasquale says, suddenly sad:
*Ciao, signora Airota.*

*Signora Airota.* Even her friend from the rione is judging her. He no
longer says Lenù, Elena, Elena Greco. For him and how many others? For
herself? She uses Pietro's surname almost always now, ever since hers lost
its little halo. She cleans the bathroom — they have left it in bad
shape — and Pietro pronounces: *non voglio mai più quei due in casa mia* —
I never want those two in my house again; anyone who talks like that about
intellectual work is a fascist even if he doesn't know it; she — *un tipo
che conosco bene, nella testa non ha niente.*

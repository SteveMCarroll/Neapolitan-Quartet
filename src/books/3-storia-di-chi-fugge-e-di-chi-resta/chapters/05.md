## Summary

Adele arranges the seating so that Nino is beside Elena and Tarratano across from her. They order, and the conversation immediately goes back to the man with thick eyeglasses — a literature professor and a Christian Democrat columnist for *Corriere della Sera*. Free of the bookshop's manners, Adele and Tarratano savage him and praise Nino. They ask him to repeat, word for word, what he had said to the man at the exit; Nino demurs, then half‑invents it: something like *"to protect Authority in every form, you'd be ready to suspend democracy."*

From there the three of them talk past Elena, with growing energy, about the secret services, the colonels' Greece and its torture, Vietnam, the unexpected rise of the student movement across Europe, and an essay by *professor Airota* in *Il Ponte* on the state of the universities — which Nino says he agreed with word for word. "I'll tell my daughter you liked it," Adele says, "Mariarosa thought it was awful." — "Mariarosa only gets excited about what the world can't give." — "Exactly."

Elena, who has been buried in exams, her thesis, and the rushed publication of her own book, knows almost nothing about any of it. She listens in silence. She finds herself simultaneously elbow‑to‑elbow with Nino — just the sleeves touching, but that contact has its own current — and conscious of being entirely outside the conversation. Nino has kept his old habit of citing exact numbers: how many students are enrolled, what the buildings actually hold, how many hours the *baroni* — the senior professors — really teach, how many sit in parliament or on private boards instead. Tarratano notices her silence; she excuses herself to the bathroom.

At the mirror she is angry with herself. *"Nino has always had the gift of showing me how behind I am, the moment he opens his mouth."* She decides she has to start reading seriously again. Then her thoughts twist: *brutta, bassa, ho troppo petto* — *ugly, short, too much chest.* He never wanted her, she tells herself; that's why he chose Lila. And she circles back to the line — *"she's badly put together, in sex too"* — that she had let pass at the door. Next time, she resolves, she'll be braver: she'll ask him what he actually means by a woman being *"fatta male nel sesso"* ("badly made in sex"). She catches herself imagining sleeping with Nino that very night and his thinking the same of her, and tells him afterwards over drinks at his university. She steels herself: on the walk back to the hotel, she will reproach him on Lila's behalf — *that 'crooked' sex of yours produced a child, the small intelligent Gennaro; you don't get to talk about it like that.*

She leaves the bathroom. Adele waves her over, cheeks flushed: *«è arrivata finalmente la sorpresa»* — "the surprise has finally arrived." The surprise is **Pietro**, sitting beside her future mother‑in‑law.

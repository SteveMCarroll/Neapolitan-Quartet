## Summary

Lila does not phone for months; she must be very busy. Elena does not
call either, though she feels the need. To take the edge off the
emptiness she tries to draw closer to Mariarosa, but the obstacles
are many. Franco now lives full-time at her sister-in-law's
apartment, and Pietro likes neither Elena's attachment to his sister
nor her contact with her former *fidanzato*. If Elena stays in Milan
more than a day his mood sours, the imaginary aches multiply,
tensions spike at home. Franco himself, who rarely leaves the
apartment except for the medical care he still needs, is not glad of
her presence either: the girls' high voices grate on him and he
sometimes disappears altogether, alarming the household. Mariarosa
also has a thousand commitments and is permanently surrounded by
women — her flat is a kind of *centro di ritrovo*, a gathering place
that takes in intellectuals, bourgeois women, workers on the run
from violent partners, drifting girls. She has little time for Elena
and is so universally everybody's friend that Elena cannot quite
feel sure of their own bond. And yet — for a few days in that flat
— the desire to study and even to write reawakens in her. She at
least *feels* capable.

The women in Mariarosa's circle talk a great deal about themselves.
With Franco shut in his room they are all women, and yet they
struggle to define what a woman is. Every gesture, every thought,
every dream, when analysed deeply, seems not to belong to them.
This burrowing exasperates the most fragile, who cannot bear the
*eccesso di autoriflessione* — the excess of self-reflection — and
insist that the road to freedom requires nothing more than to
*tagliar via i maschi*, to cut off the men. The times are mobile,
arching like waves. Many of the women fear a return to flat calm
and so cling to the crest with extreme formulas, looking down with
fear and rage. When word spreads that Lotta Continua's stewards
have charged a separatist women's march, the tempers harden to the
point where, if the strictest of them discovers Mariarosa has a man
in the house — which she neither declares nor hides — *la discussione
diventava feroce, le rotture drammatiche*: discussions turn vicious,
breakups dramatic.

Elena hates these moments. She wants stimuli, not conflicts;
hypotheses to test, not dogmas. Or so she tells herself, and now
and then Mariarosa, who listens in silence. On one such evening she
tells her sister-in-law about her relationship with Franco at the
Normale, what it had meant to her. *Gli sono grata* — "I'm grateful
to him" — she says, I learned a great deal from him; it pains me
that he treats me and the girls so coolly now. She thinks aloud:
perhaps there is something off in *questa volontà degli uomini di
istruirci* — this male will to teach us. I was a girl then and I
didn't notice that, in his wanting to transform me, there was proof
that I did not please him as I was; he wanted me to be someone
else, or rather: he did not desire *una donna e basta* — just a
woman — but a woman in his image of how he himself might have been
had he been a woman. For Franco, I was *una possibilità di
espandersi nel femminile* — a chance to extend himself into the
feminine, to take possession of it. I was the proof of his
omnipotence, the demonstration that he could be not only a man
properly but also a woman. And now that he no longer perceives me
as a part of himself, he feels betrayed.

She comes out with the formula just like that. Mariarosa listens
with genuine interest — not the slightly mock kind she shows
everyone. *Scrivi qualcosa su questo tema*, she urges — write
something about this. She is moved; she murmurs that she never got
to know the Franco Elena described. *Forse è stato un bene* —
perhaps it was for the best, she would never have fallen for him,
she detests overly intelligent men who tell her how to be; she
prefers this suffering, reflective man she has taken in and tends.
Then she insists: write it down, what you just said.

Elena nods, and in a slightly breathless way — half pleased with
the praise, half embarrassed — says something about her marriage,
about how Pietro tries to impose his way of seeing. This time
Mariarosa bursts out laughing. The half-solemn pitch of the
conversation evaporates. Franco lumped together with Pietro? You
must be joking — *Pietro riesce a stento a tenere insieme la propria
virilità* — "Pietro can barely hold his own masculinity together,
let alone the energy to impose a vision of woman on you." Want to
know something? I would have sworn you wouldn't marry him. I would
have sworn that if you did, you'd have left him within a year. I
would have sworn you'd never have children with him. *Il fatto che
state ancora insieme mi sembra un miracolo* — the fact that you are
still together strikes me as a miracle. You really are a good girl.
*Povera te.* Poor you.

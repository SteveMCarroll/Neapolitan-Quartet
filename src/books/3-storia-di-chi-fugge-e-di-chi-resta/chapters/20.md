## Summary

She leaves at first light. Silvia is asleep with the baby at her breast; Juan is nowhere. She catches Mariarosa just back from putting Franco on his train — sleepy, awkward. *Dormito bene?* — slept well? Elena says she talked a lot with Silvia. About Sarratore? Yes. *So che siete amici* — I know you're friends. He told her himself. *Abbiamo spettegolato un po' su di te* — we gossiped about you a bit. Is it true mirko-sarratore is his? Yes. Mariarosa stifles a yawn: *Nino è affascinante, le ragazze se lo contendono* — Nino is fascinating, the girls fight over him; meno male, these are happy times, you take what you want, especially because he gives off a force that communicates joy and energy. The movement needs people like him, she adds — but they need to be guided, grown, oriented. The very gifted are always in danger of slipping into bourgeois democrat or company technocrat. They regret not having had time together and promise to do better next time. Elena collects her bag from the hotel and goes.

It's only on the long train south that she takes in this second paternity. A drab grey extends from Silvia to Lila, from mirko-sarratore to Gennaro. The passion of Ischia, the night of love at Forio, the secret affair in piazza dei Martiri, the pregnancy — all of it dims, reduces to a mechanism Nino apparently activated with Silvia after leaving Naples, and probably with countless others. She's offended on her own behalf and on Lila's, as if Lila were crouched in a corner of her head and she were feeling Lila's feelings. He has betrayed Lila and her both. Despite his gifts, Nino is *un uomo frivolo, superficiale* — a frivolous, superficial man, an animal that drips sweat and fluids and leaves behind him, like a by‑product of distracted pleasure, *materia viva concepita, nutrita, formata dentro ventri femminili* — living matter conceived, nourished, formed inside women's bellies. She remembers the day Melina, looking down from a window in the rione courtyard, mistook the young Nino for his father. Donato's old lover had spotted resemblances Elena couldn't see. Now Melina was right: Nino isn't running from his father at all; he is already his father and won't admit it.

And yet, on the boiling train, she can't manage to hate him. She thinks back to seeing him at the bookshop and embeds him in the events and words of the trip: sex has chased her, grabbed her, present in every gesture and book — partition walls collapsing, the chains of good manners snapping. Nino is living the season intensely. He fits the contentious Statale assembly with its hot smell; he fits the disorder of Mariarosa's apartment (whose lover, she now thinks, he was). With his intelligence, his desires, his pull, he moves comfortably and curiously through this time. Maybe she was wrong to fold him into his father's filthy hungers; maybe his behaviour belongs to a different culture. Silvia and Mariarosa had underlined it: the girls want him, he takes them, no coercion, no guilt, only the rights of desire. Perhaps when Nino once told her Lila was *fatta male* even in sex, he was telling her: the time of demands is over, loading pleasure with responsibility is a distortion.

By the time she reaches Naples, with surprise and dismay, part of her has given in and admits: what's the harm — life is to be enjoyed with people who know how to enjoy it. And as she heads back into the rione she realises that precisely *because* every woman wants him and he takes them all, she — who has wanted him forever — wants him even more. She decides to avoid him at all costs. As for Lila — say nothing? say everything? She'll decide when she next sees her.

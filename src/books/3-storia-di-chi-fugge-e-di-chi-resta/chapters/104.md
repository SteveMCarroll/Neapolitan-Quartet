## Summary

They arrive late at the trattoria. The Sarratore family is already
seated. Nino introduces them to his wife Eleonora and Elena's mood
turns on a dime. Yes, Eleonora has a pretty face and beautiful black
hair, exactly as in the photograph. But she is shorter than Elena —
and Elena is not tall. She is *senza seno* (flat-chested) and chubby.
Her flame-red dress is *malissimo* on her. She is overloaded with
jewellery. And the moment she opens her mouth a shrill voice comes out
in the accent of a well-bred Neapolitan woman *educata da giocatrici
di canasta in una casa con vetrata sul golfo* — schooled by canasta
players in a house with a glass wall onto the bay.

The shrillness is the least of it. Over the course of the meal
Eleonora reveals herself to be uneducated, despite studying law, and
addicted to denouncing everything and everyone with the satisfied air
of a woman who fancies herself contrarian. She tears strips off
Florence — *cos'ha in più di Napoli?*, what does it have over Naples?
— and the trattoria, *pessima* (dreadful), and the owner, *maleducato*
(ill-mannered), and whatever Pietro says, *che stupidaggine* (what
nonsense), and the children — *madonna quanto parlate, facciamo un
po' di silenzio, per favore*, my God how you chatter, a bit of quiet
please — and naturally Elena, asking why on earth she went to Pisa
when *Lettere a Napoli è assai meglio*, the humanities faculty in
Naples is much better, and announcing that she has never heard of
this novel of yours, when did it come out, eight years ago I was
fourteen. Rich, capricious, vulgar. Even the pleasant features keep
being spoiled by little grimaces of *fastidio* (annoyance) and
nervous laughter — *ih ih ih* — that chops up her sentences. Only with
her son and with Nino is she tender. Albertino is a beautiful, fat,
happy baby, whom Eleonora praises constantly; her husband is no less
flawless, and she touches him, hugs him, kisses him, approves every
phrase. *Cos'aveva in comune* with Lila, Elena wonders, or even with
Silvia? Nothing. So why on earth did Nino marry her?

She spies on him for the whole evening. He is kind to his wife,
submits to the kisses, smiles affectionately at the silly rude
sayings, plays absently with the baby. But his behaviour toward Elena
and the girls doesn't change: warm with Dede and Elsa, brightly
debating with Pietro, finding moments for Elena too. *La moglie*,
Elena wants to think, *non lo assorbiva* — the wife does not absorb
him. Eleonora is one of the many *tasselli* (tiles, pieces) of his
*vita mossa* — his eventful life — but exerts no real influence on
him. Nino goes his own way. So Elena relaxes, more and more, especially
when he holds her wrist for a second and almost strokes it,
recognising the bracelet from earlier; especially when he teases Pietro
about whether he has left her some time of her own; especially when,
straight after, he asks her how the writing is going.

"I've finished a first draft," she tells him.

Nino turns to Pietro, serious: "Have you read it?"

"Elena doesn't let me read anything of hers."

"You're the one who doesn't want to," Elena answers, without bitterness,
as though it were a private joke between them.

Eleonora cuts in — she doesn't want to be cut out of anything. *Che
roba è?* (What is it?), she asks. But before Elena can answer,
Eleonora's distracted head has already taken her somewhere else, and
she demands, festively: "Will you come with me tomorrow to look at
the shops, while Nino works?" Elena smiles with feigned warmth, says
yes; Eleonora launches into a minutely detailed list of what she
intends to buy.

Only as they leave the trattoria does Elena manage to fall in step
beside Nino and murmur: *te la senti di dare uno sguardo al mio testo?*
— do you feel up to taking a look at my draft? He looks at her with
genuine surprise: *davvero me lo faresti leggere?* — you would
really let me read it? If it doesn't bore you, yes. *Furtivamente* —
on the sly — heart pounding, as if she didn't want Pietro, Eleonora,
the girls, to notice, Elena slips him the pages.

## Summary

The date of her departure is closing in, but with Pietro nothing
improves: every agreement, *foss'anche solo per quel viaggio a
Montpellier* — even if only for the Montpellier trip — turns out
to be impossible. He cycles through threats. *Se te ne vai non ti
farò mai più vedere le bambine* — if you go, I'll never let you
see the girls again. Or: if you take the girls, I'll kill myself.
Or: I'll report you for *abbandono del tetto coniugale* (desertion
of the marital home). Or: let's go away together, the four of us,
to Vienna. Or, turning to the girls: *bambine, vostra madre
preferisce il signor Nino Sarratore a voi* — your mother prefers
Mr. Nino Sarratore to you.

She starts not to be able to take any more. She remembers how
Antonio fought her when she left him, years ago. But Antonio was
a boy, had inherited Melina's *testa labile* — fragile mind — and
above all had not had Pietro's education, hadn't been trained
from childhood to pick rules out of chaos. Perhaps, she thinks, I
gave too much weight to the cultivated use of reason, to good
reading, to a well-governed language, to political allegiance.
Perhaps in the face of being left, we are all the same; perhaps
not even a very ordered head can take the discovery of not being
loved. Her husband — there is nothing to be done — is convinced
he has to protect her *a tutti i costi dal morso velenoso dei
miei desideri* — at all costs from the venomous bite of her
desires; and so, to stay her husband, he is prepared to use any
means, even the most abject. The man who had wanted a civil
marriage, the man always in favour of divorce, by some ungoverned
internal movement now demands that their bond last forever as if
they had married before God. When she insists on putting an end
to the story, first he tries every path of persuasion, then
breaks things, slaps himself, suddenly starts singing.

When he goes that far she gets angry and shouts insults at him.
At which he changes abruptly *come una bestiola atterrita* — like
a frightened little animal — comes to her side, apologises, says
he isn't angry with her, *è la sua testa che non funziona* — it's
his head that isn't working. Adele, he reveals to her one night
in tears, had always betrayed his father; he had discovered it as
a small boy. At six he had seen her kiss an enormous man dressed
in blue, in the great Genoa living room that gave onto the sea.
He remembers every detail: the man had big moustaches like a dark
blade; a brilliant spot on the trousers like a hundred-lira coin;
his mother against the man like a *arco così teso che rischiava
di spezzarsi* — a bow so taut it might snap. She listens in
silence, tries to console him: calm down, *sono falsi ricordi* —
they're false memories, you know they are. But he insists: Adele
wore a pink sundress, *una spallina le era scivolata dalla spalla
abbronzata* — a strap had slipped from her tanned shoulder; her
long nails looked like glass; she had a black braid hanging from
her nape *come una serpe* — like a snake. He ends, moving from
suffering to anger: *capisci cosa mi hai fatto, capisci dentro
quale orrore m'hai precipitato?* — do you understand what you've
done to me, what horror you've plunged me into? And she thinks:
Dede too will remember; Dede too will one day shout something
similar. Then she pulls herself away and convinces herself that
Pietro is telling her about his mother now, after so many years,
*apposta* — on purpose — to induce that very thought in her, to
hurt her and hold her back.

She drags on, *stremata* — exhausted — day and night; nobody
sleeps any more. If her husband tortures her, Nino in his own way
is no less wearing. When he senses she is worn out by tensions
and worries, instead of consoling her he gets edgy: *tu pensi che
per me sia più facile* — you think it's easier for me, but here
it's hell too, I'm afraid for Eleonora, I'm afraid of what she
might do; don't think I'm not in just as much trouble as you, I'm
probably in more. And he proclaims: *però io e te insieme siamo
più forti di chiunque altro* — you and I together are stronger
than anyone else; our union is a non-negotiable necessity, is
that clear to you, tell me, I want to hear it, is that clear? It
is clear. But his words don't help much. Her strength comes
rather from imagining the moment she will finally see him again
and they will fly to France. *Devo resistere fino ad allora* — I
have to hold on till then, she tells herself; after that we'll
see. For now she wants only a pause in the agony.

At the peak of a violent quarrel in front of Dede and Elsa, she
says:

*Basta. Parto per cinque giorni, soltanto cinque giorni, poi
tornerò e vedremo cosa fare. Va bene?* — Enough. I'll go for five
days, only five days, then I'll come back and we'll see what to
do. All right?

He turns to the girls: *vostra madre dice che sarà assente per
cinque giorni, ma voi ci credete?* — your mother says she'll be
away for five days, but do you believe her? Dede shakes her head
no, and Elsa too. *Lo sappiamo tutti che ci lascerai e non
tornerai più* — we all know you'll leave us and not come back.
As if by an agreed signal, both girls hurl themselves against her,
hug her legs, beg her not to go, to stay with them. She can't
hold out. She kneels, holds them round the waist, says: *va bene,
non parto, voi siete le mie bambine, resto con voi* — all right,
I won't go, you are my little girls, I'll stay with you. The
words calm them, calm Pietro too. She goes to her room.

*Oh Dio santo* — Oh good God — how out of joint everything is:
them, her, the world around her. A truce is possible only by
telling lies. Two days to departure. She writes a long letter to
Pietro, then a short one to Dede with instructions to read it to
Elsa as well. She packs a suitcase and slides it under the bed in
the guest room. She does the shopping, stuffs the fridge. She
cooks for lunch and dinner the dishes Pietro adores; he eats with
gratitude. The girls, relieved, start squabbling again over
everything.

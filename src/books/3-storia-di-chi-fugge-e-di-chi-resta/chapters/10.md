## Summary

Elena reads the *Corriere* review covered in cold sweat. Her novel is being used as a peg for a sweeping attack on a whole generation: in the last decade, the reviewer claims, in factories, offices, universities, publishing and cinema, an entire world has collapsed under the pressure of *"a spoiled youth without values."* She is quoted in fragments to prove she is a fit representative of that mal‑raised generation, and is dismissed as *"una ragazzina impegnata a nascondere la propria mancanza di talento con paginette pruriginose di mediocre trivialità"* — *"a little girl busy hiding her lack of talent behind small titillating pages of mediocre triviality."* The smiling photo of her own face inside such an offensive piece is what she can't bear. She walks home crying and dumps the paper before her mother can see it.

At the empty kitchen table she eats pasta and potatoes mechanically, leafing through her novel and despairing: *"maybe it's worthless; maybe it was only published as a favour to Adele."* Elisa comes in with a slip from **signora Spagnuolo** — Gigliola's mother — whose telephone is the rione's emergency contact for Elena. There have been three calls: *Gina Medotti* from the publishing house's press office, Adele, and Pietro. The three names give weight to a fear she has been holding off: the bad words are already spreading. By the end of the day they will be everywhere — read by Pietro, by his family, by the publishing house, perhaps by Nino, by her old Pisa professors, by Galiani and her children. And, perhaps, by Lila.

There is a knock — signora Spagnuolo, breathless from running up the stairs to say Elena's fiancé is on the line again. Elena races to the phone. Pietro tries to console her and passes on Adele's advice: never mind the substance, the important thing is that *they're talking about the book.* Then he adds: *tomorrow there will be a piece in **l'Unità**.* Elena answers icily — *"I'd rather no one mentioned me at all"* — and hangs up.

She doesn't sleep. At dawn she runs to the newsstand and opens *l'Unità* right there, by the elementary school. Her photograph again — this time at the top, beside the headline *Giovani ribelli e vecchi reazionari* ("Young rebels and old reactionaries"). The piece is signed by someone she has never heard of, but it is well written: it praises her novel without qualification and tears the Corriere reviewer apart. The words act as a balm. She walks home, leafs through her own book again, and finds it suddenly well‑constructed and confidently written. Her mother eyes her: *"have you won the lottery?"*

In the late afternoon signora Spagnuolo reappears to fetch her to the phone yet again. (She does it gladly, full of admiring chat about clever girls; she mentions in passing that Gigliola has been lucky to be engaged to Michele Solara — otherwise she'd be slaving in the pastry shop for life.) On the line is Adele. *"Have you read it?" — "Yes." — "Are you pleased?" — "Yes." — "Then here's more good news: the book is starting to sell. If it keeps going we'll reprint." — "What does that mean?" — "It means that our friend at the Corriere thought he was destroying us, and instead he worked for us. Ciao, Elena, enjoy the success."*

## Summary

She rediscovers herself, *all'improvviso invulnerabile e invincibile*
— suddenly invulnerable and invincible — as in some earlier season
of her life when everything seemed permitted. *Ero nata fortunata*
— I was born lucky. Even when fate seemed against her, in fact it
had been working for her. Of course she has merits too: she is
orderly, has memory, slogs with stubbornness, has learned to use
the instruments worked out by men, can give logical coherence to
any jumble of fragments, *sapevo piacere* — she knew how to please.
But luck counted most, and she was proud to feel it at her side
like a trusted friend.

She has married *un uomo perbene* — a decent man — not a Stefano
Carracci or, worse, a Michele Solara. She will face him; he will
suffer; in the end they will find an agreement. The shock of
breaking the marriage will be traumatic. For different reasons,
neither of them wants to involve their parents, certainly not the
Airota, *gente che sapeva sempre cosa fare e a chi rivolgersi* —
people who always knew what to do and whom to turn to. But she
feels calm at last. They are two reasonable adults; they will
talk and explain themselves. One thing alone now seems
non-negotiable: *sarei andata a Montpellier* — she will go to
Montpellier.

She talks to Pietro that same evening. She confesses that Nino is
her lover. He tries everything not to believe her. When she
convinces him it is true, he cries, begs, gets angry, lifts the
glass top off the living-room table and *lo lanciò contro la
parete* — hurls it against the wall, under the terrified eyes of
the children, who have woken at the shouting and are standing
incredulous on the threshold. She is shaken but does not back
down. She puts Dede and Elsa back to bed, calms them, waits for
them to fall asleep. Then she goes back to face her husband. Every
minute *diventò una ferita* — became a wound. On top of it,
Eleonora starts bombarding them with phone calls day and night,
insulting Elena, insulting Pietro for *non sapere fare l'uomo* —
not knowing how to be a man, announcing that her relatives will
find a way to leave them and their daughters without even *gli
occhi per piangere* (eyes to cry with).

But she doesn't shrink. She is in such a state of exaltation that
she cannot feel herself in the wrong. The pains she causes, the
humiliations and aggressions she takes — all of it seems to be
working for her. Even Eleonora will come to understand that with
love there's nothing to be done; that *è insensato dire a una
persona che vuole andare via: no, devi restare* — it is senseless
to tell someone who wants to leave: no, you must stay. Pietro, who
in theory already knows that precept, will only need time to
assimilate it and turn it into *saggezza* — wisdom — into *pratica
della tolleranza* — a practice of tolerance.

Only with the girls does she find everything difficult. Pietro
insists they tell them the reasons for the quarrels. She is
against it — they are small, what can they understand? But at one
point he shouts: if you have decided to leave, you have to give
explanations to your daughters; if you don't have the courage,
stay — it means you yourself don't really believe in what you
want to do. She murmurs: *parliamo con un avvocato* — let's talk
to a lawyer. He answers: there's time for lawyers. And he
treacherously, loudly, summons Dede and Elsa, who at the first
sound of shouting always go to shut themselves in their room,
*molto affiatate* — close-knit.

*Vostra madre vi deve dire una cosa* — your mother has to tell you
something, Pietro begins; sit down and listen. The girls sit
composed on the sofa and wait. She begins:

*Io e vostro padre ci vogliamo bene, ma non andiamo più d'accordo
e abbiamo deciso di separarci* — your father and I love each other
but we no longer get on and we've decided to separate.

*Non è vero* — Pietro interrupts, calmly — that's not true; it's
your mother who has decided to go away. And it's not true either
that we love each other: *lei non me ne vuole più* — she doesn't
love me any more.

Bambine, she protests, *si può continuare a volersi bene anche se
non si vive più insieme* — you can keep loving each other even
without living together. He interrupts again: even that is false.
*O ci vogliamo bene, e allora viviamo insieme e siamo una
famiglia; o non ci vogliamo bene, e allora ci lasciamo e non
siamo più una famiglia* — either we love each other and we live
together and we are a family; or we don't love each other and we
leave each other and we aren't a family any more. If you tell
lies, what can they understand? Explain *con verità e chiarezza*
— with truth and clarity — why we are leaving.

She tries: I'm not leaving you, you're the most important thing I
have. I just have *dei problemi con vostro padre*.

*Quali?* — which? Spell them out.

She sighs, murmurs: I love someone else and I want to live with
him.

Elsa watches Dede to know how to react. Dede stays impassive, so
Elsa stays impassive. Pietro, on the other hand, loses control
and shouts:

*Il nome: di' come si chiama quest'altra persona. Non vuoi? Ti
vergogni? Lo dico io: quest'altra persona la conoscete, è Nino,
ve lo ricordate? Vostra madre vuole andare a vivere con lui.*

— The name: say what this other person is called. You won't? You're
ashamed? I'll say it: this other person — you know him, it's Nino,
do you remember? *Your mother wants to go live with him.*

Then he starts to cry, desperately, while Elsa murmurs, a little
alarmed: *mi porti con te, mamma?* — will you take me with you,
mum? She doesn't wait for the answer. When her sister gets up and
almost runs from the room, she follows.

That night Dede screams in her sleep. Elena startles awake, runs
to her. She is asleep, but she has wet the bed. Elena wakes her,
changes her, changes the sheets. When she puts her back, Dede
murmurs that she wants to come into her bed. Elena agrees, holds
her close. Every so often the child jolts in her sleep, checks
that she is there.

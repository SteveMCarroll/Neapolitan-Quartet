## Summary

Elena hauls herself back into the conversation. She doesn't want to sit there silent. She also wants Franco to see she isn't the girl he knew — that she's become someone else, and she wants Mariarosa to hear him say so. With the baby quiet and Silvia gone, she picks her opening and disagrees with her ex. The disagreement is improvised: she isn't moved by any solid conviction, she just wants to speak against him. She doubts that class struggle in France is as ripe as people are claiming; she finds the *unità studenti‑operai* — the student‑worker unity — fairly abstract for now. The men actually listen; Juan nods each time she says *popolo* — "the people." That irritates Mari. *"Stai dicendo che la situazione non è oggettivamente rivoluzionaria,"* he underlines — you're saying the situation isn't objectively revolutionary — and she knows that tone, it means he's defending himself by mocking her.

They go at each other, sentences overlapping: *what does "objectively" mean? — it means acting is unavoidable. — so if it isn't unavoidable you sit on your hands? — no, the revolutionary's task is always to do what's possible. — in France the students did the impossible, the education machine has broken and won't be fixed. — admit things have changed and will keep changing. — no one is asking you for notarised certification that the situation is objectively revolutionary, the students just acted.* They stop talking together. The exchange was anomalous — not in content, in the warmth of the tone, the dropped manners. Mariarosa's eyes glint with amusement; she has caught that Elena and Franco were once more than university acquaintances. She drags Silvia and Juan off to find a ladder and bedding, leaving Franco and Elena alone.

He fixes the floor, half‑smiles: *"sei rimasta la piccoloborghese di sempre"* — you're still the same petty bourgeois. It's the old label he used to taunt her with in Pisa when she was afraid of getting caught in his room. She snaps back: *the petty bourgeois is you, by origin, by culture, by behaviour.* He says he didn't mean to offend. Then, with a logical leap that throws her, he asks about *"quella tua amica a cui tenevi tanto"* — that friend you cared so much about. Had she told him about Lila in Pisa, in what terms? She says Lila is well, working in a sausage factory on the edge of Naples, that the marriage didn't work, and gives him a number to look her up if he comes south. He weighs her face for a way to hurt her as little as possible, then asks: has she read your book?

She turns the question. He says: yes, of course. How did it strike you? *Buono* — good. In what sense? *C'erano belle pagine* — there were nice pages. Which? "The ones where you give the protagonist the ability to weld together the fragments of things in her own way." That's it? *Non è abbastanza?* — isn't that enough? She forces it: no, clearly you didn't like it. He tries to soften; she pushes. Finally: *"Dentro non c'è granché, Elena. Sotto amoretti e smanie di ascesa sociale nascondi proprio ciò che varrebbe la pena raccontare."* Inside there isn't much, Elena. Under little love stories and itches for social climbing you're hiding exactly what would be worth telling. Meaning what? *Lascia perdere, è tardi* — drop it, it's late. He puts on a benevolent‑irony mask but the new tone holds: this is a man with a real task who gives everything else just a drop at a time. *"Hai fatto il possibile, no? Ma questo, oggettivamente, non è il momento di scrivere romanzi."* You did your best, sure. But this, objectively, is not the moment for novels.

## Summary

Elena needs a long time to absorb the news. She lashes out — he could
have warned them, he's a boor — and Pietro shrugs: they decided at the
last minute, what's the problem? The problem, unspoken, is that Nino
has come with his wife.

The whole chapter is the inside of Elena's preparation, the body
catalogue she has spent years half-acknowledging now flushed up to
the surface. She knows what she is: she grew up with one pair of
shoes at a time, dresses sewn by her mother, makeup for special
occasions only; lately, under Adele's tutelage, she has learned to
care about clothes, has even begun to enjoy making herself look nice.
But preparing for a man — what she actually calls *apparecchiarmi*,
"setting myself out like a table" — has always struck her as faintly
ridiculous: all that fuss, all that time spent disguising herself when
she could be doing something else. The colours that suit her and the
ones that don't, the cuts that slim her and the ones that enlarge, the
endless costly preparation that comes down to reducing oneself to a
laid table for *l'appetito sessuale del maschio* — the male's sexual
appetite — a dish well cooked enough to set him salivating. And then
the agony of failing: not looking pretty enough, not having concealed
deftly enough the vulgarity of the flesh with its humours and odours
and asymmetries.

She has done it before, in any case. She did it for Nino in Milan,
wanting to show him she had become someone else, that she had earned
a certain refinement, that she was no longer the *ragazzina* at Lila's
wedding or the *studentessa* at the Galiani children's party or the
green author of a single novel. And now? Now he has brought his wife,
which strikes her as cruel. She loathes the prospect of competing in
beauty with another woman under a man's gaze; she suffers at the
thought of being in the same room as the beautiful, *via Tasso* girl
she has seen in the photograph. The *signorina* of via Tasso,
schooled in the management of the body from birth, will weigh and
catalogue her, then later, alone with her husband, will dismantle her
with cold precision.

She vacillates for hours. She decides — she will invent an excuse, send
Pietro alone with the girls. The next day she can't hold to it. She
dresses, undresses, does her hair, undoes it; she hounds Pietro,
walking into his study every five minutes with a different outfit and
a different *come ti sembro?* — "how do I look?" He glances over,
distracted: *stai bene*, you look fine. What about the blue dress? He
agrees. She tries the blue dress — too tight at the hips. She goes
back: *mi stringe*, it's tight. Pietro, patient: then the green one
with the little flowers suits you better. She doesn't want to be
told she looks *better* in something, she wants to be told she looks
flawless, that the earrings are flawless, that the hair is flawless,
that the shoes are flawless. Pietro is unable to give her any of that.
He looks at her *senza vederla* — without seeing her — and she leaves
his room feeling worse: too much breast, too much backside, hips too
wide, hair too dirty-blonde, nose too big. The body of her mother. An
ungainly organism; all that's missing is for her mother's sciatica to
flare back up and leave her limping. Nino's wife, on the other hand,
is *giovanissima, bella, ricca*, and certainly knows how to be in the
world in a way Elena will never learn.

A thousand times she goes back to the original decision: she'll send
Pietro with the girls. A thousand times the decision dissolves. In
the end she goes. She wears a white blouse over a cheerful flowered
skirt, with her mother's old bracelet as her only jewellery; in her
handbag she tucks the manuscript she has been writing. She tells
herself: *chi se ne fotte di lei, di lui, di tutti* — to hell with
her, with him, with all of them.

## Summary

Elena spends the morning out and about with Eleonora and the three
children in a state of well-being so complete that, she thinks, *se
pure lei mi avesse colpito con un coltello non avrei sentito male* —
if she'd stabbed her with a knife, she wouldn't have felt it.
Eleonora, finding herself met with cheerful courtesy instead of the
expected hostility, drops her own. She compliments the girls on how
disciplined they are, then admits she is *un po' gelosa* — a little
jealous — of Elena, not because she's clever, but because Elena has
known Nino forever and she hasn't. She wishes she could have met him
as a child and known how he was at ten, at fourteen, what his voice
sounded like before it broke, what his laugh was like when he was
small. *Meno male che ho Albertino*, she says, just like his father.

Elena studies the baby and can't see any of Nino in him, perhaps it
will appear later. *Io assomiglio a papà*, Dede announces proudly;
Elsa adds: I look more like mamma. Elena's mind goes to Silvia's son
Mirko, who has always been *identico a Nino*. She remembers the
pleasure of holding him in Mariarosa's flat, soothing him. What had
she been searching for in that child, before she was a mother herself?
What had she been looking for in Gennaro, when she still didn't know
Stefano was his father? And what is she looking for in Albertino now?
She is sure Nino has long since stopped thinking of Mirko, and as far
as she knows has never shown a flicker of curiosity about Gennaro.
*Questo distratto inseminare dei maschi*, she thinks bitterly — this
distracted seeding by males, stunned by their own pleasure. They
fertilise us under the wave of an orgasm. They peer inside and pull
back, leaving behind in our flesh their *fantasma*, their
ghost-residue, like a forgotten object. Was Albertino the child of
*volontà* and *attenzione*, of will and attention? Or was he too just
in the arms of this mother-woman without Nino feeling he had any real
part in it? She shakes herself out of it, tells Eleonora that
Albertino is the spit of his father, and is pleased with the lie. Then
she narrates Nino at school for Eleonora — at the Oliviero years and
the head-teacher's contests, then at the Galiani and on Ischia — and
stops there. Eleonora, like a child, keeps asking *e poi?*

The more Elena chatters, the more Eleonora warms to her. They go in
and out of shops, and any garment Elena admires and then sets aside,
Eleonora secretly buys for her. She buys dresses for Dede and Elsa.
She pays at the restaurant. She pays the taxi that takes Elena and
the girls home — and then the taxi takes Eleonora on to the hotel,
arms full of bags. Elena and the girls wave until the car turns the
corner.

Walking back into the apartment, Elena thinks: *è un altro tassello
della mia città* — Eleonora is another tile of my city, but a piece
infinitely far from her experience. The woman uses money as if it
had no value. Whose money? Surely not Nino's. Her father is a
lawyer, her grandfather was a lawyer, her mother is from a banking
family. And what is the difference, Elena asks herself, between the
respectable bourgeois wealth of Eleonora's family and the Solara
wealth? She thinks of the dirty paths money takes before becoming a
fat salary or a substantial fee. She thinks of the boys of the rione
who scraped together their daily bread unloading contraband, cutting
down trees in the parks, working on building sites. She thinks of
Antonio, Pasquale, Enzo, hustling for change since childhood. Engineers,
architects, lawyers, banks were another thing — but their money came,
filtered through a thousand layers, *dallo stesso malaffare, dallo
stesso scempio*, from the same wrongdoing, the same plunder; a few
crumbs of it had even reached her own father as tips, helping send
her to school. Where, then, was the line beyond which dirty money
becomes clean — and vice versa? How clean was the money Eleonora
spent through the Florence heat, the cheques behind the gifts now
sitting on Elena's table? How different, really, from the cheques
Michele uses to pay Lila?

She and the girls spend the afternoon in front of the mirror,
peacocking in the new clothes. A faded-red dress from the 1940s suits
her especially well; she wishes Nino could see her in it.

He doesn't. The Sarratore family goes back to Naples without their
seeing each other again. But, against expectation, time does not
collapse. It runs lightly. Nino *would* be back, that was certain. He
*would* discuss her text with her. To avoid pointless friction she
sets a copy on Pietro's desk. Then she calls Mariarosa, telling her
that the *brogliaccio* she had described is finally in shape. Mariarosa
asks to be sent the manuscript at once. A few days later she calls
back, *entusiasta*, and asks if she may translate it herself into
French and send it to a friend in Nanterre who runs a small press.
Elena agrees. A few hours after that, Adele calls in a tone of
performed offence: how is it the things you write you now hand to
Mariarosa first, not to me? Elena, half-laughing: I was afraid you
wouldn't be interested, it's about seventy pages, it's not a novel, I
don't even know what it is. Adele: *quando non sai cosa hai scritto
vuol dire che hai lavorato bene* — when you don't know what you've
written, it means you've worked well. Let me decide whether it
interests me. Elena sends her a copy too — almost casually, almost
distractedly, on the very morning when Nino, at midday, calls her
unannounced from the station: he has just arrived in Florence.

"I'll be at your place in half an hour. I'll drop my bag and head to
the library."

"Won't you eat something?" Elena asks, naturally. And it feels normal
— *il punto d'arrivo di un lungo percorso*, the end point of a long
road — that he should come to sleep at her house, that she should
make him lunch while he showered in her bathroom, that they should
eat together, she, he, the girls, while Pietro is giving examinations
at the university.

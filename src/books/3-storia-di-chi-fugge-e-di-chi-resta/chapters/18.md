## Summary

Mariarosa comes back with Juan and Silvia, towels and nightclothes in their arms. She certainly heard Franco's last line, certainly understood he was talking about Elena's book, and says nothing — not even a polite *I liked it* or *novels can be written any time.* Elena reads the silence: even in these cultivated, politically‑possessed circles, her book is considered a little nothing, and the pages people read it for are dismissed as scraps of bigger, more disruptive texts she has never even read, or get Franco's label — a story of *amoretti*.

Mariarosa shows her the bathroom and her room with fleeting courtesy. She shakes Franco's hand goodbye — neither of them moves to embrace. She watches him disappear into a room with Mariarosa and reads, from Juan's dark expression and Silvia's unhappy look, that host and guest will sleep together. Her own assigned room reeks of stale smoke: an unmade single bed, no nightstand, no lamp but a weak ceiling bulb, piled magazines on the floor — copies of *Menabò*, *Nuovo impegno*, *Marcatré* — expensive art books, some battered, some plainly never opened. Under the bed an ashtray full of stubs; she opens the window and leaves it on the sill. The nightgown Mariarosa lent her is too long and too tight. No toothbrush — that's a recent Pisa habit anyway. She lies in bed trying to overwrite the Franco of tonight with the Franco of years ago, the rich generous boy who bought her everything and took her to Paris and to the family house in Versilia. It doesn't work. The present has the upper hand: the noise of the assembly, the political phrases ringing in her head, the way they've shrunk her book. Was she fooling herself about a literary future? Was Franco right that there is other work to do? She has to get up to switch off the light. She comes back to bed in the dark.

She struggles to sleep, the bed and the room full of unknown bodies' traces. She finally drifts, then jolts: someone has entered the room. *Chi è?* — who's there. It's Juan, without preamble, in a pleading voice as if asking a small first‑aid favour: *"posso dormire con te?"* — can I sleep with you? The request feels so absurd she has to repeat the word to be sure she's awake. *Sleep?* Yes, beside you, I won't bother you, I just don't want to be alone. *Assolutamente no.* Why? She can only manage: I'm engaged. So what? Let's just sleep. *Vattene, per favore, nemmeno ti conosco* — go, please, I don't even know you. He sits on the bed, she sees his shape and smells the cigar on his breath. *"Sei una scrittrice, scrivi d'amore."* You're a writer, you write about love — everything that happens to us feeds the imagination. He touches her foot. She can't bear it, lunges for the light, switches it on. He is still on the bed, in underwear and undershirt. *Fuori* — out — she hisses, so visibly close to screaming and so plainly ready to attack first that he gets up slowly and goes, calling her, disgusted, *una bigotta* — a prude. She shuts the door behind him. No key.

She is shaken, furious, frightened, a bloody dialect spinning in her head. She waits before getting back into bed, doesn't switch the light off. What is it she's giving people to read? What kind of person did Juan think she was? Is it the *fama di donna libera* — the reputation of "free woman" — the book is giving her? The political language she spoke at dinner — was that not just dialectical sport but a whole‑person declaration including sexual availability? Was it the shared‑side feeling that let him walk into her room, the same that let Mariarosa bring Franco into hers? Or is *she* — having held Mirko, having argued France — leaking some of the erotic charge she'd felt in the hall earlier? In Milan once before she'd been ready to sleep with Nino and betray Pietro; but that was old passion, that justifies things, while plain sex on demand revolts her. She remembers — not the Ischia beach with Donato, the part she made into a novel, but the time Donato appeared in Nella's kitchen and kissed and touched her against her will and produced, against her will, a flush of pleasure. Is there a line from that frightened girl to this woman lunged at in a lift, lunged at in a bedroom? Are the cultivated Tarratano, friend of Adele, and the Venezuelan artist Juan made of the same dough as Nino's father — a railway inspector, hack poet, *pennivendolo* — penny‑a‑word scribbler?

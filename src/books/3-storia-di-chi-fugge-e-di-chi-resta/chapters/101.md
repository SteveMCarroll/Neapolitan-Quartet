## Summary

A week passes after Nino's lunch and the phone does not ring. Elena
sulks, abandons her reading, scolds herself for waiting like a teenager
with a cold. Then Pietro comes home unusually cheerful: he ran into Nino
at the faculty, they spent the afternoon together, and Nino has invited
all four of them out to dinner so that Elena doesn't have to cook.

The day of the dinner is a long, anxious preparation. Elena bathes both
girls, then bathes herself, depilates, washes and dries her hair, tries
on every dress she owns and likes none of them; Dede and Elsa shuffle
around in her shoes, posing in the mirror in imitation. She gives up,
resigns herself to being what she is, snaps at Elsa for dirtying her
dress at the last second, and drives off to collect Pietro and Nino at
the university. The closer she gets, the more she hopes Nino will have
cancelled. He hasn't. The two men are already on the pavement,
talking — Nino with his enveloping gestures, *come se invitasse
l'interlocutore a entrare dentro uno spazio disegnato apposta per lui*
("as if he were inviting his interlocutor into a space designed
specially for him"), Pietro red-faced and laughing too eagerly. Neither
particularly notices her arrival.

Pietro takes the back seat with the girls. Nino sits beside Elena and
navigates her — right here, right again, then left — to a trattoria he
knows, where they make wonderful *frittelle* (sweet little fritters)
that he has already promised the girls. The owner greets him by name,
the waiters know him; Elena is bitterly aware that she and Pietro have
been in Florence seven years and have no equivalent place of their own.
The two men sit opposite each other; Elena ends up at the head of the
table, between the girls, who normally behave at dinner but tonight
clown and squabble for Nino's attention.

Only when the famous *frittelle* arrive does Nino turn to her, and not
in the frivolous tone of the conversation up to that point. "Why hasn't
anything else of yours come out?" he asks, with what feels like real
interest. Elena, blushing, gestures at the girls and says she has
"done other things." He tells her the novel was excellent — not as
flattery, he says: she has always been able to write. He asks what she
is working on. Reluctantly she says: *i maschi che fabbricano le
femmine* — men inventing women. He says, simply, *bello* ("good"), and
tells her to hurry up because he wants to read it. Then he shows that
he has actually read the books she has been studying — books she had
assumed no man would read — quotes a Starobinski she might find useful,
moves between Rousseau and Bernard Shaw.

He turns to Pietro and tells him, in front of everyone, that he must
leave Elena more time. Pietro shrugs: she has all day. Nino doesn't
let him off. "I'm not joking. If you don't, you're guilty not only on
the human level but on the political one. The squandering of
intelligence" — *lo sperpero di intelligenza* — "is what's at stake:
a community that thinks it natural to smother so much female
intellectual energy under the care of children and house is its own
enemy and doesn't notice." Pietro answers with irony — Elena can
cultivate her intelligence whenever she likes, provided she doesn't
take time away from him. Elena, hurt, says lightly that her husband
is announcing that she doesn't have any real interest of her own. Nino
asks her, gently: *ed è così?* — "is that so?" She replies, off-balance,
that she doesn't know, she doesn't know anything; tears rise to her
eyes; she lowers her head.

Nino rescues the moment. He tells the girls that he himself will eat
one more fritter and the *mamma* will eat one more and *papà* will eat
one more, and then enough — and that in exactly thirty days he will be
back, with these two *signorine*, and the owner will prepare them a
mountain of *frittelle*. Elsa, who has only the vaguest sense of time,
asks when a month is, when thirty days is. Elena, eyes dry again,
looks straight at Nino and asks the same thing: *quand'è un mese,
quand'è trenta giorni?* — when is a month, when is thirty days?

Pietro, expansive on the way back, tries to pay and discovers Nino
has already settled the bill. He drops Nino at the hotel and, very
cheerfully, says there is no point in him paying for hotels: the
Airotas have a guest room and next time he must come and stay. Elena,
in the back seat between the half-asleep girls, listens silently to
the men's slightly drunk chatter. Once Nino is gone she snaps at her
husband — he might at least have consulted her before issuing the
invitation. Pietro, looking at her in the rear-view mirror,
mutters that he thought she would be pleased.

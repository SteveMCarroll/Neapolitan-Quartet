## Summary

That long, rainless, dark day starts a reversal. Or maybe it had begun earlier, in details Elena hardly noticed at the time. The tour of the *rione* was useful, but it sends her home discontented. What kind of friendship is hers and Lila's, if Lila has kept Alfonso secret from her for years, knowing how close they were? Could she really not have noticed Michele's absolute dependence on her — or had she chosen to keep it quiet? And how much, in turn, has Elena kept from Lila?

She spends the evening drifting through a chaos of times and places — the spectral Manuela, vacuous Rino, Gigliola at the elementary, Gigliola in middle school, Gigliola dazzled by the Solara *Millecento*, Michele as magnetic to women as Nino but capable of an absolute passion, and Lila — Lila able to draw that passion, not the smutty alleyway kind, but a maximal *valorizzazione* of the woman, a complicated love that named her the chosen one. Elena understands Gigliola's humiliation; she feels next to her.

Later that day she sees Lila and Enzo. She tells Enzo nothing about her reconnaissance, neither for Lila's sake nor for the man living with her. She does use a moment when Lila is feeding Gennaro to tell Enzo that Lila wants to move back to the *rione*. She is honest: she doesn't think it's a good idea, but anything that helps Lila steady herself — or that Lila *believes* will steady her — should be encouraged. Time has passed; the *rione* won't be worse than San Giovanni a Teduccio. Enzo shrugs. *«Non ho niente in contrario. Mi alzerò più presto la mattina, tornerò un po' più tardi la sera.»* Elena mentions the old don Carlo shop, where the widow is moving out to join her children in Caserta. Enzo agrees on the rent. *«Sai che avrete comunque dei problemi.»* — You know you'll have trouble even so. *«Anche qui ce ne sono.»* *«Le resterai vicino?»* *«Finché lei vuole, sì.»*

They take Lila in to look. The place is in bad shape, but Lila lights up — she likes that it's at the edge of the *rione*, almost backing onto the tunnel, with windows looking out on the petrol pump of Carmen's fiancé. Enzo points out that at night they'll be woken by trucks on the *stradone* and by trains in the shunting yard. Lila loves those noises too — the sounds of their childhood. They agree on a price with the widow, and from then on Enzo, each evening, drives to the *rione* instead of home and works on making the flat decent.

The wedding date is closing in. Elena travels back and forth to Florence. Lila, as if Elena's marriage isn't happening, ropes her into shopping for a double bed, a folding bed for Gennaro, an application for a phone line. On the streets of the *rione* people stare; some greet only Elena, some both of them, some pretend not to see either. Lila looks at ease. They cross Ada, who waves cordially and hurries on. They cross Maria, Stefano's mother, who turns her face away. Stefano himself pulls over from a passing car, gets out, talks only to Elena, pats Gennaro lightly, nods at Lila with his head, drives off. They cross old, bent Fernando, Lila's father, in front of the elementary school; Lila wants Gennaro to meet his grandfather, Elena tries to stop her, but Lila insists; Fernando looks at the boy for a few seconds and says, without acknowledging his daughter: *se vedi tua madre, dille che è una troia* — if you see your mother, tell her she's a slut — and walks on.

The most unsettling encounter, though it seems trivial at the time, comes a few days before the move. On the doorstep they run into Melina holding the hand of Maria, the small daughter of Stefano and Ada. Melina is well-dressed for once, hair bleached, face heavily made-up. She recognises Elena but not Lila, or pretends not to — she addresses Elena as if Elena were still her son Antonio's *ragazza*: Antonio will be back soon from Germany, she says, his letters always ask after you. Elena praises her dress and her hair and the granddaughter clinging to her skirt. Melina, perhaps obliged, praises Gennaro and turns to Lila: is he yours? Only then does she seem to remember who Lila is — the woman whose husband her daughter Ada took — and her eyes sink into the great hollows of their sockets. *Lina, sei diventata brutta e secca, per forza che Stefano t'ha lasciata* — Lina, you've gone ugly and skinny, no wonder Stefano left you, men want flesh under their hands. Then, too sharply, she turns to Gennaro: *lo sai che questa è tua sorella? Datevi un bacio, su.* Did you know she's your sister? Give each other a kiss. Gennaro kisses the little girl, Melina sees the two faces side by side and exclaims: *hanno preso tutt'e due dal padre, sono identici* — they both took after the father, they're identical. Then she yanks the granddaughter away.

Lila has stayed silent the whole time. But something very violent has happened in her, the way it did when as a child she watched Melina eat soft soap by the handful on the *stradone*. The moment Melina is gone she jerks, frantically combs her hair with one hand, blinks, says: *diventerò così* — I'll become like that. Then she tries to tidy her hair and asks: *«Hai sentito cosa ha detto?»* Elena tries to soothe: *non è vero che sei brutta e secca*. *«Chi se ne fotte se sono brutta e secca, sto parlando della somiglianza.»* — Who cares if I'm ugly and thin, I'm talking about the resemblance. What resemblance? *«Tra i due bambini: Melina ha ragione, sono tutt'e due identici a Stefano.»* No, Elena says, the little girl yes, but Gennaro is different. Lila laughs — for the first time in ages, her old cruel laugh: *«Sono due gocce d'acqua.»* Two drops of water.

## Summary

Elena doesn't even broach abortion with Pietro — he's delighted at the
prospect of another child — and the word itself sits heavily in her
stomach. Adele mentions it on the phone; Elena swerves with platitudes
about Dede needing company. *Il libro?* her mother-in-law asks. *Sono
a buon punto*, she lies. *Mi farai leggere?* Of course. *Siamo tutti in
attesa.* I know.

Then she does something that surprises Pietro and herself: she calls
her mother. Immacolata says she can't come — your father, your
brothers. Elena yells: that means you'll be the reason I don't write
anymore. *Chi se ne fotte, non ti basta fare la vita della signora?*
Click. Five minutes later Elisa calls: I'll handle the house here,
mamma comes tomorrow.

Pietro picks her up at the station, which thrills her. Elena lays
down a list of rules the moment she walks in: don't touch the rooms;
don't spoil Dede; never get between me and my husband; supervise
Clelia without fighting her; treat me like a stranger; stay in the
kitchen or your room if I have guests. She braces for total
disregard and instead, as if the fear of being sent away has
rewritten Immacolata, her mother turns into a quiet, devoted
servant — running the house with absolute efficiency, never
disturbing anyone.

She goes back to Naples sometimes; each time Elena fears she won't
return; each time she does. She brings news from the rione: Carmen
is pregnant, Marisa has had a son, Gigliola is giving Michele Solara
a second child. About Lila, *taceva* — she keeps quiet, to avoid a
fight. She becomes a domestic ghost in the apartment, clean laundry,
childhood-flavored meals, an instantly reset order. Pietro tries
again to get rid of Clelia and Immacolata sides with him; Elena,
furious, instead of going at Pietro tears into her mother. Immacolata
withdraws to her room without a word. Pietro reproaches Elena, helps
broker a peace, which lands easily — he adores Immacolata, calls her
*una donna molto intelligente*, sits with her in the kitchen after
dinner. Dede calls her *nonna* and clings to her so hard that
Clelia's arrival starts to annoy the child. *Ecco,* Elena thinks —
there, it's all in place, you have no excuses. She forces herself to
sit down to the book.

She reviews her notes and decides for good that she has to change
direction. She wants to leave behind what Franco once called *una
storia di amoretti* — a story of little love affairs — and write
something fit for a time of mass demonstrations, violent deaths,
police repression, fear of a coup. She produces nothing past a
dozen listless pages. What's missing? Hard to say. Maybe Naples —
the rione. Maybe an image like the Fata blu. Maybe a passion. Maybe
a voice she could grant authority. She sits at the desk for hours,
nibbles at novels, never leaves the room for fear of being captured
by Dede. She is unhappy. She hears the child's voice in the
corridor, Clelia's voice, her mother's limping step. She lifts her
skirt and looks at the belly already starting to expand,
broadcasting a kind of physical well-being she didn't ask for. She
is pregnant for the second time and nevertheless empty.

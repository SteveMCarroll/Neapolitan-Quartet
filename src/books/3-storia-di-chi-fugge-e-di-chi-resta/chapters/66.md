## Summary

Elena hands herself over to her mother-in-law with instant relief.
Adele finds, within days, a young woman of just over twenty named
Clelia, from the Maremma, and trains her — house, shopping, kitchen.
Pietro, who wasn't consulted, balks: *non voglio schiave in casa mia*
— I don't want slaves in my house. Adele, calm: *non è una schiava, è
una salariata* — she's not a slave, she's a paid employee. Elena,
emboldened, snaps: *devo fare la schiava io, secondo te?* — should I
be the slave then? Pietro: who's making you? Adele crushes him with
sarcasm. Clelia stays.

Adele takes the baby off Elena, moves the crib into her own room,
runs the bottle schedule day and night. She notices Elena's limp and
takes her to a doctor friend who prescribes a course of injections;
she shows up morning and evening with the syringe and stabs Elena's
buttock with cheerful efficiency. The leg pain disappears. Adele then
quietly rebuilds the rest of her: hairdresser, dentist, conversation
about theater and cinema and the books she's translating, references
to friends she calls by first name. From Adele Elena first hears
about feminist pamphlets — Mariarosa knows the women who write them
and is *infatuata*, smitten; Adele is more guarded. She drops a
couple of the booklets on Elena: *non ti perdere niente, se vuoi fare
la scrittrice* — don't miss anything, if you want to be a writer.
Elena puts them aside. She can also feel that Adele's brilliant
chatter isn't really a need to exchange ideas — *le piaceva più
salvarmi che ascoltarmi*, she liked saving me more than listening to
me.

And. *E poi.* Despite everything, Dede still cries at night and Elena
still can't sleep through it; despite the time, she still can't
write; and Pietro, normally polite, becomes openly rude with his
mother around. Every homecoming ends in a fight between them. Elena
slowly realizes: in his department Pietro is considered an
underperforming Airota, his fast career and foreign reputation
chalked up to his surname; he's been isolated. Students think he's a
pointlessly rigorous bourgeois pedant — a class enemy. He keeps
quiet, keeps teaching, keeps failing students; but it's hard, *però
è difficile*, he almost whines at his mother one night — graffiti on
the walls, colleagues turning the kids against him, lectures
interrupted. Elena, instead of supporting him, snaps: *se fossi un
po' meno reazionario, queste cose non ti succederebbero* — if you
were less reactionary, this wouldn't happen. Pietro answers, for the
first time, harshly: *sta' zitta, parli sempre per frasi fatte* — be
quiet, you only talk in clichés.

She locks herself in the bathroom and realizes she barely knows him.
A pacifist who's stubborn as a mule; on the workers' and students'
side, but a traditional examiner who flunks them; an atheist who
forbade them to baptize Dede but admires the Christian communities
across the Arno; an Airota who can't stand Airota privilege. She
calms down, tries to be kinder, *è mio marito*, we have to talk
more. But Adele's presence is becoming a problem — some unspoken
thing between mother and son keeps detonating. One night at dinner
Adele asks him in front of Elena why he's been sleeping on the
couch. Pietro answers: *domani è meglio che te ne vai* — tomorrow
you should leave. Elena doesn't intervene, although she knows the
real reason for the couch: Pietro is trying not to wake her when
he finally goes to bed at three. Adele leaves for Genoa the next
day. Elena feels lost.

## Summary

The girls fall asleep in front of the television. Elena carries them
to bed. When she comes back, Nino is gone — he has shut himself in
his room. *Depressa*, she clears up, washes the dishes. What a stupid
thing to have asked him to stay longer, she thinks; it's better that
he leaves. And yet — how to bear the squalor without him? She would
have liked at least for him to leave with a promise that, sooner or
later, he would come back: that he would sleep in her house again,
have breakfast with her in the morning, eat at the same table at
night, talk *del più e del meno* in his amused tone, listen to her
when she wanted to give shape to an idea, never resort to irony or
sarcasm with her.

But she has to admit: if the situation has spoiled so quickly that
cohabitation has become impossible, the fault is Nino's. Pietro had
*legarsi* to him, he had liked having him around, he had cared about
the budding friendship. Why had Nino felt the need to hurt him, to
humiliate him, to strip him of authority?

She removes her makeup, washes, puts on her *camicia da notte* —
nightgown. She bolts the door, turns off the gas, lowers all the
shutters, switches off the lights. She checks on the girls. She hopes
Pietro isn't faking sleep, isn't waiting up to argue. She looks at the
night-table: he has taken the tranquilliser, he has *crollato*,
collapsed. Tenderness rises in her; she kisses him on a cheek. *Che
persona imprevedibile*, she thinks — what an unpredictable person:
extremely intelligent and stupid, sensitive and obtuse, brave and
craven, deeply cultured and ignorant, well-mannered and rough. *Un
Airota mancato*, a failed Airota, jammed somewhere along the line.
Could Nino — so sure of himself, so determined — have set him going
again, helped him improve?

She returns to the question of why this nascent friendship has turned
into one-sided hostility, and this time she thinks she sees it. Nino
had wanted to *help* her see her husband for what he really was. He
had become convinced that she held an idealised image of Pietro to
which she had subjected herself both sentimentally and intellectually.
He had wanted to unmask the insubstantial figure behind the young
professor, the author of a thesis turned into an admired book, the
scholar at work on the next publication. It was as if these last days
he had been shouting at her: *vivi con un uomo banale, hai fatto due
figlie con una nullità*, you live with a banal man, you have made two
daughters with a nullity. His project was to *liberarmi svalutandolo*,
to free her by devaluing him, to give her back to herself by
demolishing him. But, in the act, hadn't he realised that he was
offering himself, willingly or not, as an alternative *modello virile*
— a male model?

The question makes her furious. Nino had been reckless. He had thrown
chaos into a situation that, for her, constituted the only possible
equilibrium. Why disorder things without even consulting her? Who had
asked him to open her eyes, to save her? On what basis had he decided
she needed it? Did he think he could do as he pleased with her life
as a couple, with her responsibility as a mother? To what end? Where
did he think this was going? *È lui*, she tells herself, *a doversi
chiarire le idee* — he is the one who needs to clarify his ideas. If
he doesn't care about their friendship — fine. The holidays are
coming. She will go to Viareggio with the family; he has said he will
go to his in-laws' house on Capri. Are they to wait for the end of
the holidays to see each other again? Why? Even now, this summer,
they could shore up the link between their two families. She could
phone Eleonora and invite her, her husband, the child, to spend a few
days with them at Viareggio. She would even like to be invited in
turn to Capri, where she has never been, with Dede, Elsa and Pietro.
And even if none of this should happen — why not write, exchange
ideas, swap book titles, discuss their work plans?

She cannot calm down. *Nino aveva sbagliato.* If he really cared
about her, he needed to put everything back to the starting point. He
had to win back Pietro's sympathy and friendship — Pietro who was
asking for nothing else. Did he genuinely believe he was doing her
good by causing these tensions? No. No. She had to talk to him, tell
him it was foolish to treat Pietro this way.

She gets out of bed quietly. She leaves the room. Barefoot, she walks
the length of the corridor. She knocks on Nino's door. She waits a
moment. She goes in. The room is dark.

*«Ti sei decisa»* — *you've decided* — she hears him say.

She starts. She does not stop to ask herself decided to *what*. She
knows only that he is right, that she has decided. She pulls off her
nightgown in haste, lies down beside him in the dark, despite the
heat.

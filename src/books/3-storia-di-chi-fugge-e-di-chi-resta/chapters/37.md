## Summary

So that's where Pasquale goes after work — straight from the building
site, *ancora con le tracce luride della fatica* (still with the dirt
of labor on him), into this living room full of educated, affectionate
people who take him in as one of their own. Lila's anger flares back.

Nadia hugs Lila in her overcooked way: *meno male che sei qui* (thank
god you're here), leave Gennaro with my mother, we need to talk. Lila
fires back that yes, they do — *that* is exactly why she came — and
she has only a minute. Pasquale offers to drive her home. They all
move to Nadia's bedroom: a big room with a bed, a desk, walls of
books, posters of singers and films and revolutionary struggles Lila
knows nothing about. Three more young people are there. Dario — face
visibly battered from the morning's beating — is sprawled on Nadia's
pink quilt with his shoes still on. The room is full of cigarette
smoke.

Lila doesn't sit. They have got her in a mess. Their stupidity could
get her fired. The pamphlet caused an uproar, they must never come back
to that gate; thanks to them the fascists turned up, and now everyone
hates *both* the reds and the blacks. To Dario, hissing: if you don't
know how to take a beating, stay home — you realize they could have
killed you?

Pasquale tries to interject twice and she shuts him down each time, as
if his very presence in this apartment were betrayal. Armando finally
speaks. Like his mother in his fine features, eyebrows thick, the
trace of a shaved beard purpling his cheeks, voice warm and dense. He
introduces himself, says he's pleased to meet her, regrets missing her
talk at the committee. He explains, calmly: they discussed her words
at length, judged them an important *contributo*, decided to put every
word in writing. *Non ti preoccupare*, he concludes. We'll support you
and your comrades in every way we can.

Lila coughs — the smoke is murder on her throat.

— "You should have told me."

— "True, but there wasn't time."

— "If you want time, you find time."

— "We're few and the initiatives keep multiplying."

— "What work do you do?"

— "In what sense?"

— "What work do you do *to live*."

— "I'm a doctor."

— "Like your father?"

— "Yes."

— "And at this moment are you risking *your* job? Could you end up on
the street tomorrow with your child?"

Armando shakes his head, unhappy. *Fare la gara a chi rischia di più è
sbagliato, Lina* — "competing for who's risking the most is wrong."
Pasquale jumps in: he's been arrested twice, I've got eight charges on
me, there is no more or less, we're all in it.

— "Oh, no?"

— "No," Nadia says placidly. "We're all on the front line, ready to
take responsibility for our actions."

Lila forgets she's in someone else's house and shouts: *e se succede
che perdo il lavoro, vengo a vivere qui, mi date voi da mangiare, la
responsabilità della mia vita ve l'assumete voi?* — "and if I lose my
job, I come live here, you feed me, you take responsibility for my
life?"

Nadia: *Se vuoi sì.* "If you want, yes."

Three words. And Lila understands the girl means it — that even if
Bruno fired the whole staff Nadia would give the same liquor-smooth,
meaningless answer. *Serving the workers* from her book-lined room
with the sea view, telling you what to do with *your* work, with a
solution always already prepared if you ended up in the street. The
retort comes to her tongue — *I can break things much better than you,
gatta morta* ("dead cat" — sanctimonious pretender) — but she swallows
it. She tells Pasquale curtly: I'm in a hurry, are you coming or
staying? Silence. He looks at Nadia, mumbles *t'accompagno*. Lila
walks out without saying goodbye.

In the corridor Nadia hurries after her with another speech — these
working conditions are *inaccettabili*, the spark of struggle must be
lit, *non ti tirare indietro* (don't pull back). No answer.

In the living room Galiani is reading, frowning. She looks up, ignores
Nadia and Pasquale entirely, and addresses Lila.

— "Are you leaving?"

— "Yes, it's late. Move it, Gennaro, give the car back to Marco and
put on your coat."

— "Marco gave it to him as a gift," Galiani says, smiling at the
sulking boy. Lila narrows her eyes to slits: *siete tutti generosi, in
questa casa, grazie* — "you're all so generous in this house, thanks."

Galiani watches her wrestle the coat onto Gennaro. *Posso chiederle
una cosa?* Yes. *Che studi ha fatto?*

Nadia, irritated: Mamma, Lina has to go. For the first time Lila hears
nervousness in the girl's child-voice and is pleased. Galiani snaps:
*mi fai dire due parole?* — "may I get two words in?" — and asks Lila
again, gently: what studies did you do?

— "None."

— "Hearing you talk — and shout — one wouldn't say."

— "It's true. I quit after fifth grade."

— "Why?"

— "I didn't have the ability."

— "How did you know?"

— "Greco had it, I didn't."

— "If you'd studied, you'd have done as well as Greco."

— "How can you say that?"

— "It's my profession."

Lila pulls the wool cap onto Gennaro's head and says: *voi professori
insistete tanto sullo studio perché con quello vi guadagnate il pane,
ma studiare non serve a niente, e nemmeno migliora, anzi rende ancora
più malvagi* — "you teachers insist so much on study because that's how
you earn your bread. But studying is useless, and it doesn't make
people better; it makes them *more wicked*."

— "Is Elena more wicked, then?"

— "No, not her."

— "Why not?"

Lila settles the cap. *Abbiamo fatto un patto da piccole: quella
malvagia sono io.* "We made a pact as little girls: I'm the wicked
one."

## Summary

Back in Viareggio Elena is much fêted by Elsa, who says, pouting:
*papà non sa giocare bene* — dad doesn't know how to play
properly. Dede defends Pietro and tells her sister she's small,
stupid, and ruins every game. Pietro studies Elena, in a bad mood.

*Non hai dormito*, he says — you haven't slept. I slept badly.
Did you find the books? Yes. And where are they? Where do you
think? At home. I checked what I had to check, the end. Why are
you angry? Because you make me angry. We phoned again yesterday
evening, he says. Elsa wanted to say goodnight and you weren't
there. It was hot, she answers; I went for a walk. Alone? Who
else? *Dede dice che hai un fidanzato* — Dede says you have a
fiancé. Dede has a strong bond with you and is dying to take my
place. *O vede e sente cose che io non vedo e non sento* — or she
sees and hears things I don't. What do you mean? What I said.
Pietro, let's be clear: *tra le tue tante malattie vogliamo
metterci anche la gelosia?* — shall we add jealousy to your many
maladies? I'm not jealous. Let's hope so. Because *la gelosia è
troppo, non la sopporto* — jealousy is too much, I can't bear it.

In the next days clashes of that kind multiply. She keeps him at
bay, scolds him, despises herself for it. But she also feels
fury: what is being asked of her, what is she supposed to do? She
loves Nino, has always loved him; how is she to *strapparmelo dal
petto, dalla testa, dalla pancia* — tear him out of her chest, her
head, her belly — now that he wants her too? From childhood
she's built *un perfetto congegno autorepressivo* — a perfect
self-repressing mechanism. *Ora basta* — enough now. Let
everything blow up, her first.

She oscillates. For a few days she doesn't call Nino, exactly as
she'd wisely announced. Then suddenly she calls three or four
times a day with no caution at all, indifferent even to Dede
standing a few steps from the phone booth. *Madida di sudore* —
drenched in sweat in the booth-cage in the sun, exasperated by her
daughter's spy-stare, she'd fling open the door and shout: what
are you standing there for, I told you to look after your sister.

At the centre of her thoughts now is the Montpellier conference.
Nino *assillava* — kept on at her — making it more and more *una
sorta di prova definitiva* — a kind of definitive proof of the
authenticity of her feelings. They swing between violent quarrels
and declarations of indispensability, between long expensive
sulks on the trunk line and the urgency of pouring out their
desire in *un fiume di parole incandescenti* — a river of
incandescent words.

One afternoon, exhausted, with Dede and Elsa singsonging outside
the booth *mamma, ti sbrighi, ci stiamo annoiando* (mum, hurry up,
we're bored), she tells him: there's only one way I can come with
you to Montpellier. Which? *Dire tutto a Pietro* — tell Pietro
everything. Long silence. *Sei veramente pronta a farlo?* — are
you really ready to do it? Yes — but on one condition: *che tu
dica tutto a Eleonora* — that you tell Eleonora everything.
Another long silence. Nino murmurs: do you want me to hurt
Eleonora and the baby? Yes. Aren't I going to hurt Pietro and the
girls? *Decidere significa fare del male* — to decide means to do
harm. Albertino is very small. So is Elsa. And for Dede it will be
unbearable. *Facciamolo dopo Montpellier* — let's do it after
Montpellier. *Nino, non giocare con me*. I'm not playing. Then
don't — you talk to your wife and I'll talk to my husband. Now.
Tonight. *Dammi un po' di tempo* — give me a bit of time. *Per me
lo è?* — is it easy for me?

He temporises. He tries to explain. Eleonora is very fragile, he
says; she has built her life around him and the baby; as a
teenager she'd tried twice to kill herself. He keeps going, forces
himself into total honesty: breaking up his marriage would hurt
his wife and the boy, but also kick away many comforts (*soltanto
vivere in una condizione di agiatezza rende accettabile la vita a
Napoli* — only a comfortable life makes life in Naples bearable)
and a web of relationships that lets him do as he pleases at the
university. Then, carried by his own honesty, he adds: *ricordati
che tuo suocero mi stima molto e che rendere pubblica la nostra
relazione porterebbe sia me che te a una rottura insanabile con
gli Airota* — remember your father-in-law thinks very highly of
me, and making our relationship public would mean for both of us
an unhealable break with the Airota family. For some reason that
last bit hurts her.

*Va bene*, she says — fine, we end it here. Wait. I've waited
quite enough; I should have decided sooner. What do you want to
do? *Prendere atto che il mio matrimonio non ha più senso e
andare per la mia strada* — accept that my marriage no longer
makes sense and go my own way. You sure? Yes. And come to
Montpellier? *Ho detto per la mia strada, non per la tua. Tra me e
te è finita* — I said my own way, not yours. Between you and me
it's over.

## Summary

*l'Unità* takes its time. Elena spends days afraid the piece has been shelved. The morning after the neurologist visit she walks to the newsstand and flips through the paper — not buried in local odds and ends but on a national page, whole, her name in print. She feels it like a long needle going through her.

Pietro calls; Adele calls; even Guido and Mariarosa have read it. The director of her publishing house calls personally; two well-known intellectuals who collaborate with the house also call. And then — startlingly — Franco. Franco Mari, who got her number from Mariarosa, speaks with respect and warmth, says she's given a model of a thorough piece on factory conditions, and that he hopes they can meet soon. Elena keeps waiting for word from Nino. None comes. Pasquale is silent too, but Pasquale has long since stopped reading the party paper. The *l'Unità* editor calls to tell her how much the piece pleased the desk; in his usual ribbing tone he tells her to *«comprati una macchina da scrivere»* — buy yourself a typewriter — and bang out more pieces this good.

The most disconcerting call comes from Bruno Soccavo. His secretary fetches him, and then he speaks in a melancholy register, as if the article — which at first he never names — has flattened him. He says he loved her on Ischia as he has never since loved anyone. He says his father handed him a sick factory full of ugly habits and that he is only the *erede incolpevole*, the blameless heir, of a situation he too finds deplorable. He says her piece — there, he names it — has been illuminating, and he wants to fix many things at once. He apologizes for the misunderstandings with Lila. And then, at the end, quietly: *«tu conosci i Solara, loro in questo frangente difficile mi stanno aiutando»* — you know the Solaras, they're helping me through this difficult moment. *«Michele ti saluta caramente.»*

Elena thanks him, hangs up, and immediately calls Mariarosa's lawyer friend. He confirms the money is being settled and asks her to come by his office. He's not much older than she is, well-dressed, admiring of Guido Airota, friendly to a fault except for thin lips. He counts out the back-pay for Lila and warns Elena not to let anyone snatch it. He describes the chaos at the factory gates — students, union men, police — and the appearance of the *ispettore del lavoro*. And yet, on the threshold, almost embarrassed, he asks one last question:

*«Tu conosci i Solara?»* Yes, Elena says, people from my neighbourhood. *«Lo sai che dietro Soccavo ci sono loro?»* Yes. *«E non sei preoccupata?»* No. He presses: maybe knowing them since childhood, having studied far from Naples, keeps her from seeing it clearly. *«Mi è chiarissima»* — it's perfectly clear to me. He purses those thin lips, hands her the money, and lets her go.

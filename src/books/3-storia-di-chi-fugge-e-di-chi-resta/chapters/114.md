## Summary

They leave for the holiday. Elena barely sees to the children; she
hands them off to Pietro and runs constantly in search of a
telephone, if only to tell Nino she loves him. Twice Eleonora picks
up and she hangs up. Even the sound of Eleonora's voice irritates
her — it seems unfair that she has him next to her day and night;
*cosa c'entrava lei con lui, con noi* — what did she have to do
with him, with us? That irritation helps Elena overcome the fear;
the plan to meet again in Florence begins to feel doable. She tells
Pietro — and it is true — that the Italian publisher *con tutta la
buona volontà* (with all the goodwill in the world) won't manage
to print her book before January, while the French edition is due
out at the end of October. She needs to clear up some doubts
urgently, fetch a couple of books, go home. He offers to drive
back for them. *Sta' un po' con le bambine, non ci stai mai*, she
says — spend a little time with the girls; you never do.

She drives off at dawn, *il cielo striato di bianco* — the sky
streaked with white. She enters the empty flat *col batticuore*
— with her heart thumping. She undresses, washes, looks in the
mirror, troubled by the white patch of her belly and breasts;
dresses, undresses, redresses until she feels beautiful.

Around three Nino arrives. They make love till evening. For the
first time he has the leisure to dedicate himself to her body with
*una devozione, un'idolatria* — a devotion, an idolatry — for
which she is not prepared. She tries not to be outdone, wanting at
all costs to seem skilful to him. But when she sees him *annientato
e felice* — annihilated and happy — something *all'improvviso mi
si guastò nella testa*, something suddenly went wrong in her head.

For her this is a unique experience; for him a repetition. *Amava
le femmine, ne adorava i corpi come feticci* — he loved women, he
adored their bodies as fetishes. She doesn't think of his other
women she knows about — Nadia, Silvia, Mariarosa, his wife
Eleonora. She thinks of what she knows best: the madness for Lila,
*la frenesia che l'aveva portato vicino all'autoannientamento*, the
frenzy that had nearly destroyed him; how Lila had believed in
that passion, clung to his books and ideas and ambitions to give
herself a chance to change; *come era sprofondata*, how she had
sunk when he had abandoned her. Can Nino love and provoke love
only in that excessive way? Is *quel nostro amore folle la
riproduzione di altri amori folli* — is this mad love of theirs the
reproduction of other mad loves? Is his wanting-her-no-matter-what
just the template he used for wanting Lila? Even his coming into
her marital house — is it a re-enactment of when Lila pulled him
into her and Stefano's? *Stavamo non facendo, ma rifacendo?* — are
we not doing this, but redoing it?

She pulls back. *Che hai?* — what's wrong? Nothing. She kisses him,
tries to scrub from her chest the feeling of his love for Lila. He
insists. Finally she can't avoid it; she clutches at a relatively
recent echo and says, in a tone of pretend amusement: *ho qualcosa
di sbagliato nel sesso come Lina?* — am I bad in bed too, like
Lina?

His face changes. *Un estraneo che mi spaventò* — a stranger
appears in it, one who frightens her. Before he can answer she
hurries to whisper: I was joking, if you don't want to answer,
forget it. *Non ho capito cosa hai detto* — I didn't understand
what you said. I only quoted your words. *Mai pronunciata una
frase del genere* — I never said such a thing. Liar — you said it
in Milan, on the way to the restaurant. *Non è vero, e comunque
non voglio parlare di Lina* — it's not true, and at any rate I
don't want to talk about Lina. *Perché?* — why? He doesn't answer.

She turns her back. He brushes her shoulder; she hisses *lasciami
stare* — let me alone. Silence. Then he strokes her again, kisses
her shoulder softly; she gives in. Yes, she admits to herself —
he's right; I must never ask him about Lila again.

The phone rings. It's Pietro with the girls. She motions to Nino
to stay silent, jumps out of bed; her voice comes out too low,
unnaturally hushed, because she doesn't want Nino to hear and
mock. *Perché sussurri così?* Pietro asks. She raises her voice;
it comes out too loud. She makes a fuss over Elsa, tells Dede to
let her father be and to brush her teeth.

When she returns to bed Nino says: *che brava moglie, che brava
mammina* — what a good wife, what a good mummy. She answers: *tu
non sei da meno* — you're no different. They wait for the tension
to slacken, take a shower together (a new and delightful experience
— she likes washing him and being washed). She makes herself
beautiful again, this time *sotto i suoi occhi* — under his eyes,
without anxiety. He watches her, enchanted. She makes him leave
first and wait for her in the car: even though the building is
half-empty for August, she fears being seen with him.

They go out to dinner, eat much, talk much, drink a great deal.
Back home they go to bed but do not sleep at all. He says: in
October I'll be in Montpellier for five days, a conference.
*Divertiti. Vai con tua moglie?* — Enjoy it. Are you going with
your wife? *Voglio andarci con te* — I want to go with you.
Impossible. Why? Dede is six, Elsa is three; she has to think of
them.

They begin to discuss their situation. For the first time they
say words like *sposati, figli* — married, children. They pass
from despair to sex, from sex to despair. At the end she
whispers: *non dobbiamo vederci più* — we mustn't see each other
any more. *Se per te è possibile, bene. Per me non lo è* — if
that's possible for you, good. For me it isn't.

Chatter, she says. You've known me for decades and yet you've had
a full life without me; you'll forget me quickly. *Prometti che
continuerai a telefonarmi tutti i giorni* — promise you'll keep
phoning every day. No, I won't phone you any more. If you don't,
I'll go mad. *Io divento pazza se continuo a pensare a te* — I
go mad if I keep thinking about you.

They explore, with a kind of masochistic pleasure, the *vicolo
cieco* — dead end — they're in, and, exasperated by their own
piling-up of obstacles, end up quarrelling. He leaves furious at
six in the morning. She tidies the house, has a cry, and drives
all the way back hoping never to arrive in Viareggio. Halfway,
she realises she has not packed a single book that would justify
the trip. *Meglio così* — better that way.

## Summary

The blow is as violent as it is unexpected. He wants to know what
is between her and Nino. Just that question, just that name, and
the fountain starts running in her head again. Blinded by tears,
she shrieks at him *fuori di sé* — beside herself, forgetting they
are out on the balcony, that people are sleeping after a day of
sun and sea: why did you ask me that, you should have kept it to
yourself, now you've ruined everything and there's nothing more
to be done — all you had to do was keep quiet; instead you
couldn't, and now I have to leave, *ora me ne devo per forza
andare*, I absolutely have to go.

She does not know what happens to him. Perhaps he becomes
convinced he has just made a mistake that, for obscure reasons,
threatens to wreck their marriage forever. Or perhaps he sees her
all at once as *un organismo rozzo* — a crude organism — bursting
through the fragile surface of discourse and showing itself *in
modo prelogico*, in pre-logical form, *una femmina nella sua
espressione più allarmante* — a female in her most alarming
expression. What is certain is that she must seem an intolerable
spectacle: he leaps up and goes back inside.

She follows, still shouting. Out comes everything: the love for
Nino from childhood on; the new possibilities of life he had
revealed; the unused energies she felt inside her; *il grigiore in
cui mi aveva sprofondata lui per anni* — the greyness in which
*he*, Pietro, had submerged her for years; the responsibility for
having stopped her from living *con pienezza*, fully.

When her strength runs out and she collapses in a corner, she
finds him standing in front of her, *le guance infossate* — cheeks
sunken — eyes sunk in violet patches, *le labbra bianche* — white
lips, his tan turned into a crust of mud. Only then does she
understand she has *sconvolto* — shattered — him. The questions he
had asked did not allow for affirmative replies — yes, I flirted
with the drummer and more besides; yes, Nino and I have been
lovers. He had asked them only to be denied, to settle the doubts
that had risen in him, to go to bed easier. Instead she has shut
him up in a nightmare he doesn't know how to escape.

Almost in a whisper, looking for some way out, he asks:

*Avete fatto l'amore?* — Did you make love?

Again she feels pity for him. If she answered yes, the shouting
would start again; she'd say: yes, a first time while you were
asleep, a second in his car, a third in our bed in Florence. And
she would say those sentences with the voluptuous pleasure such
an inventory would give her.

Instead she shakes her head no.

## Summary

Cholera breaks out in Naples. Elena's mother grows agitated, then distracted,
breaks a soup bowl Elena loved, and announces she has to go home. Elena
understands: the cholera helps, but the real wound is the second granddaughter
not being given her name. Pietro drives Immacolata to the station. *Forse —*
Elena thinks *— s'è sempre contenuta non per fare piacere a me, ma per non
sfigurare con lui* — perhaps she has always held herself in not to please me
but so as not to look bad in front of him. On the landing the mother tries
her stiff Italian on Dede: are you sorry nonna is leaving? The child, taking
the departure as a betrayal, answers stiffly: *no*.

In a fit of self-destruction Elena fires Clelia too. Pietro is alarmed. She
says she is sick of fighting Dede's Maremma accent against her mother's
Neapolitan; she wants to be mistress of her own house again. The real reason
is guilt and a hunger to be punished. Expecting Elsa to be another sleepless
year, she gives in to the idea of being crushed by the children and chores
and the bad leg.

Elsa upends the prediction. Maybe because Elena is more experienced now and
no longer aspires to be a perfect mother, the baby latches without a fuss,
nurses long, sleeps long. Elena finally sleeps too. To everyone's surprise
Pietro takes over the apartment — cleaning, shopping, cooking, bathing Elsa,
coaxing the stunned Dede. The leg pain lifts.

Then, late one afternoon, Pietro wakes her: your friend from Naples is on the
phone. Lila has been chatting with him at length, very keen to meet him in
person. Elena listens with no patience for the friendly small talk — *Pietro
era sempre affabile* — and finally cuts in: *Hai letto o no?* — did you read
it or not. Yes. Well? "It's good." Good how? "It interested me." A lot?
"A lot." Why? "For the story: *viene voglia di leggere* — it makes you want
to keep reading." And then? "Then what?" Elena hardens: I have to know what
this stuff I've written really is, and I have nobody else who can tell me.
"I'm telling you." "You're not. You've never spoken about anything this
superficially." A long silence. Then, hardly under control, Lila says: I
have one job, you have another, what do you want from me, *sei tu quella che
ha studiato* — you're the one who went to school, you know how books are
supposed to be. Her voice breaks, almost a shout: *non le devi scrivere
queste cose, Lenù, tu non sei questo* — you mustn't write things like this,
this isn't you; nothing in what I read resembles you; *è un libro brutto,
brutto, brutto* — bad, bad, bad — and the one before was bad too.

Short, choked phrases — and then Lila is sobbing. Elena's stomach knots, not
at the verdict but at the crying. *Avrei preferito la Lila cattiva* — she
would have preferred mean Lila, snide Lila. Instead she has this. She tries
to soothe her: *calmati, su, respira* — calm down, breathe. Lila keeps
spilling: why did you make me read it, I should have kept it to myself; don't
make me read anything ever again, I'm no good, *mi aspetto da te il massimo*
— I expect the most from you — *chi sono io se tu non sei brava?* — who am I
if you aren't great? Elena murmurs, dazed: tell me always what you think,
*tu mi hai aiutata fin da quand'eravamo piccole* — you've helped me since we
were children, without you I can't do anything. Lila finally swallows the
sobs and laughs at herself: *sono una cretina* — I'm an idiot — I had
prepared a whole positive speech, I'd written it down, I wanted to make a good
impression.

Only then does Elena tell her Elsa is born. They talk Florence and Naples and
the cholera — *quale colera*, Lila scoffs, there is no cholera, just the usual
mess and fear of dying in shit, *mangiamo un sacco di limoni e nessuno caca
più* — we eat heaps of lemons and nobody craps anymore. Lila sounds suddenly
light, almost cheerful, the weight off her chest. Elena turns the talk back to
her own failure with self-mocking lightness: she had wanted the novel to
settle accounts with the rione; the murder of don Achille and the Solara
mother had urged her on. Lila laughs: *la faccia schifosa delle cose non basta
a scrivere un romanzo* — the ugly face of things isn't enough for a novel;
without imagination it isn't a face, it's a mask.

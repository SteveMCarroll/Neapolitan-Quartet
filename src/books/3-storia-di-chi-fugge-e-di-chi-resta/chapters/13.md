## Summary

Elena doesn't go to Paris. After the elections of that turbulent year, Gina sends her out promoting the book. First stop is Florence — a *controcorso* at the faculty of education with thirty‑odd students. She is struck by how poorly dressed and overheated the young women are, how angry at exams and professors. Pushed by the professor who invited her, she enthuses about the student protests, especially France's, recycling lines she's been reading. She avoids talking about the novel itself — too uncomfortable, too close to the rione reactions — and paraphrases pieces from the *Quaderni piacentini* and the *Monthly Review*. The first audience questions stay safely on her protagonist's struggle to escape her birthplace. Then, near the end, a very tall thin girl asks, with nervous laughter, why she felt the need to slip *"un pezzo scabroso"* — a racy passage — into a story otherwise so polished.

She fumbles, probably blushes, piles up sociological excuses, then lands on a line about the need to narrate every human experience frankly, *anche ciò che pare impronunciabile* — even what seems unspeakable. The line works; the professor who invited her praises it and promises to write. The praise stabilises that little speech in her head and it becomes a refrain — used everywhere, sometimes wry, sometimes solemn, sometimes ornately. She is especially fluent at a Turin bookshop, growing comfortable with the now‑familiar provocations about the beach sex scene.

She's been travelling with **Tarratano**, Adele's elderly friend whom the publisher has sent as escort. He boasts of being the first to spot the novel's promise, introduces her with the same rapturous formulas he'd used in Milan, and afterwards congratulates her warmly. Then, gently: *perché accetta così di buon grado che le sue pagine erotiche siano definite scabrose?* — why do you so readily accept the label "racy"? The novel, he reminds her, doesn't reduce to the beach scene; there are better and finer passages, and the only reason any of it sounds bold at all is that a young woman wrote it. *L'oscenità non è estranea alla buona letteratura* — obscenity is no stranger to good literature; real storytelling, even past the line of decency, is never simply *scabroso*. She is wrong‑footed: a very cultured man is telling her, tactfully, that her book's sins are venial and she has been treating them as mortal. She vows to push back harder next time.

At dinner in the hotel restaurant she half‑listens, half‑amused, as Tarratano cites Henry Miller as proof she is *"a substantially chaste writer,"* and lists 1920s and '30s women who wrote about sex in ways she can't yet imagine, calling her *cara bambina* — dear girl. She jots the names in her notebook, then realises that, beneath the compliments, this man doesn't think she has much talent; he treats her as a girl with undeserved success, and considers even the most discussed pages of her novel not really significant.

She says she's tired and helps him — he's drunk too much — to his feet. A small man, big paunch, red face, gold‑yellow fingers from cigarettes. In the lift he tries to kiss and grope her. She wrestles free with difficulty; he doesn't give up. What stays with her is the feel of his belly and his wine breath. At her age it doesn't occur to her that an elderly, cultivated, well‑connected man — Adele's old friend — could behave so basely. Once in the corridor he apologises in a rush, blames the wine, locks himself in his room.

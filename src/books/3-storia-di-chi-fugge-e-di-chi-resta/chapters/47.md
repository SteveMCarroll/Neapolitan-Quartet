## Summary

From that night until Elena's wedding in Florence on 17 May 1969 (after
which a three-day honeymoon in Venice and the start of married life),
Elena does everything she can for Lila. At first she thinks only of
nursing her through the flu. She has the Florence apartment to set up
and a flood of book obligations — the telephone rings constantly, her
mother grumbles that she gave the number to half the rione and no one
calls *her*; this *coso* in the house is more nuisance than anything.
Elena drafts notes for hypothetical new novels, tries to plug the gaps
in her literary and political culture. But Lila's frailty pulls her in.

Her mother *Immacolata* notices that the friendship has been
re-stitched and goes into open warfare — insults to both of them.
*Che hai più da spartire con quella*, what have you still got in
common with that one, think about what you are and what she is, the
filthy book wasn't enough, you want to keep being friends with a
slut? Elena plays deaf. She sees Lila every day and starts
reorganizing her life from the moment she leaves her asleep and walks
into the kitchen, where Enzo and Pasquale are still waiting.

She tells them Lila is sick, can't go back to Soccavo, has resigned.
Enzo doesn't need to be talked to: he saw long ago that she couldn't
keep going, that *qualcosa dentro stava cedendo* — something inside
was giving way. Pasquale resists. *Non esageriamo*, don't exaggerate.
Lila lives a hard life, but so do *gli sfruttati del mondo*, the
exploited of the world. He launches into his usual litany — the
peasants of the South, the workers of the North, Latin America, the
Brazilian Nordeste, Africa, Black Americans, the Vietnamese, US
imperialism. Elena cuts him off: *Pasquale, se Lina continua così
muore* — Pasquale, if Lina keeps on like this she'll die.

He won't yield. The Soccavo struggle matters; Lila's role is central;
underneath, he half-suspects all this fuss over a touch of fever
comes less from Lila than from Elena — *un'intellettuale
piccoloborghese* more worried about a low fever than about *le brutte
conseguenze politiche di una sconfitta operaia*, the ugly political
consequences of a workers' defeat. He won't say it outright; Elena
sums it up for him, calmly and explicitly, just to show she's
understood. That irritates him more. At the gate he says: now I have
to go to work, *però ne riparleremo*, we'll talk again. The moment
she's back in the apartment Elena takes Enzo aside: *tieni Pasquale
lontano da Lina se le vuoi bene, non deve più sentir parlare della
fabbrica.* Keep Pasquale away from Lila if you love her; she mustn't
hear another word about the factory.

A daily rhythm establishes itself. Elena brings a book and her
notebook in her bag, reads on the bus or while Lila dozes. Sometimes
Lila is staring at her, possibly sneaking a look at the cover, but
never asks the title; when Elena tries to read aloud — the inn
scenes from *Upton* (Fielding's *Tom Jones*) — Lila closes her eyes
as if bored. The fever breaks in a few days, the cough doesn't, so
Elena keeps her in bed. She runs the house, cooks, looks after
Gennaro. He is older, a little aggressive, capricious; she finds him
without the disarming sweetness she remembers from *mirko-sarratore*, Nino's
other son. But he swings from violent play to sudden melancholy,
falling asleep on the floor; that softens her, she grows fond of him,
and once he senses it he attaches himself to her, blocking the
housework, blocking the reading.

She works on understanding the practical mess. Money? None. She lends
Lila some — accepted after a hundred swearings to pay it back. What
does Bruno owe? Two months' wages. The severance? She doesn't know.
What does Enzo earn? She doesn't know. The Zurich correspondence
course — what does it actually open up? *Mah.*

The cough won't go. Chest pain, sweats, throat-grip, the heart
suddenly racing. Elena lists every symptom and pushes for a real
medical exam, far more serious than Armando's. Lila doesn't say yes
and doesn't refuse.

One evening, before Enzo gets home, Pasquale stops by — politely. He,
the committee, and some Soccavo workers want to know how she is. Elena
says she isn't well, needs rest. He still asks to see her, just to say
hello. She leaves him in the kitchen and goes to Lila; she advises her
not to see him. Lila makes a small grimace that means: *faccio come
vuoi tu* — I'll do what you want. It moves Elena: Lila, who has always
commanded, made and unmade, surrenders to her without a fight.

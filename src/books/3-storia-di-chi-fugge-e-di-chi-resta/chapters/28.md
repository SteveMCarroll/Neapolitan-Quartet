## Summary

Once *La fata blu* — the Blue Fairy, Lila's childhood story — has gone
up in smoke on the piazzale, Lila goes back to work. She doesn't ask
herself how much that meeting with Elena weighed; she has learned that
looking for reasons hurts her, and she waits until the unhappiness
becomes vague *malumore* (bad mood), then *malinconia*, then the
ordinary daily grind: care for Gennaro, make the beds, clean, wash and
iron the clothes of the child, Enzo, and herself, cook lunch for the
three of them, drop the boy off at the neighbour's with a thousand
recommendations, race to the factory and endure its fatigue and
abuses, come home, look after her son and the children he plays with,
make dinner, eat with the men again, put Gennaro to bed while Enzo
clears and washes up, and then go back to the kitchen *per aiutarlo a
studiare* — to help him study, something he cares deeply about and
which she, tired as she is, won't refuse him.

What does she see in Enzo? The same thing she had tried to see in
Stefano and Nino: a way of *rimettere in piedi ogni cosa* — to set
everything back upright at last in the proper way. But Stefano,
without the screen of money, had turned out hollow and dangerous;
Nino, without the screen of intelligence, *si era mutato in un fumo
nero di dolore* — had dissolved into a black smoke of pain. Enzo, by
contrast, seems incapable of ugly surprises. He had been the elementary-
school boy she had always, for obscure reasons, respected, and now he
is a man so inwardly compact in every gesture, so resolute toward the
world, so gentle with her, that she rules out the possibility he might
suddenly *sformarsi* — go out of shape.

They don't sleep together. Lila can't. Each shuts in his own room;
she hears him moving on the other side of the wall until every sound
stops and only the noises of the building remain. Sleep comes hard
despite the fatigue. In the dark, all the unnameable reasons for her
unhappiness gather around Gennaro. What will this child become? I
mustn't call him Rinuccio, so I don't drag him back toward dialect.
I have to help the children he plays with too, or he'll be spoiled by
them. I have no time, I'm not who I used to be; I don't pick up a pen,
I don't read a book any more.

Sometimes a *peso sul petto* — weight on the chest — wakes her. She
switches on the light, watches her son sleep. She recognises little of
Nino in him; the boy reminds her more of her brother Rino. Younger,
Gennaro had stuck to her constantly; now he's bored, shouts, wants to
run off and play, *le diceva male parole* — says nasty words to her.
*Gli voglio molto bene*, she thinks, but do I love him *as he is*?
Ugly question. The more she looks at him, the more she feels — even if
the neighbour finds him very bright — he isn't turning out the way she
wanted. Maybe it's a lie that quality depends on early childhood. You
have to be *costante* (steady). Gennaro isn't steady, and neither is
she. *La testa mi sbanda in continuazione* — my head is constantly
veering — I'm badly made, and so is he. Then she's ashamed; she
whispers to the sleeping child: you're good, you can read and write
already, you can add and subtract; your mother is stupid, she's never
content. A kiss on the forehead; the light off.

Sleep still won't come, especially when Enzo gets in late and goes to
bed without inviting her to study. Then she imagines an *amante* —
lover — an operaia from his factory, a fellow militant from the PCI
cell he's just joined. *I maschi sono fatti così* — men are made that
way — at least the ones I've known: they have to *chiavare* constantly
or they're unhappy. Enzo can't be different, why would he be? She
rejected him, left him alone in bed — she has no right to ask. What
scares her isn't being homeless; she has the salumificio job and feels
unexpectedly strong — much stronger than when she married Stefano with
all his money. What scares her is losing Enzo's gentleness, his
attention, *la forza tranquilla* (the calm strength) with which he had
saved her first from Nino's absence and then from Stefano's presence.
Especially in her current life he is the only one still attributing
extraordinary capacities to her.

"Lo sai che significa questo?"

"No."

"Guarda bene."

"È tedesco, Enzo, *non lo so il tedesco*."

"But if you focus, after a while you do," he half-jokes.

Enzo, who fought his way to a high-school diploma, considers her — who
stopped at fifth grade — more naturally sharp than himself, with a
miraculous knack for swallowing any subject. So when, on the slimmest
evidence, he becomes convinced that *i linguaggi di programmazione dei
calcolatori elettronici* (the programming languages of electronic
computers) hold humanity's future, and that whichever elite first
masters them will play a stupendous role in history, he turns at once
to her.

"Help me."

"I'm tired."

"We're living a shit life, Lina, we have to change."

"I'm fine like this."

"The boy is with strangers all day."

"He's old enough; he can't live under a glass bell."

"Look what you've done to your hands."

"They're mine, I do what I like with them."

"I want to earn more, for you and Gennaro."

"Mind your own business, I mind mine."

Same abrasive reflexes. Enzo has subscribed to a monthly Swiss
correspondence course — expensive for them — that involves periodic
tests sent to a Zurich data-processing centre, which mails them back
graded. Little by little he draws Lila in, and she pushes herself to
follow. But she acts differently than she used to with Nino, whom she
had bombarded to prove she could help him in anything. With Enzo she
studies *tranquilla*, doesn't try to overpower him. The evenings they
give to the course are an effort for him and a sedative for her.
Maybe that's why, on the rare nights he comes home late and seems able
to do without her, she lies awake in agitation, listening to water in
the bathroom — the water with which she imagines he is washing every
trace of contact with his lovers off his body.

## Summary

The months pass. Dede walks for the first time on her first birthday,
launching herself from Elena toward her crouching father, and from
then on her nights — and Elena's — quiet down. The baby spends more
and more time with Clelia. Elena, like someone after a long illness,
just wants light, color, crowded streets, shop windows. She has her
own money now from the prize and spends it freely: clothes for
herself, for Pietro, for Dede, new furniture, knick-knacks. She wants
to be pretty, see people, talk. But she has made no friends and
Pietro almost never brings anyone home.

Trying to come back to herself, she notices the phone has stopped
ringing. The novel is fading; her name with it. The euphoria of new
freedom curdles into worry, sometimes depression. She rereads
contemporary fiction; her own novel suddenly looks frivolous and very
traditional. She tosses the notes for the new book, which were just
the old one again. She makes herself imagine bigger stories, ones
that hold the *tumulto del presente* — the present's tumult.

She calls *l'Unità* again. It quickly becomes clear they no longer
want her pieces. She's lost ground, has no specifics, can't travel
to see anything firsthand, and writes elegant abstract sentences
that — on the party's own paper — increasingly attack the Communist
Party and the unions from the left. Why she does this is hard for
her to say. Insecurity. Mistrust of compromise — an art she has
associated, since childhood, with her father's small Town Hall
maneuvers. The duty not to forget poverty. A vague longing for
*qualcosa di grande* — something big — that would sweep through her
life so she could narrate it. And — hard to admit — her model is
still Lila, with her stubborn unreasonableness that refuses any
middle ground. Distant from Lila now in every sense, Elena finds
herself wanting to say and do what she imagines Lila *would* say and
do if she had Elena's tools and weren't shut up inside the rione.

She drops *l'Unità* and starts buying *Lotta Continua* and *il
manifesto*. Sometimes Nino's byline appears in the latter — densely
documented, tightly argued. She feels, as she did as a girl talking
to him, the urge to wrap herself in a net of crafted general
propositions to keep from drifting. She notices, without distress,
that she no longer thinks of him with desire or even love. He has
become *una figura del rammarico* — a figure of regret, the synthesis
of what she might never become despite having had the chance. They
came out of the same place; both got out brilliantly; why is she
sliding into greyness? The marriage? Dede? Being a woman, condemned
to the house and the diapers and the shit? Every well-made Nino
piece puts her in a bad mood, and Pietro — her only conversational
partner — pays for it. She accuses him of having abandoned her in
her worst time, of caring only about his career. The relationship
is getting worse and she knows it. He's miserable about work; she
won't grant it, she attacks him politically, often from positions
indistinguishable from the students giving him trouble. He listens
uncomfortably, barely answers. She suspects his line *sta' zitta,
parli per frasi fatte* wasn't a slip — it was a verdict that she
isn't really up to a serious discussion. That tears her in two: she
herself believes both that inequality makes study brutal for some
(her) and a hobby for others (Pietro), *and* that one should study
hard regardless and that she's earned the right to be proud of what
she's done. With Pietro only the first half comes out: *ti comporti
come se avessi davanti studenti tutti uguali, ma non è così* — you
act as if your students were all equal; they aren't.

She criticizes him even when he tells her about a violent row with
an older colleague — an acquaintance of Mariarosa's who tried to
counsel him to go easier on the kids who, after all, spend their
time *cambiare questa baracca* (changing this dump). Pietro,
defending himself, ended up accusing the colleague of using two
weights and two measures: indulgent with the most aggressive
students, cruel with the more timid ones. The colleague called him
a *cretino* unworthy of his chair. Elena sides with the colleague.
*Non potresti essere più cauto?* — couldn't you be more careful? *Sono
cauto.* *Non mi pare.* Then, hissing: the consequence of this way of
being is that in this whole city no one — not even your parents'
friends — invites us to dinner or a concert or anywhere.

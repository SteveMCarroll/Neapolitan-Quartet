## Summary

Elena feels abandoned and convinced she deserves it: she can't keep
her own daughter calm. She grinds on, more frightened by the day. Her
body, she decides, is refusing the role of mother. The leg pain
returns and grows. With no elevator she hauls the carriage up and
down the stairs, does the shopping, comes back loaded with bags,
cleans, cooks, and watches herself becoming *brutta e vecchia prima
del tempo, come le donne del rione* — ugly and old before her time,
like the women of the neighborhood.

And of course this is exactly when Lila calls. Elena wants to scream
at her: what did you do to me, everything was fine, now everything you
predicted is coming true. Instead she puts on the show — *tutto bene*,
the baby is meravigliosa, I'm happy — and asks after Enzo, Gennaro,
Stefano, the rione. Lila answers in a dirty, aggressive dialect:
Soccavo *deve buttare il sangue* (must bleed); if she meets Michele
she'll spit in his face; Gennaro is now *il figlio di Stefano*, square
like his father, and when Elena calls him sweet, Lila tosses back
*sei una così brava mammina, prenditelo* — "you're such a good little
mom, take him." Only when she talks about Enzo's IBM course does the
sarcasm drop. Then she asks if Elena is happy with Pietro. *Benissimo.*
*Anch'io con Enzo.* The conversations leave Elena haunted for hours
afterward by images from the past — the cortile, the doll, don
Achille's stairs.

She keeps up the same performance with everyone: with her sister
Elisa, with Carmen (now engaged to the stradone gas-station man),
with her mother. To her mother, in one weak moment, she lets slip
*cosa ti è successo alla gamba, perché zoppichi* — "what happened to
your leg, why do you limp?" Immacolata answers: *che te ne fotte,
fatti i fatti tuoi* — none of your business.

She fights to keep the darker parts of herself down. She catches
herself praying to the Madonna, an atheist, and is ashamed. More often
she just screams — not words, only breath. She walks Dede through the
corridor at night with her bad leg, no longer murmuring nonsense,
ignoring the baby, trying to read. She tries to write articles. They
come out *vuoti* — empty: *parole e basta*, Pietro tells her after
reading one. Words and nothing else. She dictates it anyway; *l'Unità*
doesn't run it; from then on, both the local desk and the national
desk start declining her pieces with polite excuses about space.
Reading itself collapses to staring at signs: she can no longer get at
the meanings. She runs across a couple of Nino's articles. They no
longer thrill her. She is glad for him in the abstract — if he writes,
he's well — but every line of his on the page makes her own
situation more unbearable.

She stops looking after herself. Pietro treats her with formal
politeness; she's a shadow to him. She suspects her marriage has just
complicated his career exactly as he was starting to be known in
England and the U.S. She admires him, which only makes her angrier.
*Basta,* she tells herself — enough; let *l'Unità* go, just find the
path to a new book. But she has nothing but listless notes. One late
afternoon Pietro comes home from the university and finds her
collapsed in the kitchen, head on the table; Dede is screaming in the
bedroom, half-naked, forgotten, having missed her feed. When the baby
is settled with the bottle, Pietro asks, devastated, *possibile che
non hai qualcuno che possa aiutarti?* — "isn't there really anyone
who can help you?" Not in this city, she says. Then your mother, your
sister. *Non voglio.* — I don't want them. *Allora chiedi a quella tua
amica di Napoli: tu ti sei adoperata per lei, lei farà lo stesso per
te.* For a split second Elena is certain Lila has slid inside Dede and
is looking at her through the baby's narrowed eyes. She shakes the
image off. Pietro, very reluctantly, phones his mother.

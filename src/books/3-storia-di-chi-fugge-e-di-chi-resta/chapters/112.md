## Summary

Nino leaves an hour later, even though Pietro insists, a little
*imbronciato* — sulky — that he stay, and even though Dede bursts
into tears. Pietro washes and reappears, ready to go out. With his
eyes down he tells Elena: I didn't tell the police that Pasquale and
Nadia had been at our house — and I didn't do it to protect you, but
because I think people are now confusing dissent with crime. She
hardly knows what he is talking about. Pasquale and Nadia have
slipped entirely out of her mind; she has to work to drag them back.
Pietro waits a few seconds in silence, hoping perhaps she will agree
with him, that for once they will be on the same side as he goes
out to face a day of heat and exams. She manages only a distracted
nod. What does she care now about his political opinions, about
Pasquale and Nadia, about *la morte di Ulrike Meinhof* (the death
of Ulrike Meinhof), about the founding of the socialist republic of
Vietnam, about the gains of the Communist Party at the polls? *Il
mondo s'era ritratto* — the world has shrunk back. She feels sunk
inside herself, inside her own flesh, which now seems the only
inhabitable space, the only matter worth puzzling over. It is a
relief when Pietro — *il testimone dell'ordine e del disordine*, the
witness of order and disorder — closes the door behind him; she
fears that under his gaze her swollen kissed lips and her scorched
body will become visible at any moment.

The moment she's alone, the certainty hits her: she will never see
or hear Nino again. And alongside it a second certainty: she can no
longer live with Pietro; she cannot bear to keep sharing a bed. She
runs through the logistics in her head. She'll leave him; she'll
take the children. But how? She knows nothing about separations and
divorces. She knows no other couple who has done it. What happens
to the children? Where do you live? Milan would be the practical
answer — the publishing house is there, she could find work. But
Dede and Elsa, their relationship with their father — could she
take them that far? Yet her thoughts keep pulling her toward Naples.
Not the *rione* — never the *rione*. The *Napoli abbagliante*, the
dazzling Naples she has never lived in, *a pochi passi dalla casa di
Nino*, a few steps from Nino's house, on via Tasso. To see him from
the window when he goes to or comes back from the university, run
into him in the street, talk to him every day. *Senza disturbarlo* —
without disturbing him. Without causing him trouble with his family;
on the contrary, strengthening her friendship with Eleonora. That
proximity would be enough.

She catches herself. Going off and pitching tent at his door would
make her look like *una donnetta fuori di testa* — some little
woman out of her mind, the kind of clinging female who horrifies
Mariarosa's friends. And not at all up to his standard. He loves
many women, sleeps in many beds, scatters children without
commitment, considers marriage *una convenzione necessaria* that
cannot cage desire. She has done without many things in her life;
she can do without Nino too.

Then the telephone rings. She runs. It's him. He has just arrived at
Naples station; behind his voice there is a loudspeaker, clamour.
Only a hello, he says — I wanted to know how you are. Fine, she
answers. What are you doing? About to eat with the girls. Is Pietro
there? No. Did you enjoy making love with me? Yes. A lot?
*Moltissimo.* I've no more *gettoni* (phone tokens left). Go on,
ciao, thanks for calling. We'll talk again. Whenever you want. She
is pleased with her self-control: she has kept him at the right
distance.

Three hours later he calls again, from another public phone. Why
are you so cold? I'm not cold. This morning you wanted me to tell
you I loved you and I told you, even though on principle I never
say it to anyone, not even my wife. I'm glad. And do you love me?
Yes. Are you sleeping with him tonight? Who else would I sleep
with? I can't bear it. Don't *you* sleep with your wife? It's not
the same. Why? I don't care anything about Eleonora. Then come
back here. How? *Lasciala* — leave her. And then?

The calls become obsessive. She adores those rings. They make
absurd, escalating oaths to each other; she asks if he has slept
with his wife since they were together — he shouts no; she has to
swear the same, and does. The calls drive her to wait by the
telephone; when she has to go out for groceries he lets it ring on
and on into the empty flat until she's back, dumping bags and
children to grab the receiver. He says, despairing: I thought you
weren't going to answer any more. Then, relieved: *però avrei
telefonato per sempre* — I would have gone on phoning forever; in
your absence I would have loved the sound of the telephone, that
empty ringing seemed the only thing I had left.

He rehearses their night in minute detail: *ti ricordi questo, ti
ricordi quello* — do you remember this, do you remember that. He
lists what he wants to do with her — not only sex: a walk, a trip,
the cinema, a restaurant, telling her about his work, hearing how
hers is going. She loses control: yes yes yes, everything, anything
you want — and then bursts out that she's leaving for the seaside
in a week, with Pietro and the girls, as if she were being deported.
He says Eleonora is going to Capri in three days; the moment she
leaves he'll come to Florence even just for an hour.

Through all this, Elsa watches and asks: *mamma, con chi parli
continuamente, vieni a giocare* — mum, who are you always talking
to, come and play. And Dede, *un giorno* — one day — says:
*lasciala stare, parla col fidanzato* — leave her alone, she's
talking to her boyfriend.

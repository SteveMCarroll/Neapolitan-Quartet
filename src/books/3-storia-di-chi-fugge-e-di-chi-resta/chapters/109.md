## Summary

A few days before Nino's departure two especially ugly episodes break
the surface.

In the afternoon Adele phones, also very happy with the new
manuscript, and tells Elena to send it to the publisher at once: they
can issue a small volume to coincide with the French edition, or
shortly after. At dinner Elena announces it offhandedly. Nino piles
on the compliments and tells the girls: *avete una mamma
eccezionale*, you have an exceptional mother. Then he turns to
Pietro:

"Have you read it?"

"I haven't had time."

"Better that you don't."

"Why?"

"It's not stuff for you."

"What do you mean?"

"It's too intelligent."

"What do you mean?"

"That you're less intelligent than Elena."

He laughs. Pietro says nothing. Nino keeps at him: *ti sei offeso?*
— are you offended? He wants Pietro to react so he can humiliate him
again. But Pietro stands up from the table: *scusate, ho da lavorare*
— excuse me, I have work to do. Elena murmurs: finish eating. He
doesn't answer. They were eating in the large *soggiorno* (living
room) and for a moment it looks like he'll cross it and shut himself
into his study. Instead he makes a half turn, sits on the sofa, and
switches on the television *alzando parecchio il volume* — turning
the volume way up. The atmosphere becomes intolerable. In a handful
of days, everything has become impossibly complicated. *Mi sentii
molto infelice* — I felt very unhappy.

"Could you turn it down a bit?" she asks.

"No."

Nino lets out a little laugh, finishes eating, helps her clear up. In
the kitchen she tells him: forgive him, Pietro works too much and
sleeps too little.

He bursts out, angrily: *come fai a sopportarlo?* — how can you stand
him?

She glances at the door in alarm; thank God the television is loud.

"I love him," she answers. And, as he keeps trying to help with the
dishes, she adds: please go, otherwise you're in my way.

The other episode is uglier still, and decisive. Elena is no longer
sure what she wants; by now she half hopes the visit will end soon so
that she can return to the routines of the household and concentrate
on her little book. But she also loves walking into Nino's room in
the mornings, putting his messy things in order, remaking his bed,
cooking with the thought that he will be at the table at night. And
the thought that all of this is about to end is anguishing. In
certain hours of the afternoon she feels half-mad. The house seems
empty despite the children; she herself feels empty; she finds her
own writing flat, suddenly superficial; she loses faith in the
enthusiasm of Mariarosa, of Adele, of the French publisher, of the
Italian one. She thinks: *appena lui se ne andrà, niente più avrà
senso* — the moment he leaves, nothing will have any meaning anymore.

She is in this state — life slipping past her with an unbearable
sense of loss — when Pietro comes home from the university particularly
*torvo* (sullen). Dinner is waiting. Nino has been in for half an
hour but has been immediately captured by the girls. She asks
gently: *è successo qualcosa?* — has something happened?

He explodes: *non mi portare mai più in casa gente delle tue parti* —
don't ever bring people from your part of the world into this house
again.

She freezes; she assumes he means Nino. And Nino, who has just
appeared with Dede and Elsa in tow, must assume the same, because he
looks at Pietro with a provoking little smile, as if waiting for the
scene. But Pietro has something else in mind. With his contemptuous
tone — the one he uses when he believes basic principles are at stake
and feels called upon to defend them — he announces: *oggi sono
ricomparsi i poliziotti e mi hanno fatto dei nomi, mi hanno mostrato
delle foto* — today the policemen turned up again and gave me names,
showed me photos.

Elena breathes out. She thinks she knows what is wearing him down —
the visits from the police, who treat him as an informer, ever since
he refused to drop the *denuncia* against the student with the gun.
She cuts in, sharply: it's your own fault, you shouldn't have done
it, I told you, now you'll never get rid of them.

Nino butts in, mocking: *chi hai denunciato?* — who did you denounce?

Pietro doesn't even turn to look at him. He is angry at Elena, with
Elena. *Ho fatto il necessario allora e avrei dovuto fare il
necessario oggi. Ma sono stato zitto perché ci sei di mezzo tu* — I
did what I had to do then and should have done what I had to do
today; but I kept quiet because you are involved.

Now Elena understands the police visit isn't the problem; it's what
they told him. She murmurs, slowly: what do I have to do with it?

His voice changes. *Pasquale e Nadia non sono amici tuoi?* — aren't
Pasquale and Nadia friends of yours?

She repeats, obtusely: Pasquale and Nadia?

The police, he says, showed him photographs of *terroristi* — and
theirs were among them.

She can't respond. What she had imagined, then, was true. Pietro has
confirmed it. For a few seconds she sees again Pasquale emptying his
pistol into Gino, kneecapping Filippo, while Nadia — *Nadia, non
Lila* — climbs the stairs to Bruno's flat, knocks on the door, enters,
shoots him in the face. Terrible. And yet, in this moment, Pietro's
tone feels off — as though he is using the news to put her in a bad
position in front of Nino, to start an argument she has no appetite
for. Nino keeps at him: *dunque sei un informatore della polizia? E
lo sa tuo padre? E tua madre? E tua sorella?* — so you're a police
informer? Does your father know? Your mother? Your sister?

Elena weakly says: let's eat. And then, to head off Nino dragging
Pietro's family into it, she minimises: *quale informatore* — what
informer — and explains vaguely about Pasquale coming to visit a while
ago, *un buon ragazzo del rione* who had ended up with Nadia, the
Galiani girl, that one. Nino is already laughing. *Nadia, oh Dio
santo, Nadia*, and to Pietro, more sarcastic than ever: only you and
two stupid cops could think Nadia Galiani is in the armed struggle;
*cose da pazzi*. *La persona più buona e gentile che io abbia mai
conosciuto.* The defence of the constituted order can do without
you for tonight. He heads for the table, calling the girls. Elena
starts serving, convinced Pietro will join them.

He doesn't. She thinks he's washing his hands, calming down. She sits
down at her place. She's agitated; she wanted a quiet evening, a
peaceful ending to the cohabitation. The girls are already eating.
Even Nino looks puzzled now.

"Start," she tells him, "it's getting cold."

"Only if you eat too."

She hesitates. Maybe she should check on Pietro. But she doesn't feel
like it; his behaviour has annoyed her. Why couldn't he have kept the
business with the police to himself, as he usually does? Why had he
said it in front of Nino — *non mi portare mai più in casa gente
delle tue parti*? She begins to eat. They eat all four, *primo*,
*secondo*, even the dessert she had prepared. Pietro doesn't come.
She becomes furious. He doesn't want to eat? Fine, let him not eat —
clearly he isn't hungry. He wants to keep to himself? Fine, the house
is big, without him there'll be no tension. Now it's perfectly clear
that the problem isn't two people who had appeared at their house
exactly once turning out to be suspected of belonging to an armed
band; the problem is that he doesn't have a quick enough mind, can't
withstand the *schermaglia tra maschi* — the skirmish between males —
that he suffers it, and takes it out on her. *Ma cosa m'importa di te
e della tua pochezza.* I'll clear up later, she says aloud, as if
issuing an order to her own confusion. Then she switches on the
television and settles on the sofa with Nino and the girls.

A long, nerve-grinding time passes. She senses Nino at once
embarrassed and amused. Dede, full now, is suddenly worried about her
father; *vado a chiamare papà*, I'll go fetch him. Go, Elena says. She
comes back almost on tiptoe, whispers in her ear: he's in bed,
asleep. Nino hears it anyway. He says:

"I'll leave tomorrow."

"Have you finished your work?"

"No."

"Stay longer."

"I can't."

"Pietro's a good person."

"Are you defending him?"

Defending him from what, from whom? She doesn't understand. She is on
the point of being angry with Nino too.

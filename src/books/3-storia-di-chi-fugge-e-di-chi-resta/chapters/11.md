## Summary

The novel really is selling. Gina from the press office calls almost daily with new write‑ups and invitations to bookshops and culture clubs, always signing off with *"il libro corre, dottoressa Greco"* — "the book is running." Elena isn't pleased; she finds the pieces shallow, all of them either copying *l'Unità*'s rave or the *Corriere*'s hatchet job. She stops hiding the bad reviews from her mother and just hands them all over. **Immacolata** can never get past four or five lines before she finds a reason to fight or, bored, slips back into pasting clippings into her scrapbook — terrified of ending up with empty pages.

The review that hurts most appears in the *Roma*. It rehashes the *Corriere* line in flowery prose and chisels away at a single point: women are losing every restraint, see Elena Greco's obscene novel for proof; it's just a leftover of the *"already crude"* *Bonjour tristesse*. What stings is the byline — **Donato Sarratore**, Nino's father. She remembers being awed as a teenager that he was the author of a book of poems. Was this his revenge for recognising himself in the lecherous father in her novel? She nearly phones to scream insults in dialect, but stops because she suddenly sees how much she and Nino have in common: both have spent years putting distance between themselves and their families.

But the *Roma* is the newspaper most read in the rione, and it shows. **Gino**, the pharmacist's son — now a muscle‑bound MSI activist swaggering in a white coat he hasn't earned — flags her down with the paper: *"hai visto cosa scrivono di te?"* — "have you seen what they write about you?" He adds, sleazy, that he's going to have to read this book of hers. The next day **Michele Solara** corners her on the street and drags her into his bar for a coffee. Gigliola serves them in stony silence. Michele needles: *"Lenù, è un libro vietato ai minori di diciotto anni"* — a book rated over‑18 — is this what Pisa taught her? He floats his theory that she and Lila have a secret deal: *"lei fa le cose brutte e tu le scrivi"* — "she does the ugly things, you write them down." Elena goes red and walks out; he calls after her, laughing.

A trip to the Carraccis' new salumeria puts her opposite **Carmen Peluso**, who is sweet, calls knowing her the best luck of her life, denounces the Sarratore piece as pure malice, and waves the much better *l'Unità* article her brother **Pasquale** passed her. Then, in the same breath, she lays out her own troubles: since Ada took Lila's place beside Stefano, Ada plays the boss in both shops and Carmen wants to quit and go work the petrol pump with her fiancé. She fishes out a battered copy of Elena's novel from a drawer — the first one Elena has seen in a rione hand — and asks, sly, *"quanto devo leggere per arrivare alle pagine spinte?"* — "how much do I have to read before I get to the dirty pages?" *Le pagine spinte.* The dirty pages. The phrase lodges.

Outside she runs into **Ada** carrying baby Maria. After what Carmen said, Elena can barely be cordial. Ada is curt: she writes to Antonio, who is *not* married with children no matter what people say; Elena *"burned out his brain and his capacity to love."* Then she pivots to the novel — no, she hasn't read it, but *"non è un libro da tenere in casa"* — it's not a book to keep at home. If the baby grows up and finds it, what then? She isn't buying. But, she adds, glad you're making money. Auguri.

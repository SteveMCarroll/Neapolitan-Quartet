## Summary

It has gone well past *fidanzamento* (engagement), then. Elena ignores
her mother's stalling — *resta qua, andiamo tutti insieme più tardi*
— calls Elisa, says she's on her way, leaves Pietro and the
children with her parents, and walks.

The rione is more degraded than ever. Buildings flaking, road full
of potholes, dirt. The walls are papered with *manifesti listati a
lutto* — black-bordered death notices, more than she's ever seen —
announcing the death of old Ugo Solara, Marcello and Michele's
grandfather. The dating shows it wasn't recent: at least two months
ago. The high-flown phrases, the painted dolent madonnas, even the
dead man's name have faded and run, but the posters cling to the
walls as if every other death has politely vanished without saying
so. There are clusters of them around the door of Stefano's
*salumeria*. The shop is open but it looks like a gash in the wall,
dark and empty; Stefano, in white coat, drifts to the back like a
ghost.

She climbs toward the railway, past what they used to call the
*salumeria nuova*. Its rolling shutter, partly off its rails, is
rust-eaten and scribbled over with obscenities. That whole zone is
abandoned; the dazzling white of years ago has gone grey. She passes
the apartment where Lila used to live: the few stunted little trees
have mostly died; *un nastro per pacchi* (parcel tape) holds the
cracked glass of the front door together. Elisa lives much further
up, in a better-kept, more pretentious area. The doorman — a small
bald man with thin moustache — stops her hostilely. She blurts:
*Solara*. He goes deferential at once and lets her by.

Only in the lift does she realise how much of herself has slid
backwards. What would be perfectly acceptable in Milan or Florence —
a woman's free use of her own body and desires, a *convivenza fuori
dal matrimonio* (living together outside marriage) — here in the
rione she finds *inconcepibile*. It's her sister's future. Has
Elisa really set up house with someone as *pericolosa* as Marcello?
And her mother is happy? The same mother who raged because Elena
married civilly and not in church; who calls Lila a *troia* for
living with Enzo, Ada a *gran puttana* for sleeping with Stefano —
that mother is fine with her youngest daughter sleeping with
Marcello Solara, *al di fuori del matrimonio*? Halfway up she
realises her *testa disciplinata* doesn't know what to say. Will
she fall back to arguments her mother would have used a few years
ago — regress to a level her mother herself has abandoned? Or will
she say: go live with whomever you like, just not Marcello Solara?
And to which girl in Florence or Milan would she ever say *leave
the man you love, whoever he is*?

When Elisa opens the door, Elena hugs her so hard the younger
woman murmurs, laughing, *mi fai male.* Elena senses her on the
defensive. Elisa moves her to the *pretenzioso salotto* — sofas
and floral armchairs with gilt backs — and unspools small talk
in a hurry: how well Elena looks, what beautiful earrings, what
a beautiful necklace, how elegant, how she can't wait to meet
Dede and Elsa. Elena describes the girls with enthusiasm, takes
off her earrings, makes Elisa try them on in the mirror, gives
them to her. Elisa brightens, laughs, murmurs: *Ho avuto paura
che fossi venuta per rimproverarmi, per dire che eri contraria
al mio rapporto con Marcello.*

A long look. *Elisa, io sono contraria. E ho fatto questo viaggio
apposta per dirlo a te, a mamma, a papà e ai nostri fratelli.*
Elisa's eyes fill: *ora mi fai dispiacere: perché sei contraria? —
I Solara sono brutta gente. — Marcello no.*

She tells Marcello's version. It started while Elena was pregnant
with Elsa. Immacolata had gone up to help, and Elisa was left
with the whole weight of the family at home. She went shopping at
the Solara supermarket; Rino, Lila's brother, offered to send the
groceries home if she'd leave the list. While Rino spoke she
noticed Marcello signalling a friendly hello from far off, as if
to make clear the favour was his idea. From then on, he was
constantly attentive, *con buona educazione*, never a single
gesture or word of the ugly Solara kind. He was a truly decent
person, *con lui ci si sentiva al sicuro*, a presence ten metres
tall.

And the whole rione, and the world beyond it, *avevano cominciato
a trattarla come una regina*. People who'd never seen her before
greeted her. *Prima non sei nessuno e subito dopo ti conoscono
anche i sorci delle saittelle* — before, you're nobody; suddenly
even the rats in the gutters know you. (Elena, she says, is used
to that from books; she isn't.) Marcello takes care of everything;
her every wish is his command. The more time passed, the more she
fell in love. She said yes. Now if she doesn't see or hear him for
a day she lies awake all night crying.

Elena sees there is no opening. Elisa believes the impossible has
befallen her. Every word is careful: she separates Marcello from
the Solara family; she speaks affectionately of his mother, of his
sick-stomached father who hardly leaves the house anymore, of *la
buonanima del nonno*, even of Michele, who up close, she says, is
not what people think — *molto affettuoso.* And, of course: thanks
to Marcello, Gianni and Peppe, who used to waste their days,
*adesso Marcello li utilizza pagandoli strabene.*

*Se le cose stanno così, sposatevi.* *Lo faremo*. But now isn't a
good time — Marcello has *affari complicati* to sort out, and
there's mourning for the grandfather (poor thing, lost his mind in
the end, didn't remember how to walk or speak — *Dio riprendendoselo
l'ha liberato*). But once things are settled, they'll marry, don't
worry. And anyway, before marriage *è meglio vedere se si sta bene
insieme, no?* — words Elena recognises as borrowed from women's
magazines, words really not so different from her own on the same
subjects, only a touch cruder.

She could say: there's nothing to wait and see, Elisa, it's already
clear — Marcello will use you up, get used to your body, leave you.
But the words sound old; not even her own mother dared use them.
She gives up. *Io mi ero tirata via, Elisa era rimasta.* What would
*she* be, if she had stayed? Hadn't she liked the Solara boys too
as a girl? And what has she gained by leaving — not even the words
to convince her sister not to ruin herself. Elisa has a delicate
face and a slender body and a soft voice. Marcello she remembers
as tall, handsome, sun-coloured, all muscle, capable of intense
durable feelings — he proved that with Lila; no one has heard of
him loving anyone else since. So — what to say? They end up with
Elisa fetching a box and showing her all the jewels Marcello has
given her, objects beside which Elena's earrings are tiny things.

*Stai attenta*, Elena tells her, *non ti perdere. E se hai bisogno
telefonami.* She makes to rise. Elisa, laughing, blocks her.
*Dove vai, mamma non te l'ha detto? Stanno venendo tutti qui a
cenare. Ho preparato un sacco di roba.* Elena, displeased: *Tutti
chi?* *Tutti: è una sorpresa.*

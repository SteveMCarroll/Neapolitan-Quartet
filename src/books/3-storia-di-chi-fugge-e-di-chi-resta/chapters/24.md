## Summary

The book keeps selling through the summer. Elena tours Italy talking
about it, learning to defend it with a more detached tone, sometimes
freezing the more intrusive members of an audience. Gigliola's words
keep coming back — Elena mixes them with her own, trying to find a
place for them.

In early September Pietro moves to Florence and takes a *alberghetto*
(small hotel) near the station while he flat-hunts. He finds a small
two-room rental near Santa Maria del Carmine and Elena goes to look.
The rooms are dark, the kitchen tiny, the bathroom windowless — the
bath itself a chipped *vasca* you sit in. Elena swallows her
disappointment: it'll do, Pietro is starting his course, he can't
waste time. And anyway it's a palace compared to her parents'.

Then, just as Pietro is about to sign the lease, Adele swings through.
She has none of Elena's deference. She pronounces the apartment a
*tugurio* — a hovel — completely unsuitable for two people who work
mostly at home. So she does what Pietro could have done and didn't:
picks up the phone and mobilises Florentine connections. Within days,
ignoring Pietro's ostentatious displeasure, she has found a five-room
place at San Niccolò — luminous, big kitchen, decent bathroom, a
favour-rent. She arranges renovations at her own expense and helps
Elena furnish.

Adele drafts in Elena as her apprentice. She floats hypotheses, gives
advice, *mi indirizzava* — steers her. But Elena often finds her
mother-in-law doesn't trust her acquiescence or her taste: if Elena
says yes, Adele probes to be sure she means it; if she says no, Adele
keeps pushing till Elena changes her mind. They almost always end up
doing what Adele wants. Elena rarely fights, *anzi mi sforzavo di
imparare* — on the contrary, she tries to learn. She's spellbound by
Adele's rhythm of speech, her gestures, hairstyles, clothes, shoes,
brooches, necklaces, earrings. Adele likes her attentive-pupil pose:
gets her to crop her hair very short, takes her to a very expensive
shop where Adele has standing discounts, gives her a pair of shoes she
herself would have bought but considered too youthful for her own age,
even takes her to her own dentist.

Meanwhile the wedding slides — apartment still needing fresh
interventions, Pietro buried in work — from autumn to spring. That
gives Immacolata more time to wage her usual war for money. To keep
the peace Elena makes sure to show her family she hasn't forgotten
them: a phone line installed, hallway and kitchen repainted, *un nuovo
parato a fiori color vinaccia* (new wine-coloured floral wallpaper) in
the dining room, a coat for Elisa, a TV bought on instalments. She
even gives herself something: enrols in driving school, passes easily,
gets her *patente* (licence). Immacolata flares up — what's the point
of a licence if you don't own a car? You're going to buy one, eh? How
much do you really have set aside? *«Non sono fatti tuoi»* — none of
your business.

The car is Pietro's; once married, Elena plans to use it. When he
drives down again to bring his parents to meet hers, he lets her drive
a little around the old rione and the new — past the elementary school,
the library, up the streets where Lila had lived as a married woman.
That bit of driving is the only fun thing she remembers from the day.
The rest is dreadful, then an endless dinner: Elena and Pietro both
struggle to coax their two families out of their discomfort. *Erano
mondi così distanti* — they were such distant worlds — that the
silences stretch on. When the Airotas leave laden with leftovers
Immacolata has forced on them, Elena suddenly thinks she has got
everything wrong. She came from this family; Pietro from that; each
carried his ancestors in his body. How would their marriage go? Would
the affinities outweigh the differences? Would she write another book?
When, about what? Would Pietro back her? Would Adele? Mariarosa?

That kind of thought is in her head one evening when a voice calls up
from the street. She recognises Pasquale Peluso at once. From the
window she sees Enzo with him. Her chest tightens — at this hour Enzo
should be in San Giovanni a Teduccio with Lila and Gennaro. *«Puoi
scendere?»* Pasquale calls. What's wrong? *«Lina non si sente bene e
ti vuole vedere»* — Lila isn't well and wants to see you. I'm coming,
Elena says, and tears down the stairs while her mother shouts after
her: where are you going at this hour, come back.

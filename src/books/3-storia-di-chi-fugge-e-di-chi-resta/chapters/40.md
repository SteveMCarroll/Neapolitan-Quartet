## Summary

Pasquale comes by in the evening, after dinner, with a glum face, and
asks Enzo — not Lila — to come along to a meeting at the San Giovanni
party section. Alone with her for a moment, Lila says: *bella stronzata,
quella di stamattina* ("nice piece of crap, that thing this morning").
He says he does what's necessary. Were your friends in on it? Armando
and Nadia, certainly, he says, but they stayed home. He seems hollowed
out, as if the violence sucked his usual restlessness dry. He hasn't
even invited her to the meeting — *cosa che non accadeva mai*. Maybe
they have *guerre di maschi* (manly wars) to fight. Maybe he's angry
that her reluctance makes him look bad in front of Nadia and Armando.
She's sure he thinks she hasn't understood why he clubbed Gino, why
he wanted to crack the guard's skull. *Buoni o cattivi, gli uomini
credono tutti che a ogni loro impresa devi metterli su un altare come
san Giorgio che ammazza il drago* — "good or bad, men all believe that
for every exploit you must put them on an altar like Saint George
slaying the dragon." He considers her ungrateful: he did it to avenge
her; he wants at least a *grazie*.

When the men go out, she goes to bed with the union pamphlets Pasquale
gave her months back — *opuscoli su lavoro e sindacato*. They anchor
her to grey daily things. She is afraid of the silence of the house,
of sleep, of her heart's bad behavior, of *le forme che minacciavano
a ogni momento di guastarsi* (forms that threatened, at every moment,
to come apart). She reads a lot despite the fatigue and — as always
— *si appassionò, imparò in fretta moltissime cose*. To feel safer she
tries to wait up for Enzo, but he doesn't come back; Gennaro's
breathing turns hypnotic; she sleeps.

The next morning, Edo and Teresa start circling her with shy friendly
words and gestures, and she doesn't push them away. She treats the
other workers kindly. She listens to whoever puffs and grouses,
nodding at whoever curses the petty injustices. She *channels* the
discomfort of one toward the discomfort of another and binds it all
together with the right words. She gives more rope to Edo, Teresa, and
their tiny *minuscolo partito*, turning the lunch break into a daily
*conciliabolo* (huddle).

She has the gift, when she wants it, of making people think they are
proposing what she is proposing. More and more workers come around,
relieved to hear their vague gripes named as *necessità giuste e
urgenti* — just and urgent needs. She bundles the de-boning room's
grievances with those of the cold cells and the cooking vats and finds,
herself surprised, that the troubles of each station depend on the
others; that together they are links of the same chain of exploitation.
She makes a careful list of the injuries the work produces: damage to
hands, bones, lungs. She gathers enough information to prove the whole
plant is in terrible shape — hygiene deplorable, materials sometimes
spoiled or of dubious provenance.

When she finally gets Pasquale alone and tells him what she has put
together in such a short time, he — usually scowling — is open-mouthed:
*ci avrei giurato che l'avresti fatto* ("I would have bet on it"). He
sets her up with a meeting with one **Capone**, secretary of the local
*Camera del lavoro* (labor exchange / union hall).

Lila recopies all her notes in her elegant hand and brings them to
Capone. He's thrilled too: where did you spring from, comrade, this is
fantastic work, *brava*. *Noi da Soccavo non siamo mai riusciti a
entrare*, he says — "we've never managed to get into Soccavo's; the
place is all fascists. But with you in there, things change."

— "How do we move next?"

— "Form a committee."

— "We already are one."

— "Good. First put this material *in order*."

— "Meaning?"

Capone looks at Pasquale. Pasquale says nothing.

— "You're asking too many things at once. Things that have never been
asked anywhere. You have to set priorities."

— "In there *everything* is a priority."

— "I know. But it's tactics: if you ask for everything you'll lose."

Lila narrows her eyes to slits. There's a brisk argument. Among other
things, it turns out a workers' committee can't go negotiate directly
with the boss — that goes through the union.

— "And I'm not the union?" she snaps.

— "Of course, but there are times and ways."

They squabble again. Capone tells her: see what you can do, open a
discussion — say, on shifts, on holidays, on overtime — and then we'll
build from there. *Le donne che si impegnano scarseggiano* — women who
get involved are in short supply. Then he reaches for his wallet:
want some money for expenses? What expenses? Ciclostile, paper, your
time. *No.* He puts the wallet back, says: don't disappear, Lina, stay
in touch, I want to talk to the union about you, *ti dobbiamo
utilizzare* — "we have to put you to use."

Lila walks out unhappy and tells Pasquale: who did you take me to?
But Pasquale soothes her: Capone is *un'ottima persona*, he's right —
*c'è la strategia e c'è la tattica*. Then he gets emotional, almost
moves to hug her, thinks better of it. *Va' avanti, Lina, fottiamocene
della burocrazia* — "press on, Lina, screw the bureaucracy. I'll
inform the committee."

Lila doesn't pare anything down. She trims her huge first draft to a
single, densely packed sheet and hands it to Edo: demands across work
organization, rhythms, plant conditions, product quality, the constant
risk of injury and illness, the miserable allowances, pay raises. Then
the question: who delivers this to Bruno?

— "You go," she tells Edo.

— "I lose my temper too easily."

— "Better."

— "I'm not the right one."

— "You're the right one."

— "No — *vacci tu*, you go. You're the union member. And you talk well,
you'll set him straight in no time."

## Summary

It's now clear to her: in his department Pietro is the boring Airota,
a far cry from his family's enthusiastic activism — *un Airota mal
riuscito*. She shares the opinion, which is no help at home or in
bed. Once Dede started sleeping, Pietro came back to their bed, but
his approaches make her flinch. She fears another pregnancy and
wants to sleep. She turns her back; if he keeps pressing his sex
against her nightgown she taps him on the leg with her heel: *non
voglio, ho sonno.* He retreats, gets up, goes to study.

A row about Clelia is really a row about everything. She tells him
flatly: she adores his intelligence and his manners, Dede is
wonderful, but she won't have more children, she can't stand the
isolation she's been pushed into, she didn't slog from childhood
just to be locked up as wife and mother. He capitulates. He buys
condoms. He starts inviting people over — acquaintances, since he
has no friends. He stops protesting when she takes Dede to
assemblies and demos, even with the streets ever bloodier.

The new regime makes things worse, not better. Dede attaches herself
even more to Clelia and cries to be taken back to her when out with
Elena, who suspects the toddler sees her as the dark woman who
scolds and mistreats *la tata solare*, sunny nanny. The condoms
dull Pietro further; he needs even more time, he suffers, she
suffers. She tries from behind to ease the pain, takes his hand and
places it between her legs hoping he'll caress her. He can't manage
both things at once — and prefers the first. When he's done he
strokes her hair and says *lavoro un po'*. She lies alone and the
solitude feels like a consolation prize.

At demos she watches the young men who fling themselves at every
risk *carichi di un'energia gioiosa* — full of joyful energy — and
finds them magnetic. But she feels alien from the colorful girls
around them: too cultured, glasses, married, never enough time. A
couple of times she daydreams that one of these young men, well-known
across Florence, drags her off the way Antonio or Pasquale used to
pull her to dance. It never happens. The complication, instead, comes
through the people Pietro now brings home. She cooks, she plays the
hostess. And she discovers that any man who throws her a little rope
is irresistible. Tall, short, fat, ugly, married, single — if he
praises one of her remarks, remembers her book kindly, gets excited
about her intelligence, she lights up, and her warmth gets back to
him fast. The bored guest turns lively and forgets Pietro is there.
A fingertip on her shoulder. A meaningful look. A knee against her
knee. A shoe against her shoe.

In those moments Pietro and Dede and the dreary obligations fall
away. She's only afraid of the moment the guest leaves and the
greyness of the house comes back: useless days, laziness, anger
hidden as mildness. So she overdoes it — laughs too loud, crosses
her legs deliberately, undoes a blouse button. She closes the
distance herself, as if part of her believes that pressing close to
this stranger will leave some of the high in her body for after he
leaves. Alone in bed later, while Pietro studies, she simply feels
stupid and despises herself. But she can't stop. The men go away
convinced they've made an impression and call the next day with
some excuse to see her. She agrees. As soon as she gets to the
meeting place she's frightened. The very fact that men twice her
age or married got turned on cancels their authority and the
*ruolo salvifico*, redemptive role, she had projected onto them.
The pleasure of the seduction game becomes a humiliating mistake.
She turns back toward Dede and Pietro. Then the next dinner comes.

A counterweight is Mariarosa, who passes through Florence often now,
sleeps over with rotating men and women, takes and offers drugs.
Pietro shuts himself in his study; Elena sits up arguing with her
into the small hours. She declines acid and hash herself, afraid
she'll feel sick. Mariarosa's language has changed — she swears
constantly, *cazzo* before every noun. Elena's careful, clean
Italian feels suddenly inadequate. Should she go back to being
Lila, then? — back to where she started? Then what was the whole
slog for? One evening Mariarosa breaks off a conversation to tell
the young man with her: *basta, andiamo a scopare.* Pietro had
invented a polite children's vocabulary for sex; Elena had picked it
up. But now, to be in the changing world, you have to put dirty
words back in circulation. Inconceivable with her husband. The few
cultured men she does meet, on the contrary, like dressing up as
*popolo basso* and enjoy treating a *signora* as a *troia*. The
formal preface, the gradual lowering, the unspoken becoming spoken:
in this game any female reserve is read as bourgeois hypocrisy. She
tries to keep up. The more she does, the more she falls under each
man's spell. In one or two cases she thinks she's in love.

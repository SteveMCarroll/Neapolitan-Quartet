## Summary

Nino stays for ten days. Nothing about that period, Elena insists, has
anything to do with the *smania di seduzione* — the seduction-fever —
she had felt in earlier encounters with him. She lists the things she
does not do, in a long inventory of restraint: she does not banter;
she does not put on a girlish voice; she does not pester him with
attentions; she does not play the part of the *donna liberata*
modelling herself on her sister-in-law; she tries no malicious
allusions; she does not look at him *intenerita* (softened); she does
not arrange to sit beside him at the table or on the sofa; she does
not go around the house half-dressed; she does not try to be alone
with him; she does not let her elbow touch his elbow, her arm or
breast his arm, her leg his leg. She is shy, composed, of few dry
words, attentive only to feeding him properly, keeping the girls from
bothering him, making him comfortable. And it is not even a choice:
she could not have behaved otherwise. He jokes a lot with Pietro,
Dede, Elsa, but the moment he addresses her he turns serious and
seems to weigh his words, as if there were no old friendship between
them. And, with him, she does the same. She is overjoyed to have him
in the house and yet she feels no urge for confidential tones or
gestures; she likes staying on the margin, avoiding contact. She is,
she says, *come una goccia di pioggia su una ragnatela* — like a
raindrop on a spider's web — and she takes care not to slip through.

They have only one long conversation, and it is entirely about the
manuscript. He raises it the moment he arrives, with precision. He
has been struck by the Ish-Isha'h passage. *Per te la donna, nel
racconto biblico, non è altro dall'uomo, è l'uomo stesso?* — he asks.
For you, the woman in the biblical account is not other than the
man, is the man himself? Yes, she says. Eve cannot, does not know, has
no material to be Eve outside Adam. Her good and her evil are good and
evil according to Adam. Eve is Adam in female form. And the divine
operation has succeeded so well that the woman herself, in herself,
does not know what she is: she has yielding features, she has no
language of her own, no spirit or logic of her own, she deforms like
nothing. *Condizione terribile*, Nino says — a terrible condition. She
spies him out of the corner of her eye to see if he is mocking her;
he is not. He praises her without a trace of irony, cites a few books
she doesn't know, repeats that the work is ready for publication. She
listens without showing satisfaction, says only: *il testo è piaciuto
anche a Mariarosa*. He immediately enquires about Mariarosa, speaks
well of her both as a scholar and for her devotion to Franco, and
heads off to the library.

After that he goes out every morning with Pietro and returns in the
evenings after him. Only rarely do they all go out together. Once Nino
insists on taking them to the cinema, to a comedy chosen for the
girls. He sits next to Pietro; she sits between her daughters. When
she catches herself laughing loudly each time he laughs, she stops
laughing altogether. She mildly scolds him in the interval for
insisting on buying ice cream for Dede and Elsa, and of course for
the two adults. *Per me no, grazie* — not for me, thanks. He jokes a
little, says the ice cream is good and she doesn't know what she's
missing, offers her a taste, she tastes it. Small things.

One afternoon they all four take a walk — she, Nino, Dede and Elsa.
They talk little; Nino mostly entertains the girls. But the walk
stays imprinted on her: she could trace every street, every place
they paused, every corner. It's hot. The city is crowded. He greets
people constantly, calls out by surname, introduces her to this one
and that with overblown compliments. His notoriety surprises her. A
very well known historian congratulates him on the *bambine* as if
they were *figlie nostre* — our daughters. Nothing else happens. Only
a sudden, inexplicable shifting of the relations between him and
Pietro.

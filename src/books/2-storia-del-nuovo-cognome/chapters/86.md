## Summary

The first obstacle to the secret affair is Nino himself. He doesn't care about gossip; he only wants to grab Lila, bite her, get inside her, and *non tollerava le separazioni* — every parting terrifies him that she'll vanish again. He drinks, smokes, stops studying. His one verbal mode is jealous reproach: *«Io ho lasciato tutto, e tu invece non vuoi lasciare niente»*. Lila wants him too, *ancora e ancora*, but she wants something else with it: she wants the old Ischia Nino back, the studious one who set her head moving. The prodigy of the elementary classroom, the child who wrote *La Fata blu*, is awake again and pushing him to become big enough to lift her out of *la signora Carracci*. Somehow it works. He goes back to his books, reads Smith and Joyce — unhappy with both — and she reads with him, hungry for his ideas.

The geometry of the day rearranges itself around the lie. In the salumeria Carmen watches Lila vanish on excuses, sees her sitting through busy rushes lost in a book or scribbling in her notebooks, and finally has to say *«Lina, per favore, mi aiuti?»* before Lila looks up. Stefano oscillates between picking fights with Rino, his father‑in‑law and the Solaras, and a wary truce at home — a more aggressive half of him wants a final showdown, but a more prudent (or *pavida*) half decides *meglio così che quando rompe il cazzo* and lets her drift. Lila feels the calculation and feeds it. Evenings she's polite over dinner, then withdraws into reading, into a space he can't enter, inhabited only by her and Nino.

What is Nino to her in this stretch? A permanent erotic daydream; a fever in the head that wants to match his; above all an abstract project of a secret couple — half hut for two hearts, half *laboratorio di idee* with him out front and her in his shadow. The rare hours alone become an unbroken flow of bodies and words, and the return to the salumeria and to Stefano's bed afterward is unbearable. *«Non ne posso più» — «Neanch'io» — «Che si fa?» — «Non lo so»*. Without a real plan she starts treating the world like a stage set: shift a flat, move a pawn, and the only game that matters can keep being played. In her notebooks the future shrinks to *il giorno dopo*; alongside it she copies cronaca‑nera murders of women and embellishes the wounds the papers leave out, as if naming the worst could shrink it.

## Summary

Inside that fatalistic game Lila finally plays the card Michele has been begging for: she agrees to run the shoe shop at piazza dei Martiri. She bargains for *assoluta autonomia* and a generous weekly wage — *quasi che non fosse la signora Carracci* — and shrugs off everyone who's hurt by it: Rino, who reads it as betrayal as the new Solara brand swallows the Cerullo one; Stefano, who rages and then uses her as a shuttle between the Solaras and his debts; Michele himself with his honeyed mediations and his demand for new shoe designs straight from her, around Rino and Stefano. She has long since understood that her father and brother will be wiped out and that Stefano will only stay afloat by tying himself to Solara money — and now she writes in her notebooks that this leaves her cold.

The shop has its own ghosts. The wall where her wedding photo once hung still carries the yellow‑black scorch of the fire that destroyed it. Standing there she realises piazza dei Martiri has been the stage of every turning point — the brawl with the via dei Mille kids, the disfigured photo, the news that her pregnancy was failing, the slow Solara takeover, and now, she decides, the end of her marriage. *Che sciatteria*, she says to Michele, pointing at the burn mark; outside she looks at the stone lions in the piazza and is afraid. She has the place repainted, opens a walled‑up door in the toilet onto an inner courtyard so a little light gets in, buys two paintings from a Chiatamone gallery, hires a Materdei girl who's trained as a secretary, and carves out the *uno‑quattro* siesta hours as her own. Michele backs every change but hovers; she keeps him on a leash.

The rione punishes her for going into the city. Pinuccia and Gigliola pour the predictable filth on her. The unexpected blow comes from Carmen, who adored Lila and now feels dumped at the salumeria under Maria Carracci's grip; she even picks fights with Enzo, who answers in his two‑word way that always seems to grant Lila a quiet privilege. Ada, lined up with Stefano, calls her *una troia* and shouts that all the men of the rione let themselves be played, including her brother and her *fidanzato* Pasquale; Pasquale defends Lila until he sees her sailing into piazza dei Martiri in Michele's Giulietta *vestita da puttana d'alto bordo* and gives up. Lila ignores all of it. The shop fills with lawyers' wives, *Il Mattino* journalists, idle *gagà* from the Circolo, drawn by her conversation more than the shoes; Michele, sniffing the air, tries to kiss her — *«Tu hai sbagliato marito, Lina. Io e te insieme in pochi anni ci prendiamo Napoli»* — and she throws him out of his own shop. From then on his visits thin and Nino's begin. For months he comes in through the courtyard door of the toilet at one, leaves at four sharp before the shop girl returns, and they have, finally, three hours of their own life every weekday. Nino publishes a piece on Naples in *Il Mattino* that earns him a reputation in university circles; Elena, reading it later, will recognise that the sharpest lines, and the very tone of the prose, are Lila's.

## Summary

Elena and Antonio kiss continuously — behind a tree, in a doorway, in
the unlit lanes — and ride two buses out to the *stagni*, the marshy
ponds along the railway. The path leads to a clump of dead trees and
then to the gutted hulk of the old cannery, all iron beams and torn
sheet metal. They have come here before. Tonight, though, Elena feels
something different working inside her — a strip of velvet pulled
taut from inside, an urgency for a pleasure violent enough to shatter
the whole day she has just lived through.

While Antonio whispers love-words in dialect, Elena imagines Lila and
Stefano already in their luxury hotel, sitting down to dinner,
preparing for the night. *Oh, to sleep pressed against a man, not to
be cold anymore.* All their previous secret encounters — fumbling
over a bra, lifting a skirt, the controlled ritual ending in his
handkerchief — feel suddenly insufficient. She wants more, and she
does not have the words to say so. She makes herself the one pushing.

When Antonio tries to undo his trousers in the usual way, she stops
him: *Aspetta.* She pulls him into the cannery itself, where it is
darker and more sheltered, but also full of the rustle and scrabble
of rats. Her heart pounds. The place frightens her; she frightens
herself. Her interior monologue narrows to a single brutal question:
*what am I, compared to Lila in her wedding gown? what am I, here in
the rusted scrap, with my skirt hiked up and my panties down,
struggling and anxious and guilty — while she gives herself naked,
languid and detached, between linen sheets in a hotel above the sea,
and lets Stefano enter her, legitimately, fearlessly, plant his seed
in her?*

She wants to lose her virginity right now, here, expressly so she can
tell Lila on her return: *I'm not a virgin either anymore — what you
do, I can do; you won't manage to leave me behind.* She raises herself
on tiptoe and physically seeks him out. Antonio realises what is
happening, helps her with his hand, and then — with visible effort —
stops. He pulls back with a long sigh and says, in dialect: *"No,
Lenù, io questa cosa la voglio fare come si fa con una moglie, non
così."* No, Lenù, I want to do this thing the way one does it with a
wife, not like this. He grabs her right hand, brings it to himself,
and she resigns herself to finishing him off by hand.

Walking out of the ponds afterward, he tells her uncomfortably that
he *respects* her and doesn't want her to do something she'd regret,
not in this place, not in this filthy hurry. He talks as if he had
been the one who pushed too far, and maybe he believes it.

She doesn't say a word the whole way back. When she knocks on the
door of her family's apartment her mother opens it and — held back,
uselessly, by Elena's siblings — slaps her hard across the face,
without a shout or even a reproach. The glasses fly off and hit the
floor.

Elena, with a sharp, almost gleeful fury and not a trace of dialect
in her voice, cries: *"Vedi che hai fatto? M'hai rotto gli occhiali e
ora per colpa tua non posso più studiare, non andrò più a scuola."*
See what you've done? You've broken my glasses, and now because of
you I can't study anymore, I won't go to school anymore. Her mother
freezes mid-strike, hand suspended in the air like an axe-blade.
Little **Elisa** picks the glasses up off the floor and says quietly,
*"Tieni, Lenù, non si sono rotti."* Here, Lenù, they're not broken.

## Summary

Just before the Christmas holidays of 1966 Elena gets a brutal flu. She rings a neighbour of her parents — *finalmente anche nel rione vecchio parecchi avevano il telefono* — to say she won't come home for the holidays, and then sinks into days of high fever and cough in an emptying *collegio*. She can't eat, can barely drink. One morning, half‑drowsing, she hears voices in dialect at full volume, like women at the rione yelling window to window, and out of the black bottom of her head comes the unmistakeable step of her mother. No knock; the door is flung open; *Immacolata* enters loaded with bags.

Her mother has barely ever left the rione, never been outside Naples, and yet she has put herself on a night train and come up to dump Christmas food prepared in advance — *cibi natalizi preparati con anticipo apposta per me* — and quarrel at the top of her voice, and order Elena, *come per magia*, back onto her feet and onto the train with her that evening, because she has the rest of the family to feed. The illness drains Elena less than the visit. The director might appear at any moment, given the shouting; the room is being rearranged without care. At one point Elena nearly faints and shuts her eyes hoping not to be followed into the dark. Her mother goes on, *servizievole e aggressiva*, reporting on her father, brothers, neighbours, friends and, of course, Carmen, Ada, Gigliola, Lila — and shaking her by an arm or a foot under the blankets each time she announces some new *brutta vicenda*.

Hungry and feverish, Elena finds she is more easily wounded than usual by everything in her mother she normally manages. She gets angry that every news item is rigged to show her *coetanee* doing badly compared to her. *«Finiscila»*. The hurt that goes deepest is sensing the *timore* under her mother's *fierezza di madre*: that any day Elena might slip and lose points and not be brag‑worthy anymore. Her mother feeds her by force, wipes her sweat, takes her temperature too many times. *Temeva che morissi privandola della mia esistenza‑trofeo?* — that she'd be demoted and sent home *senza gloria*? Her mother talks about Lila obsessively, and Elena realises with a jolt how much she has always rated Lila — even her own mother sees Lila as the better one and is suspicious of the present ordering. *Guarda com'è combattiva, guarda quanta presunzione ha negli occhi*. Elena thinks for the first time how much energy that bad leg has had to win her every day, how much *ferocia* it has demanded; her father — *un ometto debole, addestrato a essere servizievole e a tendere la mano con discrezione per intascare piccole mance* — could never have stormed into this austere building. Her mother could and did.

When her mother leaves and the silence comes back, Elena is partly relieved and partly, because of the fever, moved. She imagines her, alone, *con la sua gamba offesa*, asking each passer‑by the way to the *stazione ferroviaria*, refusing to spend five lire on a bus, getting the right ticket and the right trains, standing or perched on hard seats all night to Naples, walking the rest of the way to the rione, then back to polishing and cooking — *capitone, insalata di rinforzo, brodo di gallina, struffoli* — without rest, and telling herself in some corner of her brain *«Lenuccia è meglio di Gigliola, di Carmen, di Ada, di Lina, di tutte»*.

## Summary

Because Nino has not come back — not in ten minutes, not in an hour, not the next day — Lila turns *cattiva*. She doesn't feel abandoned; she feels humiliated. Twenty‑three days has been enough for him to confirm what she'd already half admitted to herself: she is not the right woman for him. In a fit she throws out everything he left — books, underwear, socks, a sweater, even the stub of a pencil — and then, regretting it, she cries. When the crying ends she sees herself ugly, swollen, stupid, *immeschinita dai sentimenti aspri* that he, of all people, has woken in her. The flat, which had felt like a refuge, shows itself for what it is: stained ceiling, cockroaches sliding under the stair‑door, the city's noises pouring through the walls. *Sentì per la prima volta l'infanzia che la riagguantava* — not the childhood of fantasies but the childhood of cruel deprivation and beatings. She discovers all at once that the old consolation — *diventare ricche* — has evaporated. Money, in her hands now, has stopped sparkling. Being rich means having Nino, and since he has gone she is poor in a way no cash can fix. She decides not to leave the flat, not to look for him, not to eat or drink, but to wait until she and the baby lose their outlines, *ogni possibile definizione*, and the worst thing of all — the awareness of being abandoned — drains from her head.

Then the bell rings. She thinks it's Nino; she opens it; it's Enzo. The sight of him doesn't disappoint her. She bursts out laughing, remembering the day long ago when, after she beat him in the schoolyard contest and he hit her with a stone, he came up to her with a wreath of grapes; today he must be bringing fruit again. Enzo takes the laugh for a sign of trouble. He steps in but leaves the door open — out of respect, so the neighbours don't think she's receiving men *come una puttana*. He takes in the flat and her dishevelled state, doesn't yet see the pregnancy, but understands she needs help, and announces, in his low even voice, before she can stop laughing: *«Adesso ce ne andiamo»*. — *«Dove?»* — *«Da tuo marito»*. — *«T'ha mandato lui?»* — *«No»*. — *«Chi ti manda?»* — *«Non mi manda nessuno»*. — *«Non vengo»*. — *«Allora resto qua con te»*. — *«Sempre?»* — *«Finché non ti convinci»*. And the work? *«M'ha stufato»*. And Carmen? *«Tu sei molto più importante… glielo dirò io, l'ho già deciso»*.

Lila answers with sardonic little laughs, as if neither of them were saying anything real, as if they were trading lines about a world that has stopped existing. Enzo sees it, falls quiet, walks through the flat, finds the suitcase, fills it with what's in the drawers and the wardrobe. Then he turns back to her and gives a startling little speech, his voice both concentrated and far away: *«Lina, io ti voglio bene da quando eravamo piccoli. Non te l'ho mai detto perché sei molto bella e molto intelligente, io invece sono basso, brutto e non valgo niente. Adesso tu torni da tuo marito. Non so perché l'hai lasciato e non lo voglio sapere. So solo che qui non puoi stare, non ti meriti di vivere nella monnezza. Ti accompagno fin sotto il portone e aspetto: se lui ti tratta male, vengo su e l'ammazzo… però facciamo un patto: nel caso che con tuo marito non trovi un accordo, io ti ho riportata da lui e io ti vengo a riprendere. Va bene?»*. Lila stops laughing, narrows her eyes, listens at last. There has always been something undefinable between them, something *nato nella confusione dell'infanzia*; *a Enzo, credo, lei si affidava*. He picks up the suitcase, walks toward the open door; she hesitates a second and follows.

## Summary

The facts disappoint Elena. She sits back down beside Alfonso and
Marisa, deaf to their chatter, waiting for the explosion she felt
coming. Nothing happens.

Half an hour later Stefano reappears, perfectly cordial, his face
clean, in a different suit. He drifts through the guests waiting for
his wife. When Lila comes back, she has changed out of her wedding
dress into a travelling outfit — a pastel-blue tailored suit with
very light buttons and a small blue hat. She does the bride's round
of *bomboniere* — silver spoonfuls of *confetti* for the children,
ceramic favours wrapped in white tulle for the adults — with an
absent stare and unusually sharp cheekbones. She gives them to
Stefano's family and her own; she pointedly ignores the entire Solara
family **and her brother Rino**, who tries with an anxious little
smile: *Non mi vuoi bene più?* — don't you love me anymore? She says
nothing and hands the favour to Pinuccia instead. When she reaches
Elena she passes the basket over distractedly, without even a
conspiratorial smile.

Stefano patches things up with the Solaras one by one, murmuring
"she's tired, you have to be patient," and even kisses Rino on both
cheeks. Rino, displeased, mutters to him: *"Non è stanchezza, Ste',
quella è nata storta e mi dispiace per te"* — it's not tiredness,
Ste', that one was born crooked, I'm sorry for you. Stefano answers
seriously, *"Le cose storte si raddrizzano"* — crooked things get
straightened. Then he runs after his wife, who is already at the door.

So there will be no rupture, Elena thinks. They will not run away
together. She pictures the elegant couple climbing into their
convertible, arriving at a luxury hotel on the Amalfi coast, the
day's bloody offence dissolving into a small sulk. And it dawns on
her with unexpected force that Lila has not merely *gotten married*:
she has crossed into something Elena had not understood, and the
distance between them is suddenly much larger than she realised. Lila,
by bending to the fact that her husband has cut some deal with
Marcello using her labour as collateral, has admitted that she cares
about Stefano more than about anyone or anything else — and that, for
all of it, she loves him *like girls in the photo-romance magazines*.
She will sacrifice every part of herself to him, and he won't even
notice. Elena, who reads books and loves no one — not even Nino — that
intensely, sees herself for a second as a small, dented bowl her
sister Elisa once fed a kitten from, eventually empty and dusty on
the landing.

She resolves, with a sharp wave of anxiety, to come *back* — back into
the rione like Carmela, Ada, Gigliola, like Lila herself. Stop showing
off. Stop humiliating the people who love her. She slips past her
mother to find Antonio on the terrace.

Antonio is dressed too lightly for the cold; he lights a cigarette
and pretends to look at the sea. "Let's go," she says. *"Vattene col
figlio di Sarratore"* — go off with Sarratore's son, he says. They
argue. He grips her wrist with steadily losing control and pours out,
in a strangled half-shout, everything he has been swallowing for
months: she had made him swear to stay close to her at the wedding;
he is in debt to Signora Solara for the suit he is wearing right now;
he hasn't been near his mother or siblings all day because *she* told
him not to; and her reward to him has been to spend the whole day
flirting with the poet's son. He warns her, his eyes huge and red:
*if I find out you keep seeing chillu càntaro* — that piece of
filth — *of Nino Sarratore at school or anywhere else, I'll kill you,
Lenù.*

Strangely, Elena finds the attack consoling. The pressure on her
wrist, the fear that he might hit her, the river of his pained
dialect — all of it tells her at least he cares enough. *"Mi stai
facendo male,"* she murmurs. He loosens his grip. *"Voglio stare con
te,"* she answers him, sulky. They leave the reception without
saying goodbye to anyone, walking arm in arm into the dark.

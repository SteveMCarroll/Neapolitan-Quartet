## Summary

After the stairs scene Lila keeps shuttling Dino over secretly until Pinuccia snaps: *«Tu sei una pazza… vuoi uccidere mio figlio, te lo vuoi mangiare come le streghe?»* and adds, like a stone tossed into the room, *«Quel povero cristo fa benissimo a metterti le corna»*. Lila doesn't react — only flicks a hand as if at a fly — picks up Gennaro and stops coming. Alone again, she finds herself genuinely worried for the first time: not by the betrayal but by its consequences. If Stefano is really keeping another woman and going to her every day, he could lose his head and throw mother and child out. For the first time the end of the marriage looks like a danger rather than a release — the flat, the means, the time to raise the baby well. She sleeps badly. Maybe his rages aren't just *sangue cattivo* but a man in love, the way she once was with Nino, chafing in his marriage. She organises her days around a clean flat, a hot dinner, a set table. Stefano comes home worse than ever, always *ingrugnato*. — *«Cosa c'è che non va?» — «Soldi»* — and lists Solara crimes: the new salumeria takes less, Michele acts as if the shoes were all his, old Cerullo models are being made on the cheap by suburban cobblers, new "Solara" designs are mostly slight tweaks of Lila's. Lila listens, but doesn't quite believe him; she senses he is inflating old griefs to disguise new ones. He accuses her of complicating things with Michele. *«Che gli hai fatto a quello stronzo, si può sapere?» — «Niente» — «A ogni discussione tira in ballo te ma sfotte me: parlagli, capisci che vuole»* — and finally, ugly: *«Se mi vuole chiavare che faccio, mi faccio chiavare?»*. He slaps her with the tips of his fingers and answers, *«Leggi, studi, ma sei volgare: non le sopporto quelle come te, mi fai schifo»*. He stops coming home, vanishes Sundays, won't talk about a summer rental for Gennaro — *«Ti prenderai il pullman e andrai a Torregaveta… Perché, per farti fare la troia dalla mattina alla sera?»*.

The truth surfaces by accident. Lila goes to piazza dei Martiri to ask Alfonso to track down a book and runs into a very handsome, well‑dressed young man, *uno dei ragazzi più belli che avesse mai visto, si chiamava Fabrizio*, a university teacher and a friend of Alfonso's. They talk literature, the history of Naples, child pedagogy — *una conversazione che le infiammava la testa* — and when Fabrizio leaves he kisses her on both cheeks with childlike *entusiasmo*, then Alfonso the same. From the threshold he calls *«È stata una bellissima chiacchierata»*. The pleasure curdles into melancholy: she looks around the shop and remembers Nino, the lowered shutter, the courtyard door at one, the four‑o'clock exits. *Non sentì nostalgia… solo che il tempo era passato, che ciò che era stato importante non lo era più*.

She is leaving with Gennaro when Michele Solara walks in. Effusive; calls the baby identical to her; insists on a coffee at the bar; then on driving them home. In the car he drops the irony for the first time in his life: *«Lascia tuo marito, subito, oggi stesso. Io mi prendo te e tuo figlio. Ho comprato una casa al Vomero, a piazza degli Artisti… A me interessa solo che ti posso guardare e stare a sentire»*. He even keeps glancing at her sideways, *in lieve ansia*. Lila stares at the road and tries to pull the dummy out of Gennaro's mouth. *«Hai finito?» — «Sì»* — *«E Gigliola?» — «Che c'entra Gigliola?»* — *«No, Michè, la risposta è no. Non ho voluto tuo fratello e non voglio nemmeno te. Né tu né lui mi piacete; pensate di poter fare tutto e prendervi tutto senza rispetto»*. Michele mutters about the dummy, then, darkly: *«Pensaci bene, Lina. Può essere che già domani ti penti e sei tu che vieni a supplicare me»*. And then, calmly, he tells her what everyone — her parents, her brother — knows but won't say *per quieto vivere*: Stefano has taken Ada as his lover, has done so since before Ischia; while Lila was on the island Ada went to her flat every night; the affair paused on her return, resumed, paused again when she vanished, and is now ongoing in a flat Stefano has rented on the Rettifilo. *«Mi credi?» — «Sì»*. What disturbs her is not the cheating itself but the absurdity of Stefano's behaviour when he came to Ischia to drag her home — the shouting, the beatings, the drive. *«Mi fai schifo tu, Stefano, e tutti quanti»*.

Capitolo 97 / 123

Foreign editions and Pietro&#39;s pistol student

Summary

Back in Florence Elena phones Adele to ask about the German translation Antonio handed her. Adele falls from the clouds; even she knew nothing, and calls the publisher. She rings back a little later with more news: the novel has come out not only in Germany but in France and Spain. Cosa devo fare? Elena asks — what am I supposed to do, travel, promote it abroad? Adele, kindly: nothing, just be glad. Then, more carefully: il libro disgraziatamente non s'è venduto da nessuna parte — sadly, the book hasn't sold anywhere.

Her mood blackens. She rings the press, demands a proper accounting, loses her temper, finally tells a sleepy clerk: I learned about the German edition not from you but from un mio amico semianalfabeta — a barely literate friend of mine. Then she apologises, feeling foolish. One by one the foreign editions arrive — French, Spanish, a clean German copy. Ugly covers: women in black, men with drooping moustaches and flat caps, washing strung up to dry. She flips through, shows them to Pietro, lines them up on a shelf among other novels. Carta muta, carta inutile — mute paper, useless paper.

A jangled, miserable stretch sets in. Every day she phones Elisa, who answers her anxious litany with cheerful laughter — life by car, life by aeroplane, the family's growing prosperity. At moments Elena envies her. She is tired, irritable. Elsa is sick constantly, Dede demands attention, Pietro dithers over his second book without finishing it. Elena flies into rages over nothing. Soon all three of them are afraid of her: the girls stop playing when she passes their door and watch her in alarm; Pietro begins to prefer the university library to home, leaving at dawn and coming back at night. When he returns he carries the marks of conflicts she only reads about in the papers — fascists stabbing and killing, compagni not far behind, the police newly authorised by law to fire and doing it even in Florence.

Then the bad incident she had been half-expecting. Pietro fails an exam given by a student with an important surname, very active in the campus struggle. The young man insults him in front of everyone and pulls a pistol on him. According to the version Elena hears from an acquaintance — not from Pietro himself — Pietro calmly finishes recording the fail, hands the libretto back to the student, and tells him: either you shoot for real, or you'd better get rid of that thing right now, because in a minute I am walking out of here to denounce you. The student holds him at gunpoint for long seconds, then pockets the pistol, takes his libretto and flees. A few minutes later Pietro is at the carabinieri. The student is arrested.

But it doesn't end there. The family approaches not Pietro but his father to make him withdraw the denunciation. The aged professor Guido Airota tries to persuade his son; Elena hears, with some astonishment, the old man losing his temper, raising his voice on the line. Pietro does not yield. Elena confronts him, agitatissima:

— Do you realise how you're behaving? — What should I do? — Lower the temperature. — I don't follow you. — You don't want to follow me. You are exactly like those unbearable professors at Pisa. — I don't think so. — You are. You've forgotten how we wore ourselves down trying to keep up with stupid courses and pass even stupider exams? — Mine is not a stupid course. — Ask your students. — You ask for an opinion from those qualified to give one. — Would you have asked me, if I had been a student of yours? — I have excellent relations with those who study. — You mean with the ones who fawn? — And you like the ones who play tough — like your friend from Naples? — Yes. — Then why were you always the most compliant?

She falters. Perché ero povera e mi sembrava un miracolo essere arrivata fin lì — "because I was poor and it seemed like a miracle to have got that far." Pietro: well, that boy has nothing in common with you. Anche tu non hai niente in comune con me, Elena throws back — neither do you. What do you mean? She slips sideways, doesn't answer.

Then it boils up again. Pointless to denounce him, she says, you'd already failed him. He says: he committed a crime. He was playing at scaring you, he is a boy. Pietro, cold: the pistol is a weapon, not a toy, and it was stolen seven years ago with other guns from a carabinieri barracks in Rovezzano. He didn't shoot, she screams. L'arma era carica, he answers — the weapon was loaded, what if he had? Non l'ha fatto, she shouts — he didn't. He shouts back: should I wait until he does? Non gridare, hai i nervi a pezzi. — "Don't shout, your nerves are shot." Mind your own nerves, he says. She cannot make him hear that beneath her sharp words she is genuinely terrified — for him, for the girls, for herself. Ho paura per te, she pleads — I'm afraid for you — but he doesn't comfort her, only shuts himself in his study and tries to work on his book.

Only weeks later does he mention, almost in passing, that two plain-clothes policemen came to him twice asking for information on certain students and showing photographs. The first time he sent them away with cool politeness without telling them anything. The second time he asked: Questi giovani hanno commesso reati? — have these young men committed crimes? "No, not yet." "Then what do you want from me?" And he showed them out with all the disdainful courtesy he had.

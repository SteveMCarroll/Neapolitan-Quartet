Capitolo 14 / 123

Milan — into the assembly at the Statale, and Franco again

Summary

The morning after the Turin lift incident, in the car back to Milan, Tarratano talks at length about what he considers the most exhilarating period of his life — 1945 to 1948. The melancholy is real, but it shifts into matching enthusiasm for the present: this new climate of revolution is his past coming round again. Elena nods and does the arithmetic — the man beside her is fifty‑eight. In Milan she gets out near the publisher and says goodbye, still trying to scrub off the feel of his body and the queasy sense that it lies somewhere on the same continuum as the rione's coarseness. At the office she's fêted — even the switchboard operator, who used to be cold to her, hugs her — and the editor who once nit‑picked her manuscript takes her out to lunch and starts nudging her about un nuovo romanzo — a new novel.

He reminds her she has a 3pm appointment at the Statale. Nothing to do with Mariarosa: the publisher set up a student session through its own channels. Who do I ask for? she says. Ci sarà mio figlio all'ingresso — my son will be at the gate. She grabs her bag at the hotel and heads to the university through brutal heat — densely posted walls, red flags, posters of peoples in struggle, a constant roar of voices and laughter and low‑level alarm. She wanders looking for any sign of herself. A dark boy bumps her at a run and tears off as if pursued though no one is behind him; a solitary trumpet cuts the dead air; a tiny blonde girl drags a chain with a padlock and shouts "sto arrivando" — I'm coming — to someone unseen. She pulls out her notebook to look composed. Half an hour passes; no one comes. There's no mention of her book on any wall.

She nearly bolts back to the hotel — what does she care about a handful of barely‑younger kids? — but follows a knot of women carrying bags and ends up at the door of a packed lecture hall just as a furious clamour goes up. Inside, a hard procedural fight is in progress, both in the body of the hall and around the lectern. She stays by the door, ready to leave. Looking around she takes in the gender mix and how the women hold themselves: some glued together in tight friend‑clusters — they leaflet together, laugh together, would leave together if any one of them left — others wedged in among the men with provocative ease, and these strike her as the happiest, the boldest. She feels alien — too over‑educated, too controlled, too close to marriage to belong. Via subito da questo posto — get out of this place now, she tells herself. Instead she slips deeper into the room.

What catches her is a strikingly beautiful young woman, delicate features, jet‑black hair, certainly younger than she is, standing among the most combative students with a baby of a few months in her arms. Behind her a thirtyish dark man smokes a cigar like a bodyguard. The girl is breastfeeding while she follows the argument, sometimes shouting; when the baby drops the nipple she doesn't tuck herself back in, just keeps standing there, blouse open, breast exposed, frowning, until she registers and reattaches him. To Elena she is an out‑of‑frame icon of motherhood — a teenage face, a baby, a refusal to play the placid madonna. The image looks fragile, painted on glass; the child might fall.

She is relieved when Mariarosa materialises beside the young mother, all colour and cordiality, and seems intimately familiar with her. Elena waves; Mariarosa doesn't see. Then a side door bursts open and a small group enters whose mere arrival calms the hall. Mariarosa takes the megaphone, speaks a few sentences that settle things, and hands it to a young man Elena recognises at once. It is Franco Mari, her boyfriend from the early Pisa years.

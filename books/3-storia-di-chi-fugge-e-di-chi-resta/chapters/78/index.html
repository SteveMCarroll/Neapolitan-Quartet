Capitolo 78 / 123

The slap

Summary

Elena's bond with Mariarosa gets stronger. She calls her often. As soon as Pietro notices, he starts speaking of his sister with growing contempt — she is frivolous, she is empty, she has always been dangerous to herself and to others, she was the great worry of his childhood and adolescence, la massima preoccupazione dei genitori — the chief preoccupation of their parents.

One evening he emerges from his study disheveled, hollow-faced, while Elena is on the phone with Mariarosa. He paces the kitchen, nibbles something, jokes with Dede, listens. Then snaps: è chiaro a quella cretina che è ora di cena? — is it clear to that idiot that it's dinnertime? Elena apologizes to Mariarosa, hangs up. The meal is ready, she tells him; no need to shout. He mutters that spending on long-distance to listen to his sister's nonsense is stupid. He sees she is furious and backpedals: I wasn't aiming at you, I meant Mariarosa.

But from that evening on he starts leafing through the books she is reading, mocking the lines she has underlined. Non farti abbindolare, sono sciocchezze. Don't let yourself be taken in, it's all silliness. He tries to show her the lurching logic of the feminist tracts.

One night, on exactly that subject, they fight. Maybe she goes too far. One sentence at a time she reaches the line: ti dai un sacco d'arie ma tutto quello che sei lo devi a tuo padre e a tua madre, proprio come Mariarosa — you put on a lot of airs but everything you are you owe to your father and mother, just like Mariarosa.

He reacts in a way she has not seen before. He slaps her — in presenza di Dede, in front of Dede.

She rides it out, better than he does. She has taken plenty of slaps in her life; Pietro has never given any, almost certainly never received any. She watches the revulsion settle on his face. He fixes for a second on his daughter, then walks out. She lets the anger drain, doesn't go to bed, waits up. He doesn't come back, and she begins to be afraid. She has no idea what he might do. He could have jumped into the Arno. He could be lying drunk somewhere. He could be on a train to Genoa, looking for sympathy and self-pity in his mother's arms. She realizes again how little she knows him.

She thinks: I have two daughters, I don't want to draw the conclusions too fast. She isn't going to let the lexicon of the pamphlets crash directly into her private life.

Pietro comes home around five in the morning. The relief at seeing him whole is so big that she hugs and kisses him. He mutters back: non mi ami, non mi hai mai amato. You don't love me, you've never loved me. And: comunque non ti merito. Either way I don't deserve you.

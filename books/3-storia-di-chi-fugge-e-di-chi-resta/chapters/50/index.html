Capitolo 50 / 123

Summary

From now on everything starts flowing for Elena, quasi che avessi l'arte di far correre gli eventi come acqua di sorgente — as if I had the art of making events run like spring water.

Pietro has worked for Lila too. His Pisan classicist colleague turned out to be more of a chatterbox than a wizard, but useful all the same: he knows a man in Bologna who genuinely understands computers — the reliable source of his philological fantasies — and that man gave him the number of someone in Naples he vouches for. Pietro dictates name, surname, address, phone of the Neapolitan contact. Elena makes a great fuss over him, teases him affectionately about his uncharacteristic initiative, even blows him a kiss down the line.

She goes straight to Lila. Lila has a tosse cavernosa — a deep, cavernous cough — her face is tight and pale, her gaze too watchful. But Elena is bringing good news and is excited. She shakes her, hugs her, holds both her hands, runs through everything — the call to Bruno, the article, the results of Pietro's, Adele's, Mariarosa's diligence. Lila listens as if from far away — un altro mondo dove mi ero spinta, another world Elena had pushed herself into — taking in maybe half clearly. Gennaro tugs at her constantly to play; she answers him senza calore, without warmth. Elena is happy anyway. Once, Lila opened the salumeria's miraculous till and bought Elena everything, especially books. Now Elena is opening her own drawers and returning the gift — hoping Lila will feel as safe as she now feels.

— Allora, domani mattina vieni dal cardiologo? So, tomorrow morning you'll come to the cardiologist?

Lila answers crookedly, with a little laugh: A Nadia non piacerà questo modo di affrontare le cose. E nemmeno a suo fratello. Nadia won't like this way of handling things. Nor will her brother.

— What way? I don't follow.

— Nothing.

— Lila, please, cosa c'entra Nadia, non darle più importanza di quanta già se ne dà. What does Nadia have to do with it, don't give her more importance than she already gives herself. And forget Armando, he's always been superficial.

Elena surprises herself with the verdicts — she barely knows the Galiani children. For a second she feels Lila isn't recognizing her, but seeing a spirit who's taking advantage of her weakness. Really, Elena was trying to make her see that the hierarchies of power are different now — that compared to the Airota, the Galiani are nothing, that people like Bruno Soccavo or quel guappo di Michele are even less, that she should just do as Elena says and stop worrying. But mid-speech she hears the brag in her own voice, strokes Lila's cheek, catches herself, says she admires the siblings' political commitment anyway, and throws in a laugh: però fidati di me, but trust me. Lila mutters: Va bene, andiamo dal cardiologo.

— And for Enzo, what time, what day for the appointment? — Quando vuoi, ma dopo le cinque. Whenever, but after five.

Back home she works the phone. The lawyer — she lays out Lila's situation in detail. The cardiologist — confirms the appointment. The computer expert (he works at the Ente Sviluppo) — tells her the Zurich pamphlets are worthless, but bring Enzo on such a day at such an address. l'Unità — the editor: are you taking your time? are you bringing this article or shall we wait until Christmas? Soccavo's secretary — please tell the boss that, since I've heard nothing back, an article of mine will be out shortly in l'Unità.

That last call gets an instant, violent response. Soccavo calls back in two minutes, no longer friendly: threats. Elena replies that any minute now he'll have the labor inspectorate on him and a lawyer working Lila's interests. Then, gradevolmente sovreccitata — pleasantly overexcited — proud of fighting for affection and conviction in spite of Pasquale and Franco, who still think they can teach her, she rushes to l'Unità with her article in the evening.

The editor is middle-aged, short, plump, with small lively eyes permanently lit by a benevolent irony. He sits her on a rickety chair and reads carefully. Then sets the pages down: E queste sono sessanta righe? A me sembrano centocinquanta. These are sixty lines? They look like a hundred and fifty to me. She blushes; she counted; sixty. Sì, ma scritte a mano e con una grafia che non si riesce a leggere nemmeno con la lente d'ingrandimento. Yes, but in a handwriting that even a magnifying glass wouldn't help. Però il pezzo è veramente ottimo, compagna. Trovati da qualche parte una macchina da scrivere e taglia quello che riesci a tagliare.

— Now? — E quando? When else? Una volta tanto che ho roba che se la metto in pagina si vede, mi vuoi far aspettare fino alle calende greche? For once I've got something that will show in print — and you want to make me wait till the Greek calends?

Key vocabulary

Italian English Note
acqua di sorgente spring water Elena's image of events flowing easily once she's set them in motion
tosse cavernosa a cavernous cough a deep, hollow, alarming cough — Lila's worsening symptom
senza calore without warmth how Lila now responds to Gennaro
cassetto miracoloso the miraculous till / drawer the salumeria cash drawer Lila once opened to buy Elena books
guappo small-time tough, neighborhood bully Elena's word for Michele — same register as Lila's
Ente Sviluppo Sviluppo (Development) Agency the southern-Italy development authority where the computer expert works
sovreccitata overexcited, keyed up her own word for her state as she rushes to l'Unità
compagna comrade the editor's address to her — the term of art in the PCI / l'Unità milieu
righe (printed) lines the unit the editor counts in — sixty was the assignment
lente d'ingrandimento magnifying glass even one wouldn't redeem her handwriting
calende greche the Greek calends i.e., never (the Greek calendar had no calends); a dry idiom for indefinite delay
ingrosso wholesale implied by the salami plant context — not in this chapter, but the editor will count Lila's plant among the fabbrichette of the southern outskirts
grafia (hand)writing, script what's too small to read

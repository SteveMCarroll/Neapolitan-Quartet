Capitolo 4 / 123

Walking to the restaurant — Lila in bed

Summary

They leave the bookshop. Adele and Tarratano walk ahead; Elena and Nino follow. At first she doesn't know what to say, afraid every word will land wrong, but Nino fills the silence. He praises her novel again, then pivots — at length — to the Airotas. He calls them «la più civile delle famiglie che contano qualcosa in Italia» ("the most civilised of the families that matter in Italy"); he says he knows Mariarosa ("she's always on the front line — we had a real argument two weeks ago"); he congratulates Elena on her engagement to Pietro, whose dry book on Bacchic rites he has already read; and he speaks with real reverence of the patriarch, Guido Airota, "a truly exceptional man." It chafes Elena that he already knew about her engagement, and that praise for her own book is so smoothly used as a runway for praise of Pietro's entire family.

She asks him about himself. He deflects — a forthcoming little book he calls "boring but obligatory" — and bats away her question about his first hard years in Milan with a generic line about coming from the South with no money. Then he asks abruptly: "Are you back living in Naples?" — "For now, yes." — "In the rione?" — "Yes." He, by contrast, has broken definitively with his father and no longer sees any of his family. "Too bad," she says. «Va bene così» — "It's fine like this," he answers; his only regret is that he has no news of Lina.

For a moment Elena fears he came to the bookshop only to find out about Lila. She replies brusquely, wanting to close the subject: Lila has left her husband and is living with another man. A boy. Nino makes a sour face and says: «Lina è coraggiosa, fin troppo. Ma non sa piegarsi alla realtà» — "Lila is brave, too brave. But she doesn't know how to bend to reality." She doesn't know what dedizione — devotion, self‑giving — is. Loving her, he says, was a hard experience. Pressed, he goes further: «è proprio fatta male: nella testa e in tutto, anche nel sesso» — "she's badly put together: in the head and in everything, in sex too."

Those last words — anche nel sesso, "in sex too" — hit Elena hardest. Back in the rione no man would ever have spoken about a woman he had loved that way; it was a thing said only between males, and crudely. That Nino now treats this as a normal subject to bring up with her unsettles her. Sensing he is about to say more, she retreats into bright small talk — what are you working on, do you live alone? — too obviously, and he smiles, ironic. They reach the restaurant.

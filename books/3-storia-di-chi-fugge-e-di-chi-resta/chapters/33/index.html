Capitolo 33 / 123

Smarginatura

Summary

The threats stack up: a theft charge, wage deductions, fines, mutual insults with Filippo. Bruno never reappears, though his car is still in the yard. Lila walks home knowing things will only get worse.

She snaps at Gennaro for wanting to stay at the neighbor's, makes dinner, tells Enzo to fend for himself with the canned goods, goes to bed early. She can't get warm even under the blankets — she pulls a wool jumper over her nightgown — and then, with no warning, her heart climbs into her throat and starts beating like somebody else's heart.

She has felt this before. It is the thing she will eventually christen, in 1980 eleven years from now, smarginatura — the dissolution of margins, the unsticking of the world's edges. But it has never hit this hard, and it has never hit her alone. There has always been a crowd, something external setting it off. Then she realizes with horror that she is not, in fact, alone. The whole day is pouring out of her unstuck head and floating around the room — the two committee boys, the guard, her coworkers, Bruno in the curing room, Nadia, the red flashes of the parziale, Filippo ripping the sausage out of her hands. All of it silent-movie fast.

Her heartbeats feel violent enough to dislodge the room's joinery. The walls' grip on each other slackens. Cracks open in the plaster. The shocks rattle her cot — they could crack the child, snap Gennaro open like a celluloid doll, split his chest, his belly, his head, expose his insides. Devo allontanarlo — I have to push him away. But push him too far and she'll lose him; keep him close and she'll break him. And then she thinks of the other child, the one who never formed in her belly, Stefano's child. L'ho cacciato via — I drove him out. Maybe on purpose. Why does nothing ever turn out right? Why should she hold on to anything that hasn't worked?

She slides her bare feet against Gennaro's cot to push it, gently, a small distance away. She edges into the kitchen, hugging the furniture, looking back over her shoulder for fear the floor will sink behind her and drag the boy down. She drinks from the tap, splashes her face — and the heart stops, hard, as if she'd braked. Done. The walls fit back together; the body dries and resettles.

She trembles. She is so tired the room turns. She thinks of crawling in behind Enzo for warmth — pressing against his sleeping back — and decides not to. The thought brings back her own face making la piccola smorfia graziosa — "the small charming grimace" — she'd pulled for Bruno when she told him fìdati, ho un figlio piccolo, questa cosa non l'ho fatta io ("trust me, I have a small son, I didn't do this"). A vezzo accattivante — a winning little trick — her female body acting on its own malgrado lo schifo, despite the disgust. She is ashamed of it. The thing she has always done with men — Stefano, Nino, the Solaras, maybe Enzo too — nudging them like obedient little animals toward purposes that aren't theirs. No, no, basta — no more. From now on she'll fight alone: the guard, her coworkers, the students, Soccavo, and her own head, full of demands that, ground down by the friction of people and things, stava cedendo — was beginning to give.

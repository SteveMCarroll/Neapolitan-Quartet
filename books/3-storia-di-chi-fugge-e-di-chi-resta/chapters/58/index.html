Storia di chi fugge e di chi resta · Tempo di mezzo

Capitolo 58 / 123

Tailleur, civil rite, and Pasquale in Galiani&#39;s flat

Summary

Elena resolves to step away. She has done what she could for Lila and now risks bogging herself down in fruitless thinking about who the real father of Gennaro is, about what Melina caught sight of, about the secret traffic of Lila's mind. She uses platitudes on herself: a woman always knows who fathered her children. Tu quel figlio hai sempre sentito che era di Nino, anzi l'hai voluto proprio per questo, e ora sei certa che è di Stefano solo perché l'ha detto Melina la pazza? Lila just laughs and says, almost cheerful, che stupida, come ho fatto a non capirlo — what an idiot I've been, how did I not see it. Elena gives up. If this new conviction helps Lila get well, fine; if it's just another instability, what can Elena do.

Enough. Her book has been sold in France, Spain, Germany; translations are under way. She has published two more pieces on factory women's work in the Campania, and l'Unità is pleased. The publishing house is asking for a new novel. She has a thousand things of her own to attend to; she has spent enough of herself on Lila. Encouraged by Adele, she buys in Milan a cream-coloured tailleur for the wedding — a fitted jacket, a short skirt. Trying it on in the mirror she thinks of Lila — of the lavish wedding dress, of the photograph the dressmaker hung in her window on the Rettifilo — and the comparison settles her: their worlds are distant now. She has already told Lila: no church wedding, no white gown, even Pietro reluctantly agreed to immediate family only. Lila had asked, without much interest, «Perché non vi sposate in chiesa?» — why not in church? Not believers. «E il dito di Dio, lo Spirito Santo?» — Lila quoting the little article they once wrote together as girls. I've grown up. At least throw a party, invite your friends. Pietro doesn't want one. «Non inviti neanche me?» — you won't even invite me? Would you come? She laughed and shook her head. No.

In early May, Elena tries one last initiative before leaving Naples for good. She calls Professor Galiani: she is getting married, moving to Florence, would like to come and say goodbye. Without surprise, without warmth but politely, the Galiani invites her for five the next day. And, before hanging up: porta anche quella tua amica, Lina, se ha voglia — bring your friend Lina too, if she likes.

Lila accepts without hesitation, leaves Gennaro with Enzo. Elena does her hair, her make-up, dresses in the Adele style; she helps Lila to look at least presentable, with difficulty. Lila wants to bring pastries — Elena says no. Elena does buy a copy of her own book, even though she's sure the Galiani has read it; she wants the chance to inscribe it.

They arrive on time. Two rings, silence. A third, and Nadia opens, flushed, dishevelled, missing her usual poise, as if their arrival has disordered both her clothes and her manners. Her mother isn't home, she says, and lets them in to the sitting room. She disappears.

After five minutes' silence with embarrassed half-smiles, they hear footsteps. In comes Pasquale, also somewhat rumpled. Lila shows no surprise; Elena exclaims, genuinely amazed, che ci fai qui? He answers seriously, without warmth: che ci fate voi qui. The frame flips — Elena finds herself explaining, as if to the master of the house, that she has an appointment with her old teacher. «Ah», he says, and to Lila, mocking: «Tu ti sei ripresa?» Lila: enough. «Mi fa piacere.» Elena, annoyed, answers for her — Lila is only just getting better, and at the Soccavo plant the lesson was well taught, the inspectors went in, the company paid Lila what was owed. Sì?, says Pasquale just as Nadia reappears, now perfectly composed as if about to go out. Capito, Nadia? La dottoressa Greco dice che ha dato una bella lezione a Soccavo. I didn't, Elena says. Non lei, la lezione alla Soccavo gliel'ha data il Padreterno — no, it was the Almighty.

Nadia, ignoring the free sofa, sits gracefully on Pasquale's lap. Pasquale puts an arm around her waist, leans toward Elena, and says: «Benissimo. Vuol dire che in tutte le fabbriche, in tutti i cantieri, in ogni angolo d'Italia e del mondo, appena il padrone fa bordello e gli operai rischiano, chiamiamo Elena Greco: lei telefona ai suoi amici, all'ispettorato del lavoro, ai santi che ha in Paradiso, e risolve.» — splendid: from now on, whenever a boss makes trouble and workers risk something, we'll call Elena Greco; she'll phone her friends and the labour inspectorate and the saints in heaven, and she'll fix it.

He has never spoken to her like this. Even when she was a child and he already gave himself political airs. She is hurt; she tries to answer. Nadia cuts her off and addresses Lila in her slow little voice as if Elena weren't worth speaking to. Labour inspectors don't count for anything, Lina. They went in, they wrote their papers, and what? Nothing has changed inside the plant. The people who exposed themselves are in trouble; those who kept quiet got a little money under the table; the police charged at us; the fascists came right up to the front door and beat Armando. Pasquale picks up, louder, an injured, sincere disappointment in his voice: «Spiegaci che cazzo hai creduto di risolvere. Lo sai che situazione c'è in Italia? Hai idea di che cos'è la lotta di classe?» Nadia softly: non si abbandonano i compagni.

Lila answers: it would have ended badly anyway. Cioè? Là dentro non si vince coi volantini e nemmeno facendo a mazzate coi fascisti — in there you don't win with leaflets or fistfights with fascists. How do you win, then? Lila falls silent. Pasquale, now to Lila, hissed: Si vince mobilitando gli amici buoni dei padroni? Si vince prendendo un po' di soldi e fottendosene degli altri? — by mobilising the bosses' nice friends? By taking a bit of cash and screwing the rest? Elena snaps: Pasquale, that's enough — that isn't how it went. She wants to explain, to put him down, but her head is empty; the only thing on the tip of her tongue is mean and politically useless: you talk to me like this because now you can finally put your hands on a signorina from a good family.

And then Lila, with an unexpected gesture of irritation, blocks her: «Basta, Lenù, hanno ragione loro» — enough, Lenù, they're right. Elena is winded. Ragione loro? She wants to answer her, get angry at her. But just then they hear the professor's steps in the corridor.

Capitolo 85 / 123

Viareggio with three children and the cane field

Summary

Pietro drives Elena and the three children to a brutta casa (ugly house) in Viareggio that they've rented, then goes back to Florence to try to finish his book. Elena settles into the role of signora agiata (well-off lady) with three children and many toys, an umbrella in the front row, five bikinis, menthol cigarettes, the sun darkening her skin and bleaching her hair. Every evening she phones Pietro and Lila. To Pietro she barely speaks; to Lila she passes gennaro-carracci, who recites the day's small events without much interest and says goodnight. Lila is reduced, more than ever, to a voice.

Except: part of Lila is in flesh and bone inside gennaro-carracci. He looks like Stefano, not like her, yet his gestures, his way of speaking, certain words, a particular aggression are Lila as a little girl. Elena startles sometimes to hear that voice in him, watches him in fascination as he gestures to explain a game to Dede.

But unlike his mother, gennaro-carracci is subdolo (sly, sneaky). Lila's childhood cruelty was always out in the open; no punishment ever made her hide it. gennaro-carracci plays the polite, even shy little boy, then the moment Elena turns away he torments Dede, hides her doll, hits her. The only threat that works is the threat of no ice cream — he doesn't care about being kept from the evening phone call. When he cries he says he wants to go back to Naples and Elena instantly gives in; he repays her by taking it out secretly on Dede.

Elena thinks the girl must fear and hate him. The opposite happens. Dede grows less and less reactive to his bullying and falls in love with him. She calls him Rino or Rinuccio — the name his friends use, he tells her — and trails after him, ignoring her mother's calls, in fact dragging him farther from the umbrella. Elena's whole day is a shouted refrain: Dede dove vai, gennaro-carracci vieni qua, Elsa che fai, non mettere la sabbia in bocca, gennaro-carracci finiscila. Useless. Elsa eats sand mathematically; mathematically, while Elena is rinsing Elsa's mouth out with seawater, Dede and gennaro-carracci vanish.

Where they go is a little cane field nearby. One afternoon Elena follows with Elsa and finds them naked, Dede curiously touching the cosino ritto (small erect thing) gennaro-carracci is showing her. Elena freezes a few metres off. Dede already masturbates often, face down; Elena has read about infantile sexuality, even bought her daughter a small illustrated book on what happens between male and female. But now what? Walk away? Approach casually and talk about something else? The boy is six years older — is the age gap not a danger? What decides her is two things at once: Elsa, spotting her sister, shrieks with joy and calls her; and Elena hears the parole in dialetto coming out of gennaro-carracci's mouth — heavy, vulgar courtyard words she herself learned as a child. Theories scatter. She scolds them hard, drags gennaro-carracci away by the arm. He bursts into tears. Dede looks at her, cold, fearless: sei molto cattiva — you're very mean.

She buys them both ice cream, but a new period begins: cautious watching plus worry about how the obscene Neapolitan vocabulary is seeping into Dede's speech. At night, with the children asleep, she pushes her memory: did she play those games in the courtyard? Did Lila? They never talked about it. They used disgusting words, yes, but as insults — including ones they shouted to push away the groping hands of leering adults. Anything else? She forces the question to the edge: io e lei ci siamo mai toccate? Did she ever want to, as a child, a girl, an adolescent, an adult? She stops short of an answer: non lo so, non lo voglio sapere. She admits a kind of admiration for Lila's body, yes — but rules out anything having ever happened. Troppa paura: if they'd been seen they would have been beaten to death.

For a few days she avoids putting gennaro-carracci on the phone, afraid he'll tell Lila. Then this irritates her — why should she be the worried one? — and she lets it all fade. The August days slip past in their rhythm of casa, spesa, borse stragonfie, spiaggia, gelato, telefono. She chats with the other mothers, all older than she is, pleased when they praise her children and her patience. She introduces Pietro: è professore di latino all'università. Weekends, exactly like Stefano and Rino on Ischia years ago, the husband descends; the other mothers shoot reverent looks at him and seem to admire even his unruly hair on the strength of his chair. He swims with the children, drags them into pretend-dangerous exploits, then sits under the umbrella to study, complains he isn't sleeping, forgets his tranquillisers. At night, in the kitchen, to avoid the squeaking bed, he takes her standing. Il matrimonio mi sembrava ormai un istituto che spogliava il coito di ogni umanità.

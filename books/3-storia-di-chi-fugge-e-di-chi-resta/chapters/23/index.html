Chapter 23 — Pietro&#39;s Sunday in Naples

Summary

Pietro stays three days. Vittorio and the brothers warm to him quickly — they like that he doesn't put on airs and that he treats them seriously even though school had written them off as hopeless. Immacolata stays cool. Only on the last day does she soften.

It's Sunday, and Vittorio wants to show his future son-in-law how beautiful Naples is. Pietro agrees and proposes lunch out. «Al ristorante?» — at a restaurant? — Immacolata asks, frowning. Yes, signora, we have something to celebrate. She tries to insist on cooking the gattò again; he refuses. Before they leave she pulls Elena aside: «Paga lui?» (he's paying?). Yes, ma'. Sure? Yes — he invited us.

They head into the centre dressed up. Vittorio takes over as tour guide: the Maschio Angioino, the Palazzo Reale, the statues of the kings, Castel dell'Ovo, via Caracciolo, the sea. Pietro listens attentively. Then, gently, he takes the thread from his host and begins to raccontarcela — to tell them the story of the city. Elena, who'd never paid much attention to the backdrop of her own childhood, is astonished. Pietro knows Naples's history, literature, fables, legends, anecdotes, monuments both visible and obscured by neglect. Maybe, she thinks, because he simply knows everything; maybe because he studied it deliberately, knowing it was hers, knowing her voice and gestures and body had all been shaped by it. Vittorio feels dethroned. The brothers get bored. Elena signals to Pietro to stop. He flushes and falls silent. But Immacolata, in one of her unpredictable swerves, hooks her arm into Pietro's and says: keep going, I like this, nobody's ever told me these things.

They eat at a restaurant in Santa Lucia recommended (sight unseen) by Vittorio. «Posso ordinare quello che voglio?» — can I order whatever I want? — Elisa whispers. Yes. The afternoon flies happily. Immacolata drinks too much and says a few off-colour things; Vittorio and the brothers tease again. Elena watches her future husband and feels certain she loves him: a man who knows his own worth but, when needed, si dimenticava di sé con naturalezza — forgets himself naturally. She notices for the first time his tendency to ascoltare, his comprehending tone come quello di un confessore laico — like a secular confessor's — and likes both.

She begins toying with the idea of keeping him an extra day and taking him to meet Lila — saying: I'm marrying this man, leaving Naples with him; what do you say, am I doing right? Then, at a nearby table, five or six students celebrating a pizza dinner start laughing in Pietro's direction. Elena reads the signs at once: they find his shaggy brows and high cespuglio (bushy crown) of hair funny. Within minutes Peppe and Gianni are at the students' table, attacking with the usual violence. Chairs scrape, shouts go up, a couple of fists land. Immacolata yells insults in support of her sons; Vittorio and Pietro pull them off. Pietro, oddly, looks half-amused — he hasn't really grasped why. Outside he asks ironically: is that a local custom — you stand up suddenly and go punch the people at the next table? He and the brothers come out of it more relaxed and matey than before. But as soon as he can, Vittorio takes Peppe and Gianni aside and tells them off for the disgrace they brought on the professor. Peppe almost whispers his defence: stavano sfottendo Pietro, papà, che cazzo dovevamo fare? — they were taking the piss out of Pietro, dad, what the fuck were we supposed to do? Elena is pleased he said "Pietro" and not "il professore": he means Pietro is already one of the family, a friend of great quality, and even if he looks a bit odd no one is going to mock him in their presence. But the same scene convinces Elena not to take Pietro to Lila. La conoscevo, era cattiva — she knew her, she was mean; Lila would have found him ridiculous and mocked him exactly the way the boys in the restaurant had.

In the evening, exhausted, they eat lightly at home, then all walk Pietro back to his hotel. At the door Immacolata, by now buzzing, plants two loud kisses on his cheeks. But on the way home, even while the family praises Pietro, she stays sulle sue senza fiatare — on her own, silent. Just before going to her room she turns on Elena, astiosa (bitter): «Tu sei troppo fortunata, non te lo meriti quel povero ragazzo» — you're too lucky; you don't deserve that poor boy.

Capitolo 60 / 123

On the street — and the wish she can&#39;t put down

Summary

Yes, it was time to pull out. Elena leaves the Galiani flat with a bitter mouth, never having found the courage to tell her old teacher that she had no right to treat her this way. Galiani never said a word about her book — though she had it, had surely at least leafed through it. She didn't ask Elena to inscribe the copy she'd brought, and when Elena, out of weakness, offered to do it anyway before going, the Galiani had answered neither yes nor no, had smiled, had kept talking to Lila. Worst of all, she had said nothing about Elena's l'Unità pieces except to lump them into her dismissal of the paper as bureaucratic; then she'd pulled out Lila's pages and started talking to Lila as if Elena's opinion on the subject counted for nothing, as if Elena had ceased to be in the room.

What Elena wanted to shout: yes, true, Lila has a great intelligence — an intelligence I have always recognised, that I love, that has shaped everything I've done. But I have cultivated mine, with great effort, and with success — they appreciate me everywhere — I'm not a pretentious cipher like your daughter. Instead she had sat quietly listening while the two of them talked about work and the factory and rivendicazioni. Out on the landing they'd kept going; then the Galiani saluted Elena distractedly and to Lila — with whom she had now switched to the tu form — said: fatti sentire — keep in touch — and hugged her.

Pasquale and Nadia hadn't reappeared, so Elena hadn't even been able to push back at them. She carries out a knot of leftover anger at them too. What sin is it to help a friend? She exposed herself to do it. How dare they criticise her actions?

Now, in the stairwell, then in the courtyard, then on the pavement of corso Vittorio Emanuele, it is only Elena and Lila. Elena is ready to start shouting: davvero pensi che mi vergogno di te? Cosa t'è venuto in mente? Perché hai dato ragione a quei due? Sei un'ingrata, ho fatto di tutto per starti vicina, e tu mi tratti così, hai davvero la testa malata. — Do you really think I'm ashamed of you? What got into you? Why did you side with those two? You're ungrateful; I've done everything to be there for you, and you treat me like this; your head really is sick.

But the moment they're outside, before she can even open her mouth (and anyway, what would have changed?), Lila slips her arm into hers and launches into a defence of Elena against the Galiani. She doesn't stop talking until the Piazza Amedeo metro. Che brutta gente — hai visto la vecchia come t'ha trattata? S'è voluta vendicare. Non può sopportare che scrivi libri e articoli, non può sopportare che stai per fare un buon matrimonio, soprattutto non può sopportare che Nadia, educata apposta per essere la meglio di tutte, non combina niente di buono, s'è messa col muratore e le fa la puttana sotto gli occhi. Sì, non può sopportarlo, ma tu fai male a dispiacerti, fottitene, non glielo dovevi lasciare il tuo libro, non dovevi chiedere se voleva la dedica, soprattutto non gliela dovevi fare. Questa è gente che bisogna trattare a calci in culo. Il tuo difetto è che sei troppo buona, abbocchi a tutto ciò che dicono quelli che hanno studiato come se la testa ce l'avessero solo loro, e invece non è così. Rilassati, va', sposati, fa' il viaggio di nozze, ti sei preoccupata troppo per me, scrivi un altro romanzo, lo sai che m'aspetto da te cose bellissime, ti voglio bene. — What ugly people; did you see how the old woman treated you? She wanted revenge. She can't bear that you write books and articles, that you're making a good marriage, above all that her Nadia — raised to be the best of all — has amounted to nothing and is openly playing the slut with a bricklayer. You were wrong to be hurt; don't give a damn, you shouldn't have left her your book, shouldn't have offered the inscription, certainly shouldn't have made one. These are people you treat with a kick. Your fault is that you're too good, you swallow everything from people who've studied as if they were the only ones with a brain. Relax, get married, have your honeymoon, you've worried about me too much, write another novel, you know I'm waiting for great things from you, I love you.

Elena listens, overwhelmed. There is no opening to throw any of it back — not the siding with Pasquale and Nadia, not the si vergogna di me in the study. Lila behaves as if it had been another Lila who said those things, a Lila she herself knows nothing about, of whom no explanation can be asked. Era falsa, ed era ingrata, e io, malgrado tutti i miei cambiamenti, seguitavo a esserle subalterna. She is false, ungrateful, and I, after all my changes, am still her subordinate. Of that subordination, Elena feels, she'll never free herself. And it seems unbearable. She wishes — and can't control the wish — that the cardiologist had been wrong; that Armando had been right; that Lila really was ill, and would die.

From that day on, for years, they don't see each other. They only speak by telephone. They become, for each other, fragments of voice — no verification by sight ever again. But the wish for Lila's death stays in a corner of Elena's mind. She drives it away and it doesn't go.

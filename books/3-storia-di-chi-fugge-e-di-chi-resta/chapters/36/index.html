Capitolo 36 / 123

Marco reads better than Gennaro

Summary

Galiani is taken by Lila's bluntness — sincere to the edge of rude — and by the precise, ironic Italian she speaks. She ushers Lila and Gennaro into the sitting room, where her three-and-a-half-year-old grandson Marco, blond, well-fed, is playing on the floor. Marco, saluta questo nostro nuovo amico. Lila pushes Gennaro forward to play. The two women keep talking. Lila mentions the old party, says she didn't know what anyone was talking about, half-laughs: ero molto ignorante, oggi lo sono più di allora — "I was very ignorant then; I'm more ignorant now." Galiani listens, and is hit — as everyone is hit — by what is inafferrabile and seduttivo in Lila, the siren thing.

The conversation only breaks when Gennaro slaps Marco, swears at him in dialect, and snatches a little green car out of his hand. Lila springs up, grabs her son's arm, slaps the offending hand hard several times, and over Galiani's faint lasci stare, sono bambini (let it be, they're children) forces him to return the toy. Marco cries. Gennaro doesn't shed a tear; he throws the car back at the other boy with contempt. Lila hits him again, hard, on the head.

Noi andiamo, she says. No, stay a bit. She sits back down. Non è sempre così.

— "He's a beautiful child. Right, Gennaro, that you're beautiful and good?"

— "He's not good. He's not good at all. But he's clever. Even though he's little, he can read and write all the letters, upper- and lowercase. Gennà, want to show the professoressa how you read?"

She takes a magazine off a fine glass coffee table, points at a random word on the cover, says: leggi, su. Gennaro refuses. She nudges his shoulder, repeats the order, menacing. Reluctantly he sounds it out: d-e-s-t… — and stops, glaring at Marco's car. Marco hugs the car to his chest, smiles to himself, and reads the rest in one easy sweep: destinazione.

Lila darkens. Legge bene. "Yes," says Galiani, "because I spend a lot of time with him. His parents are always out." How old is he? Tre anni e mezzo — three and a half. He looks older. Sì, è ben piantato. And how old is your son? Deve fare cinque anni, Lila admits unwillingly — about to turn five. Galiani strokes Gennaro's head, tells him kindly that his mother gave him a difficult word and he did well anyway, you can see he can read.

Then the noise of the front door, footsteps, voices male and female. Ecco i miei figli, says Galiani, and calls: Nadia. But it isn't Nadia who appears. It is a pale, fragile blonde with eyes so blue they look fake, who throws open her arms and cries: chi dà un bacio alla sua mamma? "Who's going to kiss his mama?" Marco bolts to her — she covers him in kisses. Then Armando, Galiani's elder son, Lila remembers him at once, almost wrenches Marco away: subito almeno trenta baci anche a papà — "at least thirty kisses for papà too, right now." Marco starts counting them off: uno, due, tre, quattro.

Galiani calls again, sharper: Nadia, sei sorda? — "Nadia, are you deaf? — come, you have a visitor."

Nadia finally appears in the doorway. And right behind her, looking sheepish: Pasquale.

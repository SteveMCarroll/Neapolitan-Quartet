Capitolo 39 / 123

Battle at the gate

Summary

She wakes at five drenched in sweat, fever broken. At the factory gate: no students this morning, only fascists. Same cars, same faces as before, gridavano slogan, handing out flyers. Lila guesses more violence is coming and tries to slip in head down, hands in pockets. But Gino blocks her path.

Sai ancora leggere? — "Can you still read?" — he sneers, holding out a flyer. She keeps her hands in her coat: Io sì, ma tu quando mai hai imparato? — "I can. When did you learn?" She tries to step past; he won't let her; he jams the flyer into her pocket so violently he scratches her hand with his nail. She crumples it calmly: non è buono nemmeno per pulirsi il culo ("not even good for wiping your ass"), and tosses it. Pick it up, he orders, grabbing her arm. And listen carefully: yesterday afternoon I asked quel cornuto di tuo marito (that cuckold husband of yours, Stefano) for permission to break your face, and he said yes.

Lila stares him down. E tu per spaccarmi la faccia sei andato a chiedere l'autorizzazione a mio marito? — "You went to my husband for permission to break my face?" Let go of my arm right now, strunz.

Edo, who would normally walk past, stops. Ti sta dando fastidio, Cerù? It is one instant. Gino throws a punch and Edo goes down. Lila's heart leaps; she picks up a stone and hits Gino square in the chest. As he shoves her against a lamppost and Edo struggles up, another car comes barreling down the dirt track, raising dust: Pasquale's rattletrap. Armando listened to her, she thinks bitterly, maybe Nadia too — they're polite people — but Pasquale couldn't help himself; he's come to make war. Five men climb out, Pasquale included, all from building sites, all carrying knotted clubs. They start beating the fascists with una ferocia metodica — methodical ferocity — one precise blow per man, just enough to drop him.

Lila sees Pasquale homing in on Gino, who is still a few steps from her, and grabs Gino's arm with both hands, laughing: forse è meglio che te ne vai, se no ti ammazzano — "maybe go now, before they kill you." He shoves her off and charges at Pasquale anyway. Lila pulls the bleeding, twisting Edo into the courtyard. He won't quiet down until he sees Pasquale club Gino flat.

The brawl peaks. Old junk flies through the air like missiles, spit and insults. Pasquale and another man — in just an undershirt over calce-stained blue trousers — sprint into the yard and start fracassando i vetri of Filippo's guard booth, screaming obscenities. Filippo cowers inside, terrified. Police sirens approach. Lila feels once more the piacere ansioso della violenza — the anxious pleasure of violence. Sì, she thinks: those who want to scare you have to be scared; there is no other way; mazzate contro mazzate (blows for blows); quello che togli a me me lo riprendo, quello che mi fai te lo restituisco — what you take from me I take back; what you do to me I'll do to you.

But as Pasquale's men pile into the car, as the fascists drag Gino off, as the sirens close in, Lila feels — with alarm — her heart turn into la molla troppo carica di un giocattolo, an over-wound toy spring. She knows she has to sit. Inside the gate she slides down with her back against the wall and tries to breathe.

Teresa, the heavy forty-something woman from the bone-stripping room, comes to help with Edo, mops blood off his face, teases Lila — first you almost tear his ear off and now you save him? You should have left him out there. Lui ha aiutato me e io lui, says Lila — he helped me, I helped him. Teresa, incredulous, asks Edo if it's true. He growls: non mi andava che la faccia gliela rompesse un estraneo, gliela voglio rompere io — "I didn't want a stranger breaking her face. I want to break it myself." Teresa laughs at how Filippo soiled himself; Edo grouses that they only smashed his booth, pity. Then Teresa, half-mockingly: li hai chiamati tu i comunisti? "Did you call the communists?"

Is she joking, Lila wonders, or is she a spy who'll run straight to the boss? No, she replies. But I know who called the fascists.

Who?

— Soccavo.

Capitolo 74 / 123

Adele&#39;s verdict, Elsa&#39;s birth

Summary

For five days Adele says nothing. On the sixth, at dinner — while Dede strains to feed herself so as not to disappoint Elena, and Immacolata is dying to help but holds back — Pietro asks, dry: "Have you finished your book?" Yes. "Then why did you give it to my mother and not to me?" Elena claims she didn't want to disturb him; there's a copy on her desk if he wants it. Has Adele told him she sent it? "Who else would have." Did she finish it? Yes. What does she think? "She'll tell you, that's between you two." He has taken offense.

Elena moves the typescript from her desk to his. The next morning — 30 July 1973 — she checks: it is still buried under his own books, plainly never opened. She snaps at Clelia, the Maremma-born help, for leaving everything to her mother; her mother, perversely, takes the harshness as a sign of love and strokes Elena's belly. Se è un'altra femmina come la chiami? — if it's another girl, what'll you call her? Distracted, leg aching, Elena answers without thinking: Elsa. Her mother darkens. She had been expecting: Dede porta il nome della madre di Pietro; questa, il tuo. Elena tries weakly to justify: Immacolata is impossible, she can't do that to a child. Her mother mutters and from then on won't speak to her.

At last, just before lunch, Adele calls. The usual ironic note is gone. Slowly, gravely, with elaborate qualifications, she tells Elena the book isn't good. When Elena pushes back she drops the soft phrasings and becomes blunt. The protagonist is unpleasant. There are no characters, only sketches. Situations and dialogue are stock. The prose strives for "modern" and is merely disorderly. All that hatred is unattractive. The ending is crude, western all'italiana — a spaghetti-western brawl. It does a disservice to her intelligence, her culture, her talent. The first novel, says Adele, was alive and new; this one is old in its content and so pretentious that le parole sembrano vuote — the words feel hollow. Elena mumbles that the publisher might be kinder. Adele stiffens: send it if you want, but I take it for granted they'll judge it unpublishable. Then, abruptly affectionate, she chats about Dede, the pregnancy, Mariarosa — and asks why Elena didn't give the book to Pietro. Ti avrebbe potuto consigliare. / "I doubt it." / "Do you not respect him at all?" / "No."

Shut in her room, Elena despairs — not because she has written a bad book but because the humiliation feels intolerable. She eats almost nothing, sleeps with the window closed despite the heat. At four in the afternoon the first contractions begin. She tells her mother nothing, takes the bag she has had ready for weeks, gets behind the wheel and drives herself to the clinic * sperando di morire per strada, io e il mio secondo figlio* — hoping to die on the way, she and the second child. It all goes well. Within a few hours she has another girl. The next morning Pietro begins fighting to name her after his mother-in-law — un omaggio necessario, he calls it. In a foul mood, Elena holds her ground: Elsa.

Back home from the clinic, the first call she makes is to Lila. She doesn't mention the birth. She asks if she can send the novel. Lila's breath goes thin: Lo leggo quando esce — I'll read it when it comes out. "I need your opinion now." "I haven't opened a book in a long time, Lenù, I don't know how to read anymore, I'm no good." "Please." "The first one you went ahead and published — why not this one?" "Because the first one didn't even feel like a book to me." "I can only tell you if I like it." "Fine, that's enough."

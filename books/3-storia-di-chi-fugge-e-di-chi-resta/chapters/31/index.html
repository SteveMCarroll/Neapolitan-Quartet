Storia di chi fugge e di chi resta · Tempo di mezzo

Capitolo 31 / 123

Lila at the committee meeting

Summary

Pasquale, undeterred by Lila's resistance, keeps dragging her into the political life he's built around Enzo. He brings pizza, drives Gennaro to Agerola for fresh air, pushes Lila to sign up with the CGIL union — which she does mostly per fare uno sfregio a Soccavo ("just to spite Soccavo"). He leaves pamphlets on pay slips and salary brackets where she'll find them. One demonstration on the Riviera di Chiaia, ostensibly for peace in Vietnam, turns into rocks and police charges, with Enzo cursing the moment they brought Gennaro along.

Two events stick. The first: Pasquale insists Lila come hear "an important comrade," a deputy from the Communist Party. Gennaro fusses, Lila barely hears the speech, but she registers the woman's authority and is irritated to see Pasquale and Enzo grow hostile to her line. When the deputy snaps that she'll get up and leave if they keep heckling, Lila approves — for half a second. Then Enzo shouts: «compagna, senza di noi tu nemmeno esisti» ("comrade, without us you don't even exist") — and Lila instantly flips sides, sharing the violence of that noi, that "us." She goes home furious with Gennaro for spoiling the evening.

The second event matters far more. Pasquale brings her and Enzo to a comitato — a committee of students and workers — meeting in a grand, crumbling building on via dei Tribunali. The room is small, the divide obvious: students with neat phrases pretending to learn dagli operai ("from the workers") while clearly already holding strong opinions on capital, exploitation, the betrayals of social democracy. The few girls flirt quietly with Enzo and especially Pasquale, who is treated as the prized real proletarian. Without warning, Pasquale introduces Lila by name as una compagna operaia — "a worker comrade" from a small food factory — and praises her.

Lila winces. Then a girl gets up to reply, parlando come un libro stampato ("speaking like a printed book"), citing her repeatedly as "compagna Cerullo." Lila recognizes her: Nadia Galiani, Nino's old sweetheart, the one who wrote him love letters on Ischia. Nadia gives no sign of remembering Lila in turn, which only sharpens the old humiliation of the corso Vittorio Emanuele party years ago.

Lila walks out into the corridor with Gennaro, energy churning. Then she comes back and asks to speak. In a voice pitched a little too high she tells the room she knows nothing about la classe operaia (the working class) in the abstract — only about the people in her own factory, from whom there is nothing to learn except miseria (wretchedness). Can you imagine, she asks, eight hours a day in waist- deep mortadella cooking water? Fingers cut to ribbons from boning animals? Walking in and out of cold rooms at twenty below for ten extra lire an hour — dieci lire — as your "cold allowance"? Women have to let foremen and coworkers paw their backsides without a word; if the padroncino (the young boss) wants her, a worker follows him into the camera di stagionatura (the curing room), where he gives her his practiced little speech about how the smell of cured meats excites him before he climbs on top. At the door there's a thing called the parziale (a partial-check light): red means the guard accuses you of stealing a salami, and the guard turns it red not just for thieves but for any pretty girl who won't put out or any troublemaker. The union has never set foot inside. The workers are povera gente sotto ricatto — poor people kept under threat.

When she finishes, the room is silent, then unanimous. Nadia comes to hug her — come sei bella, come sei brava, parli così bene ("how beautiful you are, how good, you speak so well") — and asks her to come back. Lila shakes her head: ho il bambino ("I have the child"), eight hours a day plus overtime, people in her situation only want to sleep. She leaves shaken, feeling she has exposed too much to people who understand abstractly but never concretely.

On the street, Pasquale takes her arm — a closeness he has never allowed himself before — and asks, stunned: tu veramente lavori in quelle condizioni? ("do you really work in those conditions?"). She shakes him off: and how do you two work? They drive home in tight silence. At the door Pasquale says only that she is sempre la più brava — "still the best, always" — and drives off. Enzo, holding the sleeping Gennaro, asks darkly why she has never told him: has someone at the factory laid a hand on her? Tired, wanting peace, she lies: con me non si permettono — "with me they don't dare."

Capitolo 76 / 123

Pulling back into the household

Summary

Elena admits — even now, ordering the memory — she cannot quite say what the sobs did to her. From a distance she sees in them a perverse gratification, as if Lila's tears, by confirming her affection and her faith in Elena, had canceled the negative verdict on both novels. Only much later does she see the other side: i singhiozzi le avevano permesso di distruggere senza appello il mio lavoro — the sobs had let Lila destroy her work without appeal, slip out of Elena's resentment, and set an impossibly high bar (non deluderla — don't disappoint her) that would paralyze every other attempt to write.

A few days later she phones Adele. Thanks for being so frank; you were right; she thinks her first book had many defects too; perhaps she needs more time. Adele heaps on compliments for her capacità di autocritica, reminds her she has a public. Elena locks the last copy of the typescript in a drawer, puts the notebooks of notes away, lets daily life absorb her. The disgust with the useless effort spills back even onto the first novel, even onto the very idea of literary writing: any image or suggestive phrase that comes to her now sets off a small wave of nausea and she moves on.

She gives herself to the house, to the children, to Pietro. She doesn't even think of recalling Clelia. She takes everything back on her shoulders to stupefy herself, and oddly it works — basta con i grilli per la testa — no more nonsense in the head. The chores are organized with an iron schedule. She tends to the babies with unexpected joy, as if besides the weight of the belly and the weight of the typescript she has shed a third hidden weight she cannot name. Elsa stays placid — long baths, long feeds, smiling even in sleep. Dede needs watching: she hates her sister, wakes claiming to have saved her from fire, from water, from a wolf, and regresses to babyhood, asking to nurse from Elena. Elena lavishes affection on her, praises her intelligence, recruits her as an indispensable helper.

Terrified of falling pregnant a third time, Elena starts taking the pill. She thickens, feels bloated, but doesn't dare stop. And her body matters less than it used to. The two daughters have settled it: she isn't young anymore. Letting her life be marked by their needs — washing them, dressing them, undressing them, the stroller, the shopping, the cooking, one in her arms, the other by the hand, both in her arms — feels not like a threat but the order of things. Va bene così — fine like this — diventare come le mamme del rione non fosse una minaccia ma l'ordine delle cose.

Pietro had resisted the pill for a long time, finally ceded. He studies her worriedly. Ti stai arrotondando. What are these spots on your skin? He fears illness — for her, for the children, for himself — but hates doctors. He is thinner, dark-circled, already gray at the temples; he complains by turns of knee, side, shoulder, and won't see anyone. Elena hauls him in. A clean bill, except a course of tranquilizers. He is euphoric for a few hours, every symptom gone — then the malaise returns. Around the time of the coup in Chile, when Dede won't let him watch the news, he spanks her with brutal force. He also wants sex more often, but only mornings and afternoons, claiming an evening orgasm wrecks his sleep.

Empty talk: night work has always been his rhythm. But she gives in anyway — no la sera, fine. She also discovers she can quiet him by shouting at him: faced with sharpness his stubbornness collapses and he obeys. She doesn't like doing it; it feels as if she is sending a painful tremor through his brain. And the obedience never lasts. He folds, takes a vow with a touch of solemnity, then is so exhausted he forgets it. She learns to stop minding a poorly washed plate.

She also learns to keep her opinions to herself. If he expounds on government measures around the oil crisis, or praises the avvicinamento del partito comunista alla democrazia cristiana — the rapprochement between the Communists and the Christian Democrats — he wants nothing back but agreement. If she pushes back he goes distant, or scolds her with his lecturer's tone: sei cresciuta male, non conosci il valore della democrazia — you grew up badly, you don't know the value of democracy, of the state, of laws, of mediation — ti piace l'apocalisse. She is his wife and supposed to listen with affection; opinions, especially if they plant doubt, are not welcome.

This thinned-out Pietro doesn't push her back toward other men. She runs into Mario, the engineer, sometimes, and discovers that the urge to seduce and be seduced is gone; she finds her earlier agitarmi a little ridiculous, meno male che era passata. She drifts away from public life too. When she does go to a debate or a march, she lugs the children along, proud of her overstuffed bags and the polite disapproval of strangers — * sono così piccole, può essere pericoloso*. She walks every day, in any weather, always with a book. She reads in any circumstance, even though the old ambition to build a world out of her reading has evaporated. Io ero questo, dovevo accettarlo — this is what I was, I had to accept it. Lila, qualsiasi piega prendesse la sua vita, era altro — Lila, whatever turn her life took, was something else.

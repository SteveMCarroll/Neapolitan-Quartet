Capitolo 8 / 123

Telling her parents — the civil wedding

Summary

Back in Naples, Elena tells her family the news: Pietro is coming to meet them; they will marry by the end of the year; she will move to Florence. There is no joy — her parents seem used to her coming and going, increasingly remote from the family's daily survival. Only her father is faintly anxious: "Does the university professor really have to come into our house?" Her mother snaps that he can hardly ask for Elena's hand without showing up.

Once everyone else is asleep, however, Immacolata corners Elena and hisses, low and furious, with red eyes: "For us you're nothing… the lady thinks she's somebody just because she studied, because she writes books, because she's marrying a professor — but my dear, you came from this belly and you're made of this stuff." She blames Elena's leaving for her brothers' failure at school, and then demands money — for a decent dress for Elisa, for fixing up the house since Elena is forcing them to receive a fiancé. Elena hands the money over without arguing. She suspects her mother is really fighting an older battle: that Elena keeps her earnings at the Poste instead of giving them up to her, the way she had done as a girl.

To soften the blow, Elena announces almost as a buonuscita, a parting gift, that she will have a telephone installed in the apartment and buy a television on instalments. Her mother stares, suddenly admiring against her will: a phone and a TV, here, in this house? — Yes. — Even after you're married? — Yes. Then she switches mood again: does the professor know there is no dowry, no money for a party? Yes, says Elena, and there will be no party.

That re‑ignites her mother. Elena pushes through with the rest of the truth: "Ma', not only is there no party — I'm not getting married in church either. We're getting married at the municipio, the town hall." All hell breaks loose. Though only mildly religious herself, Immacolata flies into a screaming rage, hobbling on her bad leg to wake her father and brothers and tell them what she has always feared: "too much studying has wrecked the girl's brain, now she lets herself be treated like a whore." She tries to throw Elena out of the house on the spot.

It is Elisa who breaks the spell. The little sister asks quietly: "Is it you who wants the registry office, or your fiancé?" Elena explains: for her, the Church doesn't matter; for Pietro, it matters enormously not to marry in church — he believes religion is honourable only when it stays out of state affairs. So either civil wedding or nothing. At this her father — Vittorio, a court usher — switches sides. He has seen too many church weddings end horribly; "the priest is no guarantee, nothing is a guarantee in this ugly world we live in." Her mother thinks of Lila and gives up screaming, though she keeps pacing and muttering insults at the future son‑in‑law: Comunista e professore? Professore di questo cazzo. Cosí pensa 'nu strunz — "Communist and professor? Professor of this so‑and‑so. Only an arsehole thinks that way." Vittorio defends Pietro patiently, vouches for the municipio — he works there himself — and finally talks her down. They argue for hours. By the end, Elena has made her parents accept a marriage that, for her, is above all a sign of her entry into the Airota world; she also savours, for once, being more daring than Lila.

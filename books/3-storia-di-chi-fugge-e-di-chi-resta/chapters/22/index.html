Capitolo 22 / 123

Chapter 22 — Pietro asks for Elena&#39;s hand

Summary

Elena picks Pietro up at the station. He's as rumpled as ever, face red with heat, lugging a heavy suitcase. He insists on buying a bouquet for her mother — and, against his usual habits, isn't happy until he's picked one abbastanza grande, abbastanza costoso (big enough, expensive enough). At the hotel he disappears, then reappears half an hour later in a blue suit, white shirt, blue tie, polished shoes. Elena bursts out laughing. He's perfect. But on the street she feels men's eyes on her, sniggers behind their backs, as though her escort doesn't merit respect: Pietro, with that gran fascio di fiori (huge bunch of flowers) he won't let her carry, is too proper for her city. She finds herself feeling she has to protect him.

Her father and brothers turn up vestiti a festa — in their Sunday best — almost too cordial. Immacolata is last to appear, the toilet flushing announcing her, hair set, a little colour on lips and cheeks. Once, Elena thinks, she had been a beautiful girl. The dinner is endless — days of cooking by her mother and Elisa. Pietro is suddenly expansive: he asks Elena's father about his town-hall job and draws him out until Vittorio drops his stiff Italian and rolls into dialect anecdotes about municipal clerks. Pietro, who cooks nothing, asks for every recipe and returns for a second large helping of gattò di patate (the Neapolitan potato bake), prompting Immacolata to promise to make it again before he leaves. The mood thaws.

After dinner Pietro turns grave and chiese a mio padre la mia mano — asked her father for her hand — using exactly that formula, his voice moved. Vittorio fumbles a flattered reply. The evening seems to be ending when Immacolata cuts in, nera — black: "Qua non siamo d'accordo che non vi sposate in chiesa." A wedding without a priest isn't a wedding. The agreement, plainly negotiated in private, has been left to her to deliver. Pietro answers her — and only her. He respects people who entrust themselves con sincerità a un dio (sincerely to a god), but he can't. Non essere credenti non significava non credere in niente — not being a believer doesn't mean believing in nothing. He loves Elena with absolute faith, and that, not an altar or a priest or a registrar, is what will hold their marriage together. Elena, he says, would love him less if he abandoned the principles he lives by — and no mother would want to hand her daughter to a man who could pull down even one pillar of his own life.

Immacolata sits unmoved. She fiddles with her wedding ring, then, instead of conceding or arguing, launches into a gelida determinazione (icy determination) catalogue of Elena's virtues. From childhood Elena was extraordinary; she was capable of things no girl in the rione had been capable of; she was the family's pride; she had never disappointed her; she had earned the right to be happy, and anyone who made her suffer would suffer a thousand times more. Elena listens uneasily, unable to tell whether her mother is sincere or signalling to Pietro that the Greco family is doing him the favour, not the reverse.

Pietro takes her seriously. When she stops he says he knows how precious Elena is and thanks her for shaping her. Then he pulls a blue astuccio (case) from his jacket and hands it shyly to Elena. Inside is a bellissimo anello d'oro rosso — a beautiful rose-gold ring — with an amethyst circled by brilliants. «Era di mia nonna», Pietro murmurs — it was his grandmother's. The whole family is glad it goes to Elena.

The gift seals the ritual. Drinking resumes, Vittorio tells more stories, Gianni asks Pietro which football team he supports, Peppe challenges him to arm-wrestle. In the kitchen Elena makes the mistake of asking her mother what she thought. «È brutto, ha i piedi storti» — he's ugly, his feet are crooked. Papà wasn't any better. "Don't you dare speak against your father." Elisa jumps in: Pietro is a real gentleman, ma' — you don't know what one looks like. Immacolata pretends to slap her; Elisa drags Elena, laughing, back to the dining room: «Beata te, Lenù» (lucky you), how fine Pietro is, how much he loves you — let me see the antique ring.

The men of the house now line up to arm-wrestle. Pietro takes off his jacket, rolls up his sleeves, sits at the table. He loses to Peppe, to Gianni, even to her father — but he takes the contest seriously, turning paonazzo (purple), a vein swelling on his forehead, protesting when his opponents bend the rules. He digs in stubbornly against weightlifters Peppe and Gianni, against Vittorio who can unscrew bolts with bare fingers, until Elena fears he'll let them break his arm rather than yield.

Capitolo 9 / 123

Reading the world — the Corriere review

Summary

The horror of a civil wedding doesn't fully die overnight, but it cools. Her mother now treats every object as if it were begging to be hurled at her daughter's face, but stops shouting. Elena ignores her, leaves early, sets the phone installation in motion, and then wanders into the bookshops at Port'Alba. She is determined never again to be left speechless the way she had been in Milan.

She buys magazines and books somewhat at random — among them, prompted by Nino's line fatta male anche nel sesso ("badly made even in sex"), Freud's Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality and a couple of slim volumes on sex. She tells herself she will study the contemporary world the way she once studied for exams — with the discipline she had brought to the newspapers Galiani had given her, or the Marxist texts Franco had pushed on her years ago. She admits, with rare honesty, that her drive isn't pure curiosity: it's the old need to do well. "You can be educated into anything," she has decided, "even into political passion."

Paying at the cash desk, she catches sight of her own novel on a shelf and looks immediately away — pride and terror in equal measure, a small jolt of pleasure followed by anguish. She remembers the book was written in twenty days, by accident, as a sedative against depression; she knows what real literature is, having lived inside the classics, and never thought as she wrote it that she was making something of value. And yet the effort of finding a form had pulled her in. She wonders for the first time, plainly: do I want to keep doing this — to write, seriously, better than I have?

She sits in piazza Cavour and tries to act like a real writer: she opens a fresh quadernetto (little notebook) and starts taking notes. She reads l'Unità from end to end. She finds her future father‑in‑law's piece in Il Ponte and is unimpressed: it uses the same rigid professor‑prose as the man with the thick glasses, and a phrase about "these new crowds of female students from modest backgrounds, in modest dress, who rightly demand from the immense labour of study a future beyond mere domestic ritual" feels, to her, like a coded reference to herself — the Airotas' clever showpiece.

Then she opens the Corriere della Sera and her breath stops. There is her own photograph, set into four dense columns of lead type, with the rione tunnel in the background. The headline reads: Memorie piccanti di una ragazza ambiziosa. Il romanzo d'esordio di Elena Greco — "Spicy Memoirs of an Ambitious Girl. The debut novel of Elena Greco." The byline is the man with the thick eyeglasses.

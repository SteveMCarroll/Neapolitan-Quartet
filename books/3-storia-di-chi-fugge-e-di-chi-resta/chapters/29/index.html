Chapter 29 — Edo&#39;s ear, Bruno Soccavo, and the seasoning room

Summary

In the factory, exhaustion makes everyone want to fottere (screw) not at home, where they arrive drained, but at work, morning or afternoon. The men reach out at every chance, make passes, sing ditties full of crude allusions. Older women laugh, rub against them with their large chests, fall in love — and the affair becomes a diversion that softens the fatigue and boredom, an impression of real life.

From day one the men close in on Lila come per annusarla — as if to sniff her. She pushes them off; they laugh or sing on their way. One morning, to settle the matter, she nearly tears the ear off a man who brushes past with a heavy line and stamps a kiss on her neck. He's named Edo — a good-looking belloccio in his forties, suave with all the women, a good teller of dirty jokes. Lila grabs his earlobe with one hand and crumples it, pulling with all her strength, fingernails sunk into the membrane, not letting go even as he howls and tries to parry her kicks. Then, furious, she goes to Bruno Soccavo to protest.

Since hiring her, Bruno has hardly seen Lila — passing glimpses. This time she gets to look at him properly. He stands up behind his desk — as gentlemen do for women entering a room — and Lila is surprised. His face is bloated, eyes veiled by excess, chest heavy, complexion flushed, come un magma against jet-black hair and white wolf-teeth. What, she thinks, does this have to do with the boy who studied law, the friend of Nino on Ischia? She feels there's il vuoto — empty space — between Ischia and this salumificio, and that across the gap Bruno — perhaps because his father is unwell and the weight of the business (and, some say, its debts) has dropped on his shoulders — has si era guastato (gone bad).

She lays out her case. He laughs. «Lina», he warns, "I did you a favour; don't make trouble for me. Everyone here is grinding away, people need to relax sometimes, or they make a fuss for me." "Relax with each other." A long, amused look. "I knew you liked to joke." "I like it when I'm the one who decides." Lila's mean tone makes him change tone himself. He says, looking away: you're still the same; how beautiful you were on Ischia. Then he points to the door: go back to work, go.

From that day on, every time he meets her in the factory, Bruno makes sure to greet her, in front of everyone, with a friendly compliment. That familiarity ends up sancire la condizione di Lila — sealing Lila's standing in the place: she's in the young Soccavo's good graces, so she should be left alone. Apparently confirmed when, one afternoon, a big woman called Teresa stops her and says, sneering: you're wanted alla stagionatura — at the seasoning room. Lila goes into the long rectangle where the sausages dry, stuffed cold cuts hanging from the ceiling in yellow light. Bruno is there, pretending to inspect, really wanting to talk.

He moves around the room, sniffing and squeezing like the expert, asking after Pinuccia, the cognata (sister-in-law). Then, without looking at her, examining a soppressata, he says: she was never happy with your brother; that summer she fell in love with me as you fell in love with Nino. He moves on and adds, his back turned: thanks to her I discovered that pregnant women love making love. Without giving Lila a moment to comment or ironise, he stops in the centre of the room and says he has always felt good in the drying room — something satisfying, full: il prodotto che arrivava a compimento (the product reaching completion), refining itself, ready for market. Look, touch — it's roba compatta, dura (solid, hard stuff), smell the scent: assomiglia all'odore di quando maschio e femmina si abbracciano e si toccano — it's like the smell of a man and woman holding each other. Like it? You don't know how many girls I've taken in here since I was a boy.

He grabs her waist, slides his lips down her long neck; he already has her culo (rear) in his grip; he seems to have a hundred hands, groping over and under her apron, frantic, panting — un'esplorazione senza piacere, una pura smania intrusiva (an exploration without pleasure, pure intrusive frenzy).

The smell, the move — it brings back Stefano's violences. For a few seconds Lila is annihilated, afraid he means to kill her. Then la afferrò la furia: rage. She punches him in the face and between the legs, shrieks: you're a piece-of-shit man, you've got nothing down there, come here, take it out, I'll rip it off, strunz (asshole in dialect).

Bruno backs away. Touches his bloodied lip, laughs in embarrassment, mutters apology — I thought there could at least be a little gratitude. You mean I have to pay up or you fire me, is that it? He laughs again, shakes his head: no, no, if you don't want to you don't want to, I've said sorry, what else should I do? But she is beyond herself, only now beginning to feel the trace of his hands, knowing it will last; it won't come off with soap. She backs to the door and warns: this time you got off lightly — but whether you fire me or not, I swear I'll make you curse the moment you touched me. She leaves while he murmurs: I haven't done anything to you, come here, facciamo pace (let's make peace).

She returns to her post — among the steam of the vats, an inserviente (attendant) whose job includes keeping a pavement dry that won't stay dry. Edo, of the ear, watches her. Everyone — operai e operaie — keeps their eyes on her as she marches back. She returns no one's look. She grabs a rag, slaps it on the tiles, drags it across the swamp of a floor, calling out, threatening: let's see if any other figlio di zoccola (son of a bitch) wants to try it on. Her workmates bend to their tasks.

For days she waits to be fired. It doesn't come. Whenever she crosses Bruno he smiles kindly; she returns a cold nod. No consequences — apart from the loathing of those mani corte (short hands) and stabs of hate. But the foremen, seeing Lila still doesn't give a damn, suddenly start tormenting her — shifting her jobs constantly, working her to the bone, throwing obscenities at her. A sign they have been given permission.

Lila tells Enzo nothing of the near-torn ear, of Bruno's assault, of the daily torments. If he asks how the salumificio is going she answers sarcastically: why don't you tell me how your place is going? When he stays silent she teases him a bit and then they get back to the Zurich course. They take refuge there for many reasons — chief among them, to avoid asking themselves about the future: what they are to each other, why he looks after her and Gennaro, why she lets him, why they have shared a house for so long and Enzo every night waits for her in vain, turns over, goes for a glass of water as a pretext, glances at the door with the half-frosted glass to see whether her light is still on. Mute tensions — busso, lo lascio entrare (I knock, I let him in) — doubts on both sides. In the end they prefer to stun themselves by sparring with diagrammi a blocchi (block diagrams) as if they were gym apparatus.

"Let's draw a diagram of opening a door," says Lila.

"Of tying a tie," says Enzo.

"Of tying Gennaro's shoelaces."

"Of making coffee with the napoletana."

From the simplest gestures to the most complicated, they wrack their brains to schematise daily life — even though the Zurich tests don't require it. And not because Enzo demands it, but because as usual Lila, having started slowly, has caught fire — and now, despite the icy flat, is gripped by the urge to reduce the whole miserable world they live in to the truth of 0 and 1. She seems to be reaching for an abstract linearity — the abstraction that begets all abstractions — hoping it will give her a lindore riposante (restful cleanness).

"Let's diagram the factory," she proposes one evening.

"Every single thing you do?"

"Yes."

He looks at her: "OK, let's start with yours."

She grimaces, mutters goodnight, and goes to her room.

Capitolo 108 / 123

Nino begins to mock Pietro

Summary

It starts at dinner. Pietro speaks admiringly of a professor in Naples, at that time fairly respected, and Nino says: ci avrei scommesso che quel coglione ti piaceva — I'd have bet that dickhead was the one you liked. Pietro looks disoriented and smiles uncertainly. Nino piles on, teasing him for letting himself be so easily taken in by appearances.

At breakfast the next morning, the second incident. Nino raises Elena's old schoolgirl row with the religion teacher about the Holy Spirit — Pietro doesn't know the story and wants the details, and Nino, addressing the girls rather than him, tells it as if it had been some great childhood feat of their mother's. Pietro praises her — sei stata molto coraggiosa, that was brave of you — and then, slipping into the tone he uses when the television says something stupid and he feels obliged to correct the record for Dede, explains what really happened to the twelve apostles on the morning of Pentecost: a sound come di vento, like wind; flames al modo del fuoco; the gift of being understood by anyone in any language. He launches into a little lecture for Elena and Nino on the virtus that pervaded the disciples, quoting the prophet Joel — io spanderò il mio spirito sopra ogni carne — and explaining that the Holy Spirit is an indispensable symbol for thinking about how multitudes find ways to confront each other and organise into communities. Nino lets him talk, with an ever more ironic expression. At the end he says: ci avrei scommesso che dietro di te si nascondeva il prete — I'd have bet there was a priest hidden behind you. And to Elena, in amusement: sei sua moglie o la sua perpetua? — are you his wife or his housekeeper? Pietro reddens, falters. He has always loved this sort of subject; Elena can feel him hurting. He mutters: forgive me, I'm wasting your time, let's go to work.

These moments multiply, without any visible cause. Between Elena and Nino the relations stay the same — formal, courteous, distant — ma tra lui e Pietro gli argini si ruppero, but between him and Pietro the banks of the river break. At breakfast and at dinner, the guest now addresses his host with an escalating series of mocking remarks, almost insulting in places, of the kind that humiliate you in the friendliest possible way — with a smile on the lips — and against which you cannot rebel without seeming oversensitive.

Elena knows those tones. The smart ones in the rione used to use them to bend the slow ones, to spin them speechless into the centre of the sfottò — the public mockery. Pietro is mostly disoriented: he likes Nino, he respects him, and so he doesn't react; he shakes his head as though pretending to be amused; sometimes he seems to be wondering what he did wrong and waits for things to return to the old, friendly tone. But Nino keeps going, implacabile. He turns to her, to the girls, doubles the dose, fishing for their assent. The girls assent, amused; even Elena assents a little. And all the while she is thinking: perché fa così? — why is he behaving like this? If Pietro takes offence, the relations between them will be ruined.

Only Pietro doesn't take offence — he simply doesn't understand — and his old neuroses begin to take him back. His face goes tired again, the wear of these last years reappears around his alert eyes and the lined forehead. Devo fare qualcosa, Elena tells herself — I must do something, and quickly. But she does nothing. What she struggles to push away is not admiration but eccitazione — excitement; yes, perhaps that is the word — at watching an Airota, a deeply learned Airota, lose ground, become confused, answer with limp little quips the rapid, brilliant, even cruel attacks of Nino Sarratore, her schoolmate, her friend, born like her in the rione.

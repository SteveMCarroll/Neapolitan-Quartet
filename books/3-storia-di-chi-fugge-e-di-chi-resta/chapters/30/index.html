Capitolo 30 / 123

Chapter 30 — Pasquale becomes a regular, and brings Nunzia

Summary

The fragile equilibrium between Lila and Enzo shifts when Pasquale reappears. He's working on a building site nearby and has come to San Giovanni a Teduccio for a meeting of the local PCI section. He and Enzo bump into each other in the street and old confidence revives at once — political talk, the same discontents. Enzo holds back at first; Pasquale, despite his serious rione job (sezione secretary), turns out to be anything but cautious. He attacks the party as revisionista and the union for closing both eyes too often. The two fraternise to the point that Lila finds Pasquale in her flat at dinnertime and has to scrape something together for him.

The evening starts badly. Lila feels watched, has to fight not to explode. What does he want — to spy, to take the story back to the rione? With what right does he sit there and judge her? He doesn't offer one friendly word, no news of her family, of Nunzia, of Rino, of Fernando. He gives her men's looks like the ones at the factory, sizing her up — and when she catches him at it he turns his eyes away. He must be finding her imbruttita, plain — must be thinking: how did I, as a boy, fall in love with her, what a fool. And no doubt he thinks her a bad mother — she could be raising her child in salumeria-Carracci comfort and instead has dragged him into this poverty. At some point Lila snorts, says to Enzo: clear up, I'm going to bed.

But Pasquale takes a deep breath and switches to ceremonial register. A bit moved, he says: Lina, prima che vai ti devo dire una cosa — before you go I have to tell you something. There's no woman like you. You throw yourself into life with a force that, if we all had it, the world would have been different long since. The ice broken, he tells her: Fernando is back to resoling shoes; Rino has become Stefano's cross and is constantly begging him for money; Nunzia has all but stopped leaving the house. But you did right, he insists: nobody in the rione has kicked the Carracci and the Solaras as hard in the teeth as you have, e io sto dalla tua parte — and I'm on your side.

After that night he comes around often, eating into the Zurich studying. He arrives at dinnertime with four hot pizzas, performs his usual all-knowing routine about how the capitalist (and anti-capitalist) world works, and the old friendship strengthens. He clearly lives without affection: his sister Carmen is engaged and has little time for him. He reacts with attivismo rabbioso (furious activism), which Lila finds appealing and curious. Broken by the work on the site, he still does union work, throws blood-red paint at the American consulate, leads when there's punching to be done with fascists, sits on a comitato operai-studenti where he is always fighting with the students. And the party — for his harsh positions he expects any day to be removed as section secretary. With Enzo and Lila he speaks unguardedly, mixing personal resentment with political argument.

Dicono a me che sono nemico del partito — they tell me I'm the party's enemy, he complains, that I cause too much chaos, that I have to stay calm. It's they who are destroying the party, turning it into a cog of the system, reducing antifascism to vigilanza democratica (democratic vigilance). And do you know who they've put in charge of the Movimento sociale section in our rione? Il figlio del farmacista, Gino, a slave to Michele Solara. And I'm supposed to put up with fascists raising their heads in my rione? My father — moved now — gave everything to the party, and what for? For this watered-down antifascism, for this shit today? When that poor man ended up in prison, innocente, innocentissimo — he hadn't killed don Achille — the party abandoned him, even though he'd been a great compagno, fought le Quattro giornate and at Ponte della Sanità, even though after the war he had stuck his neck out more than anyone in the rione. And my mother Giuseppina, did anyone stand by her? As soon as he names his mother, Pasquale picks Gennaro up onto his knees and asks: do you see how beautiful your mother is — do you love her?

Lila listens. Sometimes the thought rises that she should have said yes to this boy — the first who noticed her, before she set her sights on Stefano and his money, before she tangled herself with Nino. To stay in her place, not sin with pride, calmare la testa (quiet the mind). Other times Pasquale's invective drags her back into childhood, into the rione's ferocity, to don Achille, to his murder which she had recounted so often as a child, with so many invented details, that she half feels she'd been present. Then the arrest of Pasquale's father surfaces, and quanto gridava il falegname, the carpenter shouting, his wife shouting, Carmen shouting — and she doesn't like it, real memories mixing with false ones, seeing the violence, the blood. She shakes herself, ill at ease, slips out of the flow of his grievances; to calm herself she nudges him to recall, say, Christmas or Easter in his family, his mother Giuseppina's cooking. Pasquale notices, and must conclude Lila is missing family affection the way he is.

So, one day, he arrives without warning and announces cheerfully: guarda chi ti ho portato — look who I've brought. He has brought Nunzia — Lila's mother. Mother and daughter embrace; Nunzia weeps a long weeping, gives Gennaro a stuffed pinocchio. But the moment Nunzia tries to criticise her daughter's choices, Lila — who had at first shown pleasure at the sight of her — says: ma', either we act as if nothing has happened, or you'd better go. Nunzia takes offence and goes back to playing with the child, often saying, ostensibly to the boy: se tua mamma va a faticare, a te, povera creatura, a chi ti lascia? — if your mother goes out to work, who does she leave you with, poor little thing?

Pasquale realises he's done a wrong thing; says it's late, time to go. Nunzia stands, turns on her daughter, half threat, half plea. You, she complains — first you made us live like signori and then you ruined us. Your brother feels abandoned and won't see you. Your father has scancellata (struck out) your name. Lina, please — I won't tell you to make peace with your husband, that's not possible — at least straighten things out with the Solaras. Because of you they have taken everything. Rino, your father, we Cerullos — we're nobody again. Lila listens and then almost shoves her out the door, telling her: ma', it's better if you don't come back. She shouts the same at Pasquale.

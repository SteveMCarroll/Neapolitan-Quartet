Capitolo 100 / 123

Nino comes to lunch

Summary

Nino. The full beard from the bookshop years is gone but the hair is long and unkempt. Otherwise he is the boy of always — tall, very thin, bright-eyed, scruffy. He hugs Elena, kneels to fuss over the two girls, gets up apologising for the intrusion. She manages a few distant words — vieni, accomodati, come mai a Firenze? "come in, sit down, what are you doing in Florence?" She feels as if she has warm wine in her brain, can't give weight to what is happening: he — he — in her house. Something seems wrong in the organisation of inside and outside. What was I imagining and what is happening, which is the shadow and which the living body? Pietro explains: I ran into him at the faculty, I invited him to lunch. Elena smiles and says yes, all set, where there's food for four there's food for five, keep me company while I lay the table. She looks calm; she is agitatissima; her cheeks ache from the forced smile. Why is Nino here, e cos'è qui, cos'è è? — and what does here mean, what does is mean?

T'ho fatto una sorpresa, Pietro says a little anxiously, like a man who fears he has done the wrong thing — I made you a surprise. And Nino, laughing: I told him a hundred times to call you ahead, I swear, but he wouldn't. Then he explains: it was Elena's suocero — father-in-law — who told him to look them up. He had met Guido Airota in Rome at the congresso del partito socialista and one word led to another: he was going to Florence on business, the old man mentioned Pietro and his new book and a volume Pietro urgently needed. Nino had offered to deliver it himself. And here they were at lunch, the girls competing for his attention, he amusing with them both, courteous with Pietro, of few and very serious words with Elena.

"I've been to this city so often for work," he says, "but I had no idea you lived here, that you had two beautiful little girls. Meno male che c'è stata questa occasione — luckily this chance came up."

She fishes: still teaching at Milan? — knowing he doesn't. No, I'm in Naples now. Geografia. Geography. Cioè? — What does that mean? Geografia urbana. Why the return? Mia madre non sta bene. "My mother isn't well." Heart. Brothers fine. Father — il solito, the same as ever — but time passes, you grow up, lately they have got closer. Ha i suoi difetti e le sue qualità — he has his faults and his qualities. He turns to Pietro: quante storie abbiamo fatto contro i padri e contro la famiglia — how much fuss we made against fathers and family — e adesso che tocca a noi, come ce la caviamo? And now that it's our turn, how are we handling it?

"I'm fine," Pietro says, faintly ironic. "No doubt. You have married an extraordinary woman, and these two princesses are perfect, well-mannered, elegant. What a pretty little dress, Dede. And the fermaglio with little stars, Elsa — who gave you that?" "Mamma," says Elsa.

Slowly Elena calms. The seconds resume their orderly count and she takes stock: Nino is sitting at her table beside her, eating the pasta she made, cutting Elsa's cotoletta carefully into little pieces, attacking his own with appetite, mentioning with disgust the tangenti — bribes — Lockheed paid to Tanassi e Gui, praising her cooking, debating alternativa socialista with Pietro, peeling an apple in one long serpentine that sends Dede into ecstasy. A benign fluid spreads through the flat that she has not felt in a long time. How lovely that the two men should get along, should agree, should genuinely like each other.

She starts clearing in silence. Nino jumps up and offers to do the dishes — on condition the girls help. Mettiti seduta, he tells her, sit down — and engages the delighted Dede and Elsa, asking now and then where to put this or that, still chatting with Pietro.

He really is here, after all this time. Without meaning to, she watches the fede — the wedding ring — on his finger. He has not once mentioned his marriage, she thinks; he spoke of his mother, his father, but not of his wife and son. Perhaps not for love. Perhaps marriage of convenience. Perhaps forced into it. Then the flutter of hypotheses stops. Out of nowhere he turns to the girls and starts telling them about his son Albertino, in a voice now funny, now tender, as if the boy were the hero of a fable. Finally he dries his hands, pulls a photograph from his wallet, shows it first to Elsa, then to Dede, then to Pietro, who passes it to Elena. Albertino is very beautiful, two years old, in his mother's arms with a pout. Elena looks at the boy for a few seconds and moves quickly to study her. Splendid: big eyes, long black hair, just past twenty. She is smiling, i denti erano una chiostra scintillante senza irregolarità — her teeth a glittering even row — and her look seems to Elena innamorato, in love. Elena hands the photo back, says: I'll make the coffee. She stays alone in the kitchen while the four of them move into the living room.

Nino has a work appointment, profuses apologies, slips away after the coffee and a cigarette. Riparto domani, he says — I leave tomorrow — ma torno presto, la settimana prossima. Pietro repeatedly tells him to keep in touch; he promises he will. He takes warm leave of the girls, shakes Pietro's hand, gives Elena a cenno — a small nod — and is gone. The moment the door closes behind him Elena is overwhelmed by the greyness of the apartment. She waits for Pietro, who had got on so well with him, to find something hateful to say about the guest — he almost always does. Instead: finalmente una persona con cui vale la pena passare il tempo — "at last someone worth spending time with." The line, for some reason she cannot name, wounds her. She turns on the television and watches it with the girls for the rest of the afternoon.

Capitolo 98 / 125

Gennaro, Ulisse, and the maestra Oliviero

Summary

For the baby's sake Lila starts going out again. She wheels little Gennaro through the new rione in the heavy carrozzina monumentale Rino gave her at ruinous expense, dressed all in blue or white, alone. When he cries she walks to the salumeria and nurses him there, soothing her mother‑in‑law and amusing the customers and irritating Carmen, who works with her head down. She likes feeling him attaccato, likes the milk flowing out of her into him, svuotandole gradevolmente il seno. Her notebooks confess that the only relation that gives her benessere is this one, and that she dreads the day he stops feeding.

On warmer days she walks the carriage to the little gardens in front of the church. People stop, admire the baby, and she's pleased. At the old salumeria, where she goes to change him, the women fuss over Gennaro; Ada — apron too white, lipstick on thin lips, manner increasingly serva‑padrona — busies herself in a way that says the carriage and the child are in her way. Stefano, in private only distracted with the baby, in public — when customers coo and want to hold him — ostentava disinteresse, won't even look at him. Lila changes Gennaro in the back room and leaves quickly. At the gardens she studies the boy's face for traces of Nino and wonders whether Stefano sees what she can't.

One day she notices a funeral in the nearby church and goes; it is Enzo's mother Assunta. He stands stiff and very pale; she doesn't go up with condolences. Another day, on her bench, she's reading a fat green‑spined book — finger marking the page — when a thin old woman on a cane stops in front of her, cheeks already sucked back into the throat by her own breathing. «Indovina chi sono». Eventually Lila recognises gli occhi della donna and jumps up to embrace the maestra Oliviero, who pulls away annoyed. Lila proudly presents «Si chiama Gennaro» and waits for the usual praise, but the maestra ignores the child, fastens on the book. «Cos'è?» — «S'intitola Ulisse». «Parla dell'Odissea?» — «No, parla di quant'è terra terra la vita d'oggi… che abbiamo la testa piena di sciocchezze. Che siamo carne, sangue e ossa… che vogliamo solo mangiare, bere, fottere». The Oliviero rebukes her for the word as if they were back in class. «Non leggere libri che non puoi capire, ti fa male» — «Ci sono tante cose che fanno male». The duel rolls into its old grooves: «Eri destinata a cose grandi» — «Le ho fatte: mi sono sposata e ho avuto un figlio» — «Di questo sono capaci tutti» — «Io sono come tutti» — «Ti sbagli» — «No, vi sbagliate voi, e vi siete sempre sbagliata». The maestra studies her, and Lila reads on her face l'ansia dell'errore: the old woman is hunting for the intelligence she once saw, needing not to have been wrong. Lila decides she will give her nothing — non voglio che mi faccia la predica per come mi sono sprecata. But she cannot help being terrified, mid‑exam, that the verdict will be the wrong one: sta scoprendo che sono stupida, sta scoprendo che tutta la mia famiglia è stupida, che sarà stupido Gennaro. She mutters that she has to go, packs the book away, takes the carriage's handle and walks off. Vecchia pazza, she thinks — credeva ancora di poterla bacchettare. She leaves the maestra at the gardens, small, clinging to her cane, eaten by a disease she refuses to give in to.

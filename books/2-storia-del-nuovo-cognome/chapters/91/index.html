Capitolo 91 / 125

Twenty-three days, and Pasolini in the rain

Summary

The cohabitation lasts twenty‑three days. Hour by hour Lila's relief grows. She doesn't miss the comforts of Stefano's flat, doesn't grieve over her parents, her brothers, the baby nephew. She doesn't worry that the money will run out. What matters is that she wakes up with Nino and falls asleep with him, sits beside him while he studies, that they argue, joke, walk home pressed together through cold and rain after a film, a book launch, a political debate. One evening they go to hear a writer who also makes films, uno scrittore che si chiamava Pasolini. Nino doesn't want to: «È ricchione, e fa più bordello che altro» — but Lila drags him to the same circle where, long ago, Elena dragged her on Galiani's orders. She comes out fired up, but on the pavement opposite a group of fascists is shouting insults. Nino wants to slip away. Lila, raised on mazzate, won't flee — she trades insult for insult, laughing — and only goes quiet when she recognises Antonio among the attackers, eyes and teeth gleaming like metal, throwing punches without shouting. She lets Nino pull her into the alley and says nothing about it.

On the way home there's friction. They disagree about what Pasolini said — pareva che fossero andati in posti diversi a sentire persone diverse. More than that, Nino now lets slip that she's eccessiva in public: she shouts comments, picks fights if hushed, importuni i relatori attaccando bottone. Non si fa. Lila has always known that, but had believed that with him anything was possible, even closing distances in a single jump, even speaking a tu per tu with people who count — hadn't she handled the buyers at piazza dei Martiri, hadn't one of them placed his first piece in Il Mattino? She kisses him and tells him he's meglio di loro. He starts going out alone, evening after evening. When he stays home he complains about the noise of the building, grumbles about having to go ask Donato for money, gets irritable instead of dazzled when she yokes far‑apart ideas. He's so behind on exams that he stops coming to bed with her; «Va', poi vengo». He looks at the shape of her body under the blanket and thinks: non sono ancora laureato, non ho un lavoro… sto qui con questa persona che è sposata, che è incinta, che vomita tutte le mattine, che mi impedisce ogni disciplina.

When Il Mattino refuses his next article he is shaken. Lila consoles him, then says she'll phone the editor she met at piazza dei Martiri. He explodes. «Non telefoni a nessuno. Perché quello stronzo non è mai stato interessato a me ma a te». He calls her come una goccia d'acqua: teng teng teng. Finché non si fa a modo tuo, non la finisci; says he should never have listened to her, that the article only failed because she made him rewrite it four times. «Lina, scegliti una cosa tua che ti piace, torna a vendere scarpe, torna a vendere salame, ma non voler essere quello che non sei rovinando me». Erano ventitré giorni che vivevano insieme. She tells him to go. He pulls his jacket on over the sweater and slams the door. Lila sits on the bed: he'll be back in ten minutes — he left his books, his razor, the shaving soap. Then she cries, blaming herself: pur di liberarmi la testa, gli ho fatto scrivere qualcosa di sbagliato. She waits all night. He doesn't come back the next morning, or after.

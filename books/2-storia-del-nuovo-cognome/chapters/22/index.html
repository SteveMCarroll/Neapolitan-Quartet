Capitolo 22 / 125

The women of the rione

Summary

A short, essayistic chapter — almost no plot, just one of Elena's clearest set-pieces of social observation. Walking home from Lila's, she finds herself looking at the women on the stradone as if for the first time. She has spent her life looking at girls — Ada, Carmela, Gigliola, Marisa, Pinuccia, Lila, herself — and not really at the mothers. Now she sees the women of the old rione: Nunzia Cerullo, Maria Carracci, Giuseppina Peluso, Melina, her own limping mother. They are ten, at most twenty years older than her, and they have lost the connotati femminili — the womanly features — that her generation makes so much of. They have been eaten by the bodies of their husbands, fathers, brothers, whom they now physically resemble: same wide hips, same swollen ankles, same heavy chests, same exhausted faces.

She asks the chapter's central question: when does that transformation start? Housework? Pregnancies? The beatings? Will Lila someday be deformed like Nunzia — will Fernando's hard face break out of Lila's delicate one, the way don Achille's ogre came up through Stefano? Will she — Elena, with all her Latin and Greek and Hesiod and Anaximander — turn into the swollen, limping mother she fears most? She decides she has been intercepting Lila's feelings without realising it: it's Lila's body, suddenly being overrun by something foreign growing in it, that has put this question into her head.

She remembers Lila as a child staring at the schoolmistress fallen from her cattedra like a broken puppet; at Melina eating soap on the street; at the body of don Achille's murderer — Lila had been sure it was a woman, a female body breaking down out of pure need for hatred, revenge, or justice. That last image is the chapter's real engine: a body becoming unable to hold its shape any longer.

Capitolo 46 / 125

Enter Bruno Soccavo

Summary

Elena meets Nino's amico, Bruno Soccavo — short, twenty-ish, black curls, a face butterato dall'acne. The three walk her back to the Cuotto house along il mare vinaccia del crepuscolo. Nino doesn't take her hand again. Bruno keeps walking ahead or behind come per non disturbare. At the door Bruno blurts out: domani ci vediamo? and Nino asks for precise directions to their beach. Elena runs up the stairs felicissima. Inside, Pinuccia immediately sells her out: Mammà, Lenuccia s'è fidanzata col figlio del poeta. Nunzia takes it solemnly and lectures Elena on the long wait that comes with marrying a student. Lila chimes in cruelly: ti sta dicendo che farai la muffa. Nunzia rebukes her and points out that Lila si è sposata troppo giovane. Lila storms off to her room.

Monday: Lila is in a bad mood and takes it out on Elena. She makes Elena carry her bag to the beach, sends her back to the house twice for a foulard and forbicette per le unghie, and almost throws the money she's been giving Elena in her face — fu come quando uno fa cenno di volerti dare uno schiaffo e poi non te lo dà. In the water Lila relentlessly practices the strokes Sarratore taught her on the Maronti and overtakes Elena in a single morning. Around four, the vento fresco picks up and Nino and Bruno walk up the battigia — l'articolo il, Pinuccia jokes, naming Nino by his sentence-opening tic. Nino spreads his towel next to Elena's and gestures her onto a corner of the blue cotton. Mi sentii felice: he has come back. Bruno volunteers to walk Pinuccia to the cocco fresco stand so the baby doesn't get a voglia di cocco.

Once they're alone with Nino, Lila — who's been silent — quietly puts a question to him. Nino has been monologuing about Naples and Campania being in the hands of predoni, devastatori, dissanguatori, costruttori, camorristi, monarchico-fascisti, all of them bottegai who never pay taxes. Lila asks: che vuol dire bottegai? When Nino says "shopkeepers," she replies, evenly: mio marito è bottegaio. Then: le pagate le tasse? È la prima volta che ne sento parlare. She drags in Pasquale and his muratore work that depends on the same cemento Nino has just denounced, and the family money that viene pari pari from her father-in- law's strozzinaggio. She wants, she says without irony, to be helped to understand. Nino, rattled, retreats to anecdotes about him and Bruno eating only uova fritte e salumi. On the way home that night Lila does something nobody expects: she asks Elena to lend her a book. Elena, baffled — Lila hasn't opened a book in three or four years — hands over the Beckett volume she'd been using to uccidere le zanzare.

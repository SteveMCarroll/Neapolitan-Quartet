Capitolo 41 / 125

Arrival on Ischia

Summary

Second Sunday in July: Stefano, Lila, Rino, Pinuccia, Nunzia, and Elena disembark at Ischia. The two husbands stagger under luggage, sulky at having lost a Sunday and a car. The house — found in haste through a Neapolitan ham supplier — is a poor building on the road to a place called Cuotto, owned by a thin spinster landlady. They climb a narrow stairway; the rooms are dim, jammed with sacred images and oil lamps. Pinuccia and Lila bicker over the two bedrooms with sea views and divvy them up. Nunzia's room has an oddly placed porthole; Elena's tiny room overlooks a hen yard and a stand of reeds.

After a meal at a dark trattoria (which Rino, finally, pays for) the women want to walk down to the beach. The men refuse, yawn, and as soon as they're back in the house — con qualche battuta allusiva — they pull their wives off to the bedrooms. Elena and Nunzia spend two hours in the kitchen washing every plate, glass and pot. Nunzia is amazed that Elena can remember a long list of missing things to ask the landlady for: perciò sei così brava a scuola. The two couples reappear; the men nearly miss the ferry and run off swearing.

Only when the women are sure the men have boarded do they breathe out. A whole week to themselves. Pinuccia is scared by an icon of the Virgin in her room (multiple swords driven into the heart) and decamps to sleep in Lila's bed. Elena shuts herself in the cubicle to savour her secret — Nino is at Forio, very close, and tomorrow maybe I will see him on the beach. She lies awake listening to chickens and wind in the reeds, then opens the window, lets in mosquitoes, then closes it and spends an hour hunting and crushing them with the book Galiani had lent her — Beckett's Tutto il teatro. She doesn't want Nino to find her covered in welts, or to catch her reading "theatre"; she switches to a complicated book on the idea of nation. Falls asleep over it.

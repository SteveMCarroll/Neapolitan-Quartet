Capitolo 107 / 125

Pietro Airota, and a Sunday with his family

Summary

It is also a lonely year for another reason. Everyone at piazza dei Cavalieri knows she spent nights in Franco's room, that she went alone with him to Paris and to Versilia, and she has a fama di ragazza facile. She can't tell anyone how dearly the adjustment to libertà sessuale had cost her — io stessa me lo nascondevo — nor preach Franco's gospel that mezze vergini were the worst kind of woman, nor explain that the worst of Pisa is nothing next to what Lila has lived. She has to take the snide remarks of girls, the leers at her seno grande, the modi spicci of new suitors who hope to step into Franco's place, and the vulgar words that come back when she says no. One afternoon, leaving a café in via San Frediano with two friends, a rejected suitor shouts after her «Napoli, ricordati di riportarmi il pullover blu che ho dimenticato da te». A young man she's noticed in lectures — occhialuto, timidissimo, una matassa ingarbugliata di capelli nerissimi, i piedi storti — follows her to the collegio and finally calls «Greco». He introduces himself: Pietro Airota. He is ashamed of his classmates and ashamed of himself for not stepping in to defend her buon nome. «Non ho un buon nome», she answers, and he stumbles off in half‑apology, half‑farewell.

The next day she seeks him out. He's writing his dissertation in Latin literature too; he doesn't say tesi, he says lavoro; he lets slip libro, the book he intends to publish subito dopo la laurea. At twenty‑two he carries himself like a man already on a chair somewhere, citing scholarship without strain. «Davvero pubblicherai la tesi?» — «Se è buona sì» — «Si pubblicano tutte le tesi che vengono bene?» — «Perché no». He studies i riti bacchici; she, il quarto libro dell'Eneide. They talk only about Latin and Greek literature, not about Fellini and Antonioni or American nuclear weapons in West Germany. His memory is prodigiosa: he braids far‑apart texts and recites them as if they lay open on the desk, senza saccenteria, senza presunzione. She sees that he is bravo come non sarei mai stata: where she is cautious for fear of howlers, he has a tranquilla disposizione al pensiero ponderato. After three or four walks together the world rearranges itself around her again. «Che gli fai ai maschi? Hai conquistato anche il figlio di Airota». She doesn't yet know who Airota the elder is; she asks around and learns he teaches Greek literature in Genoa and is una figura di spicco del partito socialista. She talks less and less about her dissertation now, for fear of saying something silly in front of his.

One Sunday Pietro brings her, breathless, to meet his parents and sister, who have come to Pisa. She panics, dresses carefully, fears wrong subjunctives, la faccia da baccalà, an autista, a vast car. When they arrive the panic eases. Professor Airota is a medium‑height balding man in a rumpled grey suit, with a tired wide face and large glasses; Adele, his wife, is magra, non bella ma fine, elegante senza sfoggio; the car is an old Millecento like the Solaras' first one, driven up not by a chauffeur but by Pietro's sister Mariarosa, twenty‑four, graziosa, occhi intelligenti, who hugs and kisses Elena like an old friend. Mariarosa lectures in Art History at Milan and works on Piero della Francesca. She and her father spar across the lunch table over the centre‑left's interclassismo vs mediazione, the Patto Atlantico, the Vietnam bombings, the campus revolts in America and the anti‑imperialist fires across Latin America and Africa — fast exchanges full of warmth and irony, un'abitudine conviviale di vecchia data. Elena recognises what she has never had and now knows she will always lack — l'addestramento a sentire intimamente mie le questioni del mondu, a way of having ideas that aren't just chips to barter for grades or stand against humiliation. She finally cracks: «Gli americani, dopo Hiroshima e Nagasaki, andavano processati per crimini contro l'umanità». Silence; then Mariarosa shouts brava and shakes her hand. Heart pounding, Elena pours out fragments — pianificazione, neocapitalismo, precipizio socialdemocristiano, Piaget, marciume fascista in ogni articolazione dello stato — confused and breathless. The mood is friendly; fui felice di essermi esposta. She is also relieved that no one in the bella famigliola asks where she's from or what her father does. Ero io, io, io. They stay together through the afternoon, walk into the evening; on every street Professor Airota greets people who know him, two university colleagues stop with their wives to greet him warmly.

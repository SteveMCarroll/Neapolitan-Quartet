Storia del nuovo cognome · Giovinezza

Capitolo 125 / 125

The book in spring; Nino in the bookshop

Summary

She had told Lila nothing about what the novel was about or when it would come out, nor about Pietro or the plan to marry in un paio d'anni. Lila's life mi aveva sopraffatta and it takes days to give her own back its shape. What restores her at last — ma quale me stessa? — are the bozze: centotrentanove pagine, carta spessa, the words of the quaderno now pleasantly estranee in their type. She spends ore felici rereading and correcting at the kitchen table with Gianni and Elisa studying beside her and her mother bustling con sorprendente cautela not to disturb. She goes back to Milan and, per la prima volta nella mia vita, takes a taxi at the editor's prompting. From Milan she goes to Pisa, then home to Naples; the temptation to arrive at the rione by taxi is strong, but she takes the bus. Devo avere addosso comunque qualcosa che mi rendeva diversa: Ada, walking the baby, doesn't recognise her at first, then comes back: «Come stai bene, non ti avevo riconosciuta, sei diventata un'altra». The compliment soon stings: volevo restare io, vincolata a Lila, al cortile, alle bambole perdute, a don Achille, a tutto. Era l'unico modo per sentire intensamente ciò che mi stava accadendo.

In spring the book comes out and gives her una nuova identità — much more than the laurea did. When she shows a copy to her parents and brothers they pass it round silently, ma senza sfogliarlo. Her father: «È il mio cognome», spoken senza soddisfazione, come se invece di essere fiero di me, avesse scoperto che gli avevo rubato soldi dalle tasche. The first reviews appear; Elena reads them anxiously, wounded by any slight criticism. The kindest she reads aloud, her father brightens; Elisa needles her — «Ti dovevi firmare Lenuccia, Elena fa schifo». Her mother starts pasting the favourable clippings into an album she has bought for the purpose. One morning Immacolata, who knows perfectly well, asks her fiancé's name. «Pietro Airota». — «Tu quindi ti chiamerai Airota». — «Sì». — «E se farai un altro libro, sulla copertina ci sarà scritto Airota?» — «No». — «Perché?» — «Perché mi piace Elena Greco». — «Anche a me». But her mother never reads it; nor does her father, Peppe, Gianni, Elisa; at first nor does the rione. A photographer comes and shoots her for two hours by the gardens, along the stradone and at the tunnel; a picture appears in il Mattino. She expects strangers to stop her — none do. Nemmeno Alfonso, Ada, Carmen, Gigliola, Michele Solara, the latter being not quite illiterate like his brother Marcello, ever says il tuo libro è bello, o, che so, il tuo libro è brutto. They greet her warmly and walk past.

Her first encounter with readers is at a Milan bookshop, an event Adele has pressed for and travels up from Genoa to attend. Adele keeps her company all afternoon at the hotel; Elena has a tremito alle mani che non se ne andava, bocca amara. She's furious that Pietro stayed in Pisa with work. Mariarosa drops by from Milan briefly. Avevo davanti una quarantina di estranei dall'aria fine e coltivata che mi fissavano in silenzio, con uno sguardo senza simpatia, in gran parte costretti a stare lì dal prestigio degli Airota. The rite begins. An eminent older critic — un anziano critico, professore universitario all'epoca molto stimato — praises the book; she follows nothing of it, only worries about her own turn. When it comes, she parlò senza sapere bene cosa stessi dicendo, gesticulates, over‑exhibits her competenza letteraria, over‑plays her cultura classica. Silence. Cosa stavano pensando di me? She catches herself imploring Adele with her eyes and is ashamed. The first audience question comes from a man Adele clearly dislikes: he laments the decadenza dell'editoria, denounces connivenza mercantile with the critics' columns, and ironically, then hostilely, attacks her novel — citing le pagine un po' spinte. She flushes and burbles a confused reply, then falls silent. The critic asks «Altri?». From the back, a hand goes up. «Prego».

A tall young man — lunghi capelli arruffati, una gran barba folta e molto nera — speaks sprezzantemente polemico against the previous intervention and, in places, against the elderly critic himself. He says we live in a Paese provincialissimo, where complaining is the favourite sport while nobody rolls up their sleeves to make things work. Then he turns to la forza modernizzatrice of her novel and praises it. Lo riconobbi innanzitutto dalla voce, era Nino Sarratore.

— Fine del secondo libro.

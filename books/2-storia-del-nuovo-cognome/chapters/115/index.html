Capitolo 115 / 125

Graduation, an engagement ring, a quaderno

Summary

About San Giovanni a Teduccio Elena knows nothing except that Bruno Soccavo's salami factory is somewhere in that zone — the very association makes her uneasy. She hasn't thought of the Ischia summer in months; she discovers now that the happy side of it has faded and the lato sgradevole has grown. Even smells and sounds from then disgust her, but the memory that surprises her into long crying fits is the evening at the Maronti with Donato Sarratore. With distance she sees that her first experience of penetration — in the dark, on cold sand, with quell'uomo banale che era il padre del ragazzo che amavo — was degradante. New shame, layered on the old.

She drives herself at her tesi, reads pages of it aloud to Pietro, who is kind, quotes Virgil from memory, suggests passages. She is grateful and resentful at once. The deeper anxiety is the assistant who follows both of them: he treats Pietro as a future cattedra, her as a bright normal student. She often goes silent in his office out of pride and fear. She decides she must do better than Pietro — he knows much more than her, but he is grigio, non ha fantasia. She tears up her work and starts over chasing a less‑safe idea; the professor praises her but lightly, come se il mio affannarmi fosse solo un gioco ben giocato. Capii presto che Pietro Airota aveva un futuro e io no. The assistant compounds the moment by being friendly: «Lei è una studentessa di grandissima sensibilità. Pensa di insegnare, dopo la laurea?». She thinks he means the university and blazes with hope, talks of carrying on with Aeneid IV. He realises the misunderstanding and recovers with talk of the autumn competitive exam negli istituti magistrali — «Abbiamo bisogno di ottimi professori che formino ottimi maestri». Vergogna, vergogna, vergogna.

She stalls for weeks. She lies on her bed considering quitting at the last bend, going back, teaching middle school — Professoressa Greco — admitting she didn't travel far. She dwells nostalgically on Franco, on Paris and the books and the modifications he made of her, then catches herself: was any of it really beautiful, or was the shame already there? Possibile che anche i momenti felici del piacere non reggano mai a un esame rigoroso? Possibile. The blackness of the Maronti reaches forward into Franco and Pietro both. She pulls back. With a quaderno a quadretti she begins, in the third person, to write what happened to her on the beach that night under Barano. Then Ischia. Then Naples and the rione, with names and places altered, and a dark force acquattata nella vita della protagonista that solders the world together con i colori della fiamma ossidrica into a violet‑blue dome that sparks and then splits into senseless grey fragments. Twenty days, head down. She rereads, doesn't like it, drops it. But — come se la vergogna fosse passata da me al quaderno — she comes out calmer, finishes the thesis, sees Pietro again.

Pietro graduates with the family and friends in attendance and Elena realises she no longer envies the script of his life; she's glad to be at the party, and especially glad of Mariarosa, with whom she debates the fascist coup in Greece. Elena's own sessione follows. She doesn't tell her parents (she's afraid her mother would feel obliged to come and celebrate), wears one of Franco's still‑wearable dresses, gets her doctorate in lettere a few months short of twenty‑three, centodieci e lode. Her father went only as far as fifth grade, her mother to second; nessuno dei miei antenati… aveva mai saputo leggere e scrivere correntemente. Che prodigioso sforzo avevo fatto. Some friends celebrate her, and Pietro. They go to a restaurant — the first time he has taken her — and over dinner he asks «Siamo fidanzati?». She smiles, «Non lo so». He produces a small package — a green case with un anello coi brillantini. «Per tutto quest'anno ho creduto di sì. Ma se tu hai un'opinione diversa consideralo un regalo per la laurea». The ring fits. She thinks of the heavier ones Stefano gave Lila; it's still the first jewel she owns. «Siamo fidanzati», she says, and leans over the table to kiss him. He gives her a second gift — le bozze della sua tesi‑libro. She fishes the quaderno out of her bag and gives it to him: «È un romanzo, un unicum: unica copia, unico tentativo, unico cedimento. Non ne scriverò mai più… C'è persino qualche pagina un po' audace». He thanks her, puzzled, puts it on the table; she immediately regrets it, non avrei dovuto metterlo in imbarazzo con una storiella nemmeno battuta a macchina. But the strange thing is she doesn't feel uneasy with him: lui era lui, io ero io. She tells him she's sat the competitive exam for scuole magistrali and will return to Naples; he is unfazed and lays out his plan: two years to stabilise at the university, then they marry — settembre 1969. Leaving, he forgets the quaderno. She points it out divertita («Il mio regalo?»); he runs back for it. They walk a long way along the lungarno, kiss and embrace; she asks half‑seriously if he wants to sgattaiolare into her room. He shakes his head and kisses her again with passion. C'erano intere biblioteche tra lui e Antonio, ma si assomigliavano.
